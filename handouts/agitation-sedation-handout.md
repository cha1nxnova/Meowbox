# Acute Agitation and Procedural Sedation in the ER: Topic Discussion Handout

Nov 16, 2026 · @Kadin Booker

Agitation and procedural sedation are essentially the same job from two directions: we give a sedative to a patient who cannot be fully worked up, and the safety comes from matching the drug to the cause or the procedure and watching the airway afterward. This handout covers ER stabilization only. Grades are printed in each society's own wording (ACEP Level A, B, C for the clinical policies, "strong consensus" with GRADE certainty for the 2026 sedation guideline). Project BETA statements are consensus and carry no grade. Items marked † came from a secondary summary or from recall of the source and were not confirmed in the primary document (see Notes on marked items).

## Recognizing and grading agitation

Agitation is "an extreme form of arousal that is associated with increased verbal and motor activity" \[2\], and **new-onset agitation is medical until proven otherwise** (Project BETA consensus, ungraded) \[2\]. It was present in 2.6% of visits at one urban county ER, and 23% of those patients had features of delirium \[7\].

**The 60-second screen for every agitated patient** \[2\]

| Check | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Full vital signs with temperature | Abnormal vitals point to a medical cause. Hyperthermia marks the lethal end of the spectrum |
| Finger-stick glucose | Hypoglycemia is "easily reversible" |
| Pulse oximetry | Hypoxia looks like combativeness |
| Attention and orientation | Fluctuating awareness means delirium, and delirium means a medical cause |
| Signs of trauma, toxidrome exam | Head injury, anticholinergic, sympathomimetic and withdrawal pictures each change the drug |
| Medication and substance history from EMS, family or the chart | The history found 94% of medical problems in one cited series, the exam 51% |

**Red flags that this is not psychiatric** \[2\], \[6\]

| Red flag | Think of |
| --- | --- |
| First episode after age 45 with no psychiatric history | Delirium, stroke, tumor, infection, endocrine cause |
| Abnormal vital signs or abnormal neurologic exam | Sepsis, meningitis or encephalitis, thyrotoxicosis, intracranial bleed |
| Decreased awareness with attention problems, or visual hallucinations | Delirium |
| Overt intoxication or withdrawal | Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal are "the most medically concerning" |
| Rigidity, hyperreflexia or clonus | Neuroleptic malignant syndrome ("lead pipe" rigidity) or serotonin syndrome (myoclonus, hyperreflexia) |
| Known psychiatric illness that looks different this time | A new medical problem on top |

Among restrained ER adults in one five-hospital study, the chief complaint was medical in 45.4%, alcohol or drug use in 33.7% and psychiatric in only 12.3% \[14\].

**Agitation scales**

The trials do not share a scale, so "adequately sedated" means something different in each one.

| Scale | Range | Calm | Severe | Where it is used |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AMSS (Altered Mental Status Scale) | -4 to +4 | 0. Trials call 0 or less "adequate sedation" | +4 is the most agitated | The Hennepin trials: Klein 2018, Martel 2021, Cole 2021 \[18\], \[19\], \[20\] |
| SAT (Sedation Assessment Tool) | -3 to +3 | 0 | +2 to +3 ("combative, violent, out of control" is +3) | Australian trials and the UK guideline \[12\] |
| RASS† | -5 to +4 | 0 | +3 to +4 | Built for the ICU. Barbic 2021 enrolled at +3 or more and called -1 or less sedated \[23\] |
| BARS | 1 to 7 | 4 | 7 (violent, requires restraint)† | Triage tool favored by Project BETA. "Any score other than a 4 should trigger an evaluation by a clinician" \[3\] |

**Hyperactive delirium with severe agitation.** ACEP replaced "excited delirium" with this descriptive term in a 2021 task force report and withdrew its 2009 white paper in October 2023† \[11\]. The AMA (2021) and ACMT (2023) reject "excited delirium" as a diagnosis as well \[16\], \[17\]. Essentially it is a presentation and not a diagnosis: altered mental status, extreme agitation, and a hyperadrenergic state with fever and acidosis†. We treat it as a resuscitation, as continued struggling under restraint drives the hyperthermia and acidosis that kill \[12\].

## Acute agitation: ER algorithm

The sequence is a synthesis of Project BETA \[2\], \[3\], \[4\], \[5\] and the ACEP clinical policy \[1\], not a published algorithm. We move down a step only when the one above fails or is unsafe, and steps 4 to 6 are alternatives, not a ladder. Verbal de-escalation "frequently can be successful in less than 5 minutes" \[3\]. Restraint is a last resort at any step and always comes with medication \[5\], \[6\].

**The ten domains of de-escalation** (Project BETA, consensus) \[3\]

| Domain | In practice |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Respect personal space | At least two arm lengths. Both of us can reach the door |
| 2. Do not be provocative | Hands visible and open, stand at an angle, no staring |
| 3. Establish verbal contact | One person talks. Introduce ourselves and our role |
| 4. Be concise | Short sentences, simple words, repeated as often as needed |
| 5. Identify wants and feelings | "What were you hoping would happen today?" |
| 6. Listen closely | Repeat back what we heard |
| 7. Agree or agree to disagree | Agree with the truth, the principle or the odds |
| 8. Set clear limits | Respectful, reasonable, stated as a fact and not a threat |
| 9. Offer choices and optimism | Oral or injection, food, a blanket, a phone call |
| 10. Debrief the patient and staff | After any restraint or involuntary medication |

## Agitation drug chart

The ACEP clinical policy (approved October 2023, published January 2024) is the only graded US guideline, and it has no Level A recommendation \[1\]. Its wording below was read through the lead author's summary and not the journal PDF† \[9\].

**What ACEP recommends for severe agitation in the ER**† \[1\], \[9\]

| Level | Recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| A | None specified |
| B | For more rapid and efficacious treatment, "use a combination of droperidol and midazolam or an atypical antipsychotic in combination with midazolam" |
| B | If a single agent must be given, "use droperidol or an atypical antipsychotic due to the adverse effect profile of midazolam alone" |
| B | For efficacious treatment, haloperidol alone or in combination with lorazepam |
| C (consensus) | Where safety of the patient, bystanders or staff is a concern, "consider ketamine (intravenous or intramuscular)" |
| None | No recommendation for or against any agent out of hospital (consensus statement). None specific to patients over 65 |

Oral medication comes first whenever the patient will take it (Project BETA consensus) \[4\]. In 2025 a Hennepin cohort found oral and IM first doses reached adequate sedation in the same median 15 minutes, with rescue in 31.8% vs 28.4% \[55\].

**Key trials**

| Trial | Compared | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Klein, Ann Emerg Med 2018 (n=737) \[18\] | IM midazolam 5, olanzapine 10, ziprasidone 20, haloperidol 5 and 10 mg | Sedated at 15 min: midazolam beat haloperidol 5 mg by 30%, haloperidol 10 mg by 28%, ziprasidone by 18% and olanzapine by 9% (interval crosses zero) |
| Martel, Acad Emerg Med 2021 (n=115) \[19\] | IM droperidol 5, ziprasidone 10 or 20, lorazepam 2 mg | Sedated at 15 min: 64% vs 25% vs 35% vs 29% |
| Cole, Ann Emerg Med 2021 (n=1,257) \[20\] | IM droperidol vs olanzapine | Median 16 vs 17.5 min. Rescue 17% vs 24% |
| Taylor, Ann Emerg Med 2017 (n=349) \[21\] | IV midazolam plus droperidol vs droperidol vs olanzapine | Sedated at 10 min: the combination won by 25.0% and 25.4% |
| Calver, Ann Emerg Med 2015 (DORM II, n=1,009) \[22\] | Droperidol 10 mg, safety | Abnormal QT in 13 (1.3%), 7 with another cause. No torsades. Median 20 min to sedation |
| Cole, West J Emerg Med 2020 (n=16,546) \[52\] | Droperidol, safety | One case of torsades (0.006%) |
| Barbic, Ann Emerg Med 2021 (n=80) \[23\] | IM ketamine 5 mg/kg vs midazolam 5 plus haloperidol 5 mg | Median 5.8 vs 14.7 min. Serious adverse events 12.5% vs 5.0% (not significant) |
| Lin, Am J Emerg Med 2021 (n=93) \[24\] | Ketamine vs haloperidol plus lorazepam | Sedated within 5 min 22% vs 0%, within 15 min 66% vs 7% |
| Cole, Clin Toxicol 2016 (prehospital) \[25\] | IM ketamine 5 mg/kg vs haloperidol 10 mg | Median 5 vs 17 min. Intubation 39% vs 4% |
| Cole, Ann Emerg Med 2024 (n=693) \[10\] | Olanzapine alone vs with a benzodiazepine | Intubation 3.8% vs 3.5% (difference 0.3%, 95% CI -3.0 to 3.7) |
| Cole, Am J Emerg Med 2026 (methamphetamine) \[54\] | IM droperidol, olanzapine, midazolam, lorazepam | Median 16, 16, 13 and 29 min. Lorazepam needed rescue in 46% |

Overall the pattern is that midazolam and droperidol are the fast single agents, the droperidol plus midazolam combination is faster still, ketamine is fastest and riskiest, and ziprasidone, lorazepam and haloperidol alone are slow.

A 2026 network meta-analysis of individual patient data (18 trials, n=3,411) points the same way: an antipsychotic with a benzodiazepine beat haloperidol alone for sedation within 15 to 30 minutes (OR 4.86 in severe agitation, 12.93 in moderate), with very low confidence and more hypotension with benzodiazepines \[121\]. The 2026 toxicology review dissents and advises against combinations in general \[57\]. Much of the Hennepin data also comes from a mostly alcohol-intoxicated population, so it may not travel to every ER.

## Matching the drug to the cause

Project BETA's rule is to treat the most likely cause, and "medication should be used to calm patients, not to induce sleep" \[4\]. BETA is ungraded consensus from 2012, and two of its positions (avoid droperidol, avoid olanzapine with a benzodiazepine) are now contradicted by the ACEP policy \[1\], \[4\].

| Likely cause | First choice | Avoid | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alcohol intoxication | Use medication "sparingly if at all". An antipsychotic if needed (BETA names haloperidol or olanzapine, and droperidol now has cohort support) | Benzodiazepines, as they compound respiratory depression | BETA \[4\]. Droperidol gave the shortest stay in 11,787 intoxicated patients \[78\] |
| Alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal | "A benzodiazepine is the agent of choice" | Antipsychotic alone | BETA \[4\] |
| Stimulants (methamphetamine, cocaine) | Benzodiazepine first by tradition. Droperidol or olanzapine performed as well as midazolam in methamphetamine agitation | Lorazepam IM when speed matters (29 min, 46% rescue) | BETA \[4\], \[54\], \[71\] |
| Psychosis with known psychiatric illness | Antipsychotic, oral first. IM olanzapine or ziprasidone if not cooperative | A second antipsychotic dose before trying an added benzodiazepine (BETA excepts olanzapine) | BETA \[4\] |
| Undifferentiated, severe | Droperidol plus midazolam, or an atypical antipsychotic plus midazolam | Midazolam alone | ACEP Level B† \[1\] |
| Immediate danger, or hyperactive delirium with severe agitation | Ketamine 4 to 5 mg/kg IM, with cooling, fluids and full monitoring | Prolonged physical struggle | ACEP Level C† \[1\], \[12\] |
| Delirium from a medical cause | Fix the cause. Low-dose second-generation antipsychotic or low-dose haloperidol | Benzodiazepines, as "they can exacerbate the delirium" | BETA \[4\] |
| Anticholinergic delirium | Benzodiazepine. Rivastigmine 3 mg oral every 2 h to a maximum of 12 mg, or a patch† | Antipsychotics, benztropine, diphenhydramine | \[66\], \[67\] |

**Physostigmine is essentially gone.** The only US maker closed in February 2023†. The imported product (Anticholium 2 mg/5 mL, not FDA approved) was reported down to one lot expiring May 2026†. Its label dose is 0.04 mg/kg slow IV at about 1 mg/min, maximum 2 mg per dose, repeated no sooner than 5 to 20 minutes \[66\]. A wide QRS should be narrowed with bicarbonate first, with atropine at the bedside† \[67\]. We should check our own stock before listing it anywhere.

**Special populations**

| Patient | Adjustment | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Older adults | Olanzapine 5 mg IM (2.5 mg if debilitated). Droperidol 5 mg start. Haloperidol 0.5 to 1 mg†. Midazolam carried adverse events in 53% of older adults in a 2025 review (OR 5.25 vs haloperidol) | \[60\], \[56\] |
| Dementia | Every antipsychotic carries a boxed warning for mortality in dementia-related psychosis (1.6 to 1.7 times placebo). Use the lowest dose for the shortest time | \[61\] |
| Parkinson disease, Lewy body dementia | Haloperidol is contraindicated. Quetiapine 12.5 to 25 mg oral is the usual choice†. Benzodiazepine if parenteral is needed† | \[61\] |
| Known long QT or QT drugs on board | Droperidol label: contraindicated above 440 ms (men) or 450 ms (women). Igalmi, ziprasidone and haloperidol also prolong QT. A benzodiazepine is the QT-neutral option† | \[59\], \[62\], \[63\] |
| Pregnancy | No ER trial data. Haloperidol has the longest experience†. Left lateral position in late pregnancy† |  |
| Children and teens | Cause-driven. Unknown cause, moderate: lorazepam, diphenhydramine or olanzapine. No benzodiazepine within 1 hour of olanzapine. "Ketamine and barbiturates are not recommended". Avoid IM in autism unless essential | AAEP pediatric consensus 2019† \[15\] |
| Children, oral option | Oral olanzapine vs oral diazepam: 61% vs 57% sedated at 1 hour | PEAChY-O 2025 \[77\] |

Pediatric mg/kg doses are left off on purpose, as the consensus table could not be read directly and one extracted value was implausible.

## After the dose: monitoring, adverse effects and restraints

A sedated agitated patient is a sedation patient, so the monitoring should look like the second half of this handout.

**Monitoring after parenteral sedation**†

| What | When | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pulse oximetry, respiratory rate, sedation score | Continuous or every 5 to 15 min until calm and rousable, per hospital policy | Respiratory depression by capnography or saturation criteria was 12% with droperidol and 36% to 48% with ziprasidone and lorazepam in one IM trial \[19\] |
| Capnography | With ketamine, with any combination, and with alcohol on board, where available | Recommended in the 2026 toxicology review for ketamine \[57\] |
| ECG | As soon as the patient allows it. Sooner with known QT risk | The droperidol label asks for one before the dose, which is rarely possible. One torsades in 16,546 \[52\], \[59\] |
| Temperature, glucose, CK, potassium, blood gas | Severe agitation or delirium features | Hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis and acidosis are the lethal pathway \[12\] |
| Position | Supine with head of bed raised, never prone | Positional asphyxia \[6\], \[8\] |

**Adverse effects and what we do about them**

| Problem | How often | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acute dystonia | 5 in 15,918 (0.03%) across IM droperidol, olanzapine and haloperidol in one ER cohort. Chart review undercounts delayed cases \[72\] | Benztropine 1 to 2 mL (1 mg/mL) IM or IV \[74\], or diphenhydramine 25 to 50 mg† |
| Akathisia | 2.9% with droperidol in 6,353 visits \[75\] | Diphenhydramine or a benzodiazepine† |
| QT prolongation and torsades | Abnormal QT 1.3% after droperidol 10 mg. No torsades in 1,009 \[22\] | Correct potassium and magnesium, avoid stacking QT drugs |
| Respiratory depression | Highest with midazolam, with IV dosing and with alcohol. IV olanzapine: hypoxia 10.4%, major airway events 2.1% \[68\] | Reposition, oxygen, bag-mask. Flumazenil is rarely the answer† |
| Hypotension | Olanzapine (orthostatic), droperidol, combinations | Fluids, supine |
| Ketamine: intubation | 39% in one prehospital study \[25\] and up to 63% in other prehospital series†. 1.8% pooled when given in the ER \[28\] | Airway equipment and a physician at the bedside before the dose |
| Ketamine: other | Hypersalivation 18% to 38%, laryngospasm up to 5%, emergence 4% to 10%, vomiting 6% to 9% across two prehospital series† \[25\] | Suction, bag-mask, midazolam for emergence |
| Neuroleptic malignant syndrome | Rare. Fever, rigidity, altered mental status, autonomic instability \[62\] | Stop the antipsychotic, cool, benzodiazepines† |

**Olanzapine with a benzodiazepine.** The US label says the pairing "is not recommended" \[60\], the European label separates them by 60 minutes, and ACEP recommends an atypical antipsychotic with midazolam (Level B†) \[1\]. The largest ER comparison found no difference in intubation, hypoxemia or hypotension, yet its interval allows up to 3.7 points more intubation \[10\]. Caution is still most defensible in the heavily alcohol-intoxicated and the older patient.

**Ketamine and law enforcement.** ASA and ACEP "firmly oppose the use of ketamine or any other sedative/hypnotic agent to chemically incapacitate someone solely for a law enforcement purpose"† \[73\]. The decision to sedate is a medical one.

**Restraint and seclusion: CMS rules** (42 CFR 482.13(e), current to January 2026) \[8\]

| Rule | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| When | Only "to ensure the immediate physical safety of the patient, a staff member, or others", and "discontinued at the earliest possible time" |
| Order | From a physician or other licensed practitioner. "Never be written as a standing order or on an as needed basis (PRN)" |
| Order length, violent or self-destructive behavior | 4 hours for adults. 2 hours for ages 9 to 17. 1 hour under age 9. Renewable to 24 hours total |
| Face to face | Within 1 hour of starting |
| Monitoring | "At an interval determined by hospital policy". CMS sets no number |
| Drug as a restraint | A drug used to restrict movement that "is not a standard treatment or dosage for the patient's condition" |
| Deaths | Reported to CMS by close of the next business day |

A great way to frame that drug rule is that a standard dose given to treat the cause of the agitation is treatment, and the same drug given only to stop movement is a restraint. Physical restraint should always be paired with medication, as "medications are necessary whenever physical restraint of the patient is required" \[6\]. Restraint orders were written in 0.5% of adult ER visits in one five-hospital study \[14\], and unhoused adults had 1.45 times the adjusted odds of one \[79\].

## Procedural sedation: the framework

ACEP published a new two-part unscheduled procedural sedation guideline in September 2026 that updates the 2019 version \[30\], \[31\]. It does not use Level A, B or C. Every section is labeled "strong consensus" (at least 90% of the panel), and evidence statements carry GRADE certainty (very low to high). The 2014 ACEP clinical policy is still the only document with Level A, B and C grades and has not been revised \[32\].

**What the guidelines say**

| Topic | ACEP 2014 clinical policy† \[32\] | ACEP 2026 guideline \[30\], \[31\] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fasting | "Do not delay procedural sedation in adults or pediatrics in the ED based on fasting time" (Level B) | "Urgent and emergent procedures should not be delayed solely based upon fasting times." Adopts the ICAPS risk algorithm below |
| Capnography | "May be used as an adjunct to pulse oximetry and clinical assessment" (Level B) | Rhythm, blood pressure, capnography and pulse oximetry with audible tone "should be used routinely whenever feasible" (high certainty) |
| Supplemental oxygen | Not addressed | "Should be considered optional and not required" (low certainty) |
| Personnel | A nurse or other qualified individual present for continuous monitoring, in addition to the proceduralist (Level C) | A second practitioner whose primary duty is monitoring is "highly recommended". One clinician may sedate and do the procedure if it "can be immediately halted" |
| Who gives the drugs | Not addressed | "Qualified nurses should be permitted to administer all medications" under direct bedside supervision of the ordering practitioner |
| Airway scoring | Not addressed | Mallampati "should not be required" (high certainty that it predicts poorly) |
| ASA physical status | Not addressed | "Should be deemphasized or discontinued." ASA III is not a trigger for anesthesia consultation (moderate certainty) |
| GLP-1 receptor agonists | Not addressed | Sedation "should also proceed in usual fashion for most adult patients" (very low certainty) |

**Drug grades in the 2014 policy** (the question was "can it be safely administered", not "which is best")† \[32\]

| Drug | Adults | Children |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Propofol | Level A | Level A |
| Ketamine | Level C | Level A |
| Etomidate | Level B | Level C |
| Ketamine plus propofol | Level B | Level B |
| Alfentanil | Level C | No recommendation |

**The sedation continuum** (ASA statement, amended October 2024 \[33\], with the dissociative row from ACEP 2026 \[31\])

| Depth | Responsiveness | Airway | Spontaneous ventilation | Circulation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Minimal (anxiolysis) | Normal response to voice | Unaffected | Unaffected | Unaffected |
| Moderate | Purposeful response to voice or touch | No intervention required | Adequate | Usually maintained |
| Deep | Purposeful response after repeated or painful stimulation | Intervention may be required | May be inadequate | Usually maintained |
| General anesthesia | Unarousable | Intervention often required | Frequently inadequate | May be impaired |
| Dissociative (ketamine) | Trance-like, profound analgesia and amnesia | "May require repositioning" | "Essentially always maintained" | Stable |

The working rule is that whoever gives one depth must be able to rescue a patient who slips one level deeper \[33\]. ACEP 2026 adds that practice "should focus on ventilatory adequacy for safety" and not on the depth label \[31\]. ASA (2018) and ACEP (2026) disagree on whether propofol, ketamine and etomidate need anesthesia-level care†, and ASA is not among the endorsers of the 2026 guideline \[30\], \[34\].

**Pre-sedation assessment** \[31\]

| Step | What to check | Detail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Procedure | Pain, duration, need for stillness | Decides analgesia, depth and drug |
| History | Systemic disease, prior sedation, allergies, opioid use disorder, pregnancy | Severe systemic disease raises risk at every age (high certainty) |
| Airway and lungs | Micrognathia, large tongue, large tonsils, short neck, severe obesity | Mallampati is not required |
| Highest-risk adults | Moderate to severe heart failure, pulmonary edema, valve disease, acute MI | "May tolerate only minimal sedation if at all" |
| Older adults | Greater risk, yet "most remain appropriate candidates" (moderate certainty) | Lower doses, slower titration |
| Obesity | Modestly greater airway and respiratory risk (low certainty) | "Most drug doses should be adjusted based on ideal body weight" |
| Children | Sleep apnea is about five times the airway risk. Obesity, prematurity, a current cold, autism and age under 1 to 2 years each about double it | Sedation is "generally avoided" under 6 months |
| Room | Oxygen, suction, monitors, bag-valve mask, oral and nasal airways, IV supplies, resuscitation drugs | Reversal agents "must be readily available" when opioids or benzodiazepines are used |

**Oral intake and aspiration risk (ICAPS algorithm, adopted by ACEP 2026)** \[31\], \[35\]

| Risk | Who | Elective procedure | Urgent or emergent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Negligible | None of the factors below | Clear liquids unrestricted. Food about 2 h | No delay based on fasting time |
| Mild | Severe systemic disease. BMI 30 to 39. Age 12 months or less. Hiatal hernia. Upper endoscopy or bronchoscopy. Propofol as the principal sedative | Clear liquids unrestricted. Breast milk about 2 h. Food about 4 h | No delay based on fasting time |
| Moderate | Disease that is a constant threat to life. BMI 40 or more. Sleep apnea. Airway abnormality. Hyperemesis, gastroparesis or other esophageal disorder, bowel obstruction. Expected need for assisted ventilation | Clear liquids about 2 h. Breast milk about 4 h. Food about 6 h | No delay. Anesthesia care if available. If not, consider ketamine as the sole agent |

In 6,183 children sedated in six Canadian ERs, 48.1% had not fasted for solids, there were no aspirations, and each extra hour of fasting changed nothing (OR 1.00) \[36\]. The direct ER fasting evidence is pediatric and mostly ketamine, so we should say that when we apply it to adults getting propofol.

**Monitoring**

| Monitor | Evidence | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capnography | Hypoxia 25% vs 42% when the team could see it (n=132, propofol). It flagged every hypoxic event first, by a median of 60 seconds | \[37\] |
| Capnography, pooled | 3 trials, 1,272 patients: "lack of convincing evidence" that it reduces clinically significant events | Cochrane 2017 \[38\] |
| High-flow oxygen | Hypoxia 19% vs 41% with 15 L/min by nonrebreather. Respiratory depression on capnography was unchanged (51% vs 48%) | \[39\] |
| Oxygen caveat | "When using supplemental oxygen, capnography is the sole monitoring device to promptly identify impaired ventilation" | \[31\] |
| Blood pressure | Before sedation and "at appropriate intervals thereafter". No fixed interval in ACEP 2026. ASA 2018 uses every 5 minutes† | \[31\], \[34\] |
| Eyes on the patient | An "unobstructed view of the airway, chest, and skin" | \[31\] |

A great way to remember the danger window is that events cluster at induction and right after the painful part ends, as the drug is then unopposed by the stimulus \[31\].

## Procedural sedation: ER algorithm

&#91;embedded content: procedural sedation · ER algorithm, 7 steps\]

The sequence is a synthesis of the ACEP 2026 guideline \[30\], \[31\], not a published algorithm. Most rescues are repositioning and bag-mask ventilation, so step 3 is essentially where the safety comes from. Hematoma and nerve blocks are options that reduce the risks of sedation† \[49\].

## Procedural sedation drug chart

"No single sedative regimen is ideal for every patient or procedure" \[31\]. Essentially every ER sedation dose is off label, and ACEP 2026 says labeling "should not supersede" the sedation literature \[31\]. Doses below are the trial and guideline doses, with the label shown where it differs.

**Sedatives**

| Drug | ER dose (adult) | Onset and duration† | Analgesia | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Propofol | 0.5 to 1 mg/kg IV, then 0.5 mg/kg every 3 min \[88\]. Label: about 0.5 mg/kg slowly over 3 to 5 min \[80\] | 30 to 60 s. 5 to 10 min | None | Hypotension 3.5% to 8%, desaturation 7.7%, bag-mask 3.9% \[91\], \[93\]. Injection pain |
| Ketamine | 1 to 1.5 mg/kg IV over 60 s (1.5 to 2 mg/kg in children), or 4 to 5 mg/kg IM. Repeat 0.5 to 1 mg/kg IV \[31\], \[47\] | 1 min IV, 5 min IM. 10 to 15 min IV, 15 to 30 min IM | Yes | Recovery agitation, vomiting, laryngospasm, hypertension. More drug past the dissociative threshold "does not enhance or deepen sedation" \[31\] |
| Ketamine plus propofol | 1:1 in one syringe \[90\], \[91\]. Customary start 0.5 mg/kg of each†, then titrate | About 1 min. 10 to 15 min | Yes | Hypotension 1% vs 8% with propofol alone, yet severe emergence delirium 5% vs 2% \[91\] |
| Etomidate | 0.1 to 0.15 mg/kg IV†. Reported range 0.15 to 0.22 mg/kg \[107\] | Under 1 min. 5 to 10 min | None | Myoclonus 20% to 45%, lower procedural success than propofol (88.6% vs 97.2%) \[89\], \[107\]. In shortage since October 2022 \[113\] |
| Midazolam with fentanyl | Midazolam up to 2.5 mg over 2 min, repeat after 2 min. About 30% less after an opioid, and more than 5 mg total is rarely needed. Fentanyl 0.5 to 1 mcg/kg† \[84\] | 2 to 3 min. 30 to 60 min | Fentanyl only | Slowest recovery and the least predictable. ACEP 2026 favors fentanyl with propofol, or ketamine, for very painful procedures \[31\]. Both drugs in shortage \[113\] |
| Remimazolam (Byfavo) | 5 mg IV over 1 min, then 2.5 mg over 15 s at least 2 min apart. Fixed dose, not weight based \[83\] | Peak 3 to 3.5 min | None | Only labeled for procedures of 30 minutes or less. No ER randomized trial yet, only Danish cohorts (n=183 in the nurse-administered series) \[104\]. Reconstitute 20 mg with 8.2 mL saline, good for 8 h |
| Dexmedetomidine | 1 mcg/kg IV over 10 min (0.5 mcg/kg over age 65), then 0.6 mcg/kg/h \[85\]. Intranasal 3 to 3.9 mcg/kg in children for imaging \[99\] | 10 to 15 min IV. About 32 min intranasal | Weak | Bradycardia and sinus arrest with a fast bolus. Too slow for most ER procedures |
| Nitrous oxide 50% to 70% | Self-administered or continuous flow | 2 to 5 min, off in 5 min | Mild to moderate | Vomiting 5.7%, serious events 0.2% in 762 children \[100\]. Avoid with trapped gas (pneumothorax, bowel obstruction)† |

**Older adults and other adjustments**

| Situation | Adjustment | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Age 65 or older, debilitated, ASA III to IV | Propofol: no rapid bolus, give over 3 to 5 min, reduce to about 80% of the usual dose. The common "halve it" teaching has no primary source we could find | Label \[80\] |
| Age 60 or older | Midazolam: no more than 1.5 mg over 2 min, then 1 mg steps. A total above 3.5 mg is rarely needed | Label \[84\] |
| Obesity | "Most drug doses should be adjusted based on ideal body weight". Propofol by lean body weight matched lean controls in BMI 40 or more | \[31\], \[111\] |
| Hypotension or shock | Ketamine, or ketamine plus propofol, over propofol alone. Etomidate caused a smaller pressure fall than propofol (3.8% vs 7.9%) | \[89\], \[91\] |
| Opioid given in the last 30 minutes | Closer watch for cardiopulmonary depression | \[31\] |
| Egg or soy allergy | Label contraindicates propofol only with prior anaphylaxis to egg or soy. AAAAI says these patients "can receive propofol without any special precautions" (0 proven anaphylaxis in 5,446 allergic patients in one series†) | \[80\], \[105\] |
| Head or eye injury | ICP findings were mixed (2 of 8 studies up, 2 down) with no difference in perfusion pressure or outcomes in 953 intubated patients. Eye pressure rose about 1 to 3 mm Hg in children with healthy eyes†, and injured eyes were not studied | \[109\] |

**Ketamine myths the evidence has retired**

| Old teaching | What the data show |
| --- | --- |
| Give atropine or glycopyrrolate for secretions | Not needed. Airway problems from saliva in 0.11% of 947 children without atropine, and anticholinergics predicted more airway events (OR 1.82) \[92\], \[97\] |
| Give midazolam to every child to prevent emergence | No benefit in children. It helps adults (25% to 8%) \[43\] |
| Push it fast | Over 60 seconds. Rapid push causes apnea \[65\] |
| IM is gentler | IM doubled vomiting in children (26.3% vs 11.9% IV) and lengthened sedation (129 vs 80 min) \[108\] |
| Vomiting cannot be prevented | Ondansetron cut ER vomiting from 12.6% to 4.7% in children (NNT 13). Vomiting peaks near age 12 \[92\], \[96\] |

Ketamine is contraindicated under 3 months of age and in known or suspected schizophrenia† \[47\].

**Reversal agents (rescue only, never routine)**

| Agent | Adult dose | Child dose | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Naloxone | 0.1 to 0.2 mg IV every 2 to 3 min to effect \[87\]. Many start at 0.04 mg† | 0.005 to 0.01 mg IV increments every 2 to 3 min (label, postoperative) \[87\]. Weight-based 0.01 mg/kg is common practice† | Titrate so the analgesia survives. Outlasted by long-acting opioids |
| Flumazenil | 0.2 mg IV over 15 s, repeat every 60 s to 1 mg total \[86\] | 0.01 mg/kg (maximum 0.2 mg) every 60 s, to 0.05 mg/kg or 1 mg total, whichever is lower \[86\] | Seizures in benzodiazepine-dependent patients. Resedation in 3% to 9%, so keep monitoring |

Propofol, ketamine, etomidate and dexmedetomidine have no reversal agent†.

**Key trials**

| Trial | Compared | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Miner, Acad Emerg Med 2010 (n=97) \[88\] | Propofol vs ketamine in adults | Subclinical respiratory depression 40% vs 64%. Recovery agitation 8% vs 36%. Back to baseline in 5 vs 14 min |
| Miner, Ann Emerg Med 2007 (n=214) \[89\] | Etomidate vs propofol | Myoclonus 20.0% vs 1.8%. Procedural success 88.6% vs 97.2% |
| Andolfatto, Ann Emerg Med 2012 (n=284) \[90\] | 1:1 ketamine plus propofol vs propofol | Respiratory events 30% vs 32% |
| Ferguson, Ann Emerg Med 2016 (POKER, n=573) \[91\] | 1:1 ketamine plus propofol vs propofol | Respiratory intervention 3% vs 5%. Hypotension 1% vs 8%. Severe emergence delirium 5% vs 2% |
| Miner, Ann Emerg Med 2015 (n=271) \[94\] | Propofol vs 1:1 vs 4:1 propofol to ketamine | Airway interventions 29% vs 19% vs 32%. Recovery agitation 8% vs 21% vs 10% |
| Miner, Acad Emerg Med 2009 (n=145) \[95\] | Propofol with or without alfentanil | No less pain or recall. More stimulation needed to breathe (20 vs 11 patients) |
| Green, Ann Emerg Med 2009 (8,282 children) \[92\] | Ketamine, pooled individual data | Airway or respiratory events 3.9%. Vomiting 8.4%. Clinically important recovery agitation 1.4% |
| Green and Roback, Ann Emerg Med 2026 (58,752 children) \[103\] | IV ketamine, systematic review | No deaths. Serious adverse events 0.33%. Risk rises at age 10 or older, with a cold, and with added opioids |
| Türkücü, Acad Emerg Med 2025 (n=108) \[102\] | Ketamine 0.5 vs 1 mg/kg in adults | Recovery agitation no different. 7.4% of the low-dose arm needed a top-up |
| Stiell, Can J Cardiol 2021 (1,736 cardioversions) \[101\] | ER cardioversion sedation | Adverse events needing treatment 13.9%. Higher odds with midazolam (OR 1.9), fentanyl (OR 1.5) and age 85 or older (OR 2.1) |
| Tsze, JAMA Pediatr 2025 (n=101) \[98\] | Intranasal midazolam dose finding | 0.4 to 0.5 mg/kg was optimal for laceration repair |

**Picking by procedure**

| Procedure | Reasonable first choice | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cardioversion | Propofol | Propofol, etomidate and methohexital recovered equally fast in one retrospective study†. Midazolam and fentanyl carried more adverse events \[101\] |
| Fracture or dislocation reduction | Propofol with pain treated beforehand, or ketamine, or the 1:1 mix | Etomidate succeeds less often. An opioid pushed with propofol adds respiratory events without less pain \[89\], \[95\] |
| Painful procedure in a child | Ketamine alone | Lowest serious event rate of any regimen (0.4%) \[42\] |
| Child, laceration or anxiety | Intranasal midazolam 0.4 to 0.5 mg/kg, or nitrous oxide | No IV needed \[98\], \[100\] |
| Child, imaging | Intranasal dexmedetomidine | 91.3% completion, no airway interventions in 578 children \[99\] |
| Moderate aspiration risk, no anesthesia available | Ketamine as the sole agent | "Uniquely preserves protective airway reflexes" \[31\] |

## Sedation adverse events, rescue and recovery

Serious events are rare, and "the most common and important rescue interventions required during procedural sedation are airway repositioning and bag mask ventilation" \[30\].

**How often things go wrong**

| Event | Adults, per 1,000 sedations \[40\] | Children, per 1,000 sedations \[41\] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hypoxia | 40.2 | 14.8 |
| Vomiting | 16.4 (170.0 with ketamine) | 55.5 |
| Hypotension | 15.2 |  |
| Apnea | 12.4† (51.4 with midazolam) | 7.1 |
| Recovery agitation | 9.8† (164.1 with ketamine) | 17.9 |
| Laryngospasm | 4.2 (pooled estimate from one event) | 2.9 (33 of 34 cases with ketamine) |
| Intubation | 1.6 (2 in 3,636) | 4 cases in 9,136 |
| Aspiration | 1.2 (1 in 2,370) | 0 in 3,326 |

Adult data are 55 studies and 9,652 sedations. Pediatric data are 41 studies and 13,883 sedations.

**Risk by regimen in children** (n=6,295, six Canadian pediatric ERs. Serious adverse events 1.1% overall) \[42\]

| Regimen | Serious adverse events | Odds ratio vs ketamine alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ketamine alone | 0.4% | Reference |
| Ketamine plus propofol | 2.1% | 4.4 (2.3 to 8.7) |
| Ketamine plus fentanyl | 3.2% | 6.5 (2.5 to 15.2) |
| Propofol alone | 3.7% | 5.6 (2.3 to 13.1) |

**Rescue ladder**

| Problem | First moves | Drug step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hypoventilation or apnea | Stop the drug and the stimulus. Head tilt, chin lift, jaw thrust. Oral or nasal airway. Bag-mask ventilation \[30\] | Naloxone if an opioid was given. Flumazenil if a benzodiazepine was given \[31\] |
| Abnormal capnography, saturation still normal | Reassess, prepare to ventilate, "avoid additional sedative doses until concerns resolve" \[31\] |  |
| Laryngospasm | 100% oxygen, jaw thrust with pressure at the laryngospasm notch, continuous positive pressure by bag-mask† | Propofol about 0.5 mg/kg IV†. Then succinylcholine 0.1 to 0.5 mg/kg IV, or 1 to 2 mg/kg IV with intubation, or 4 mg/kg IM with no IV† \[47\] |
| Vomiting | Suction, turn to the side. Antiemetics "readily accessible" \[31\] | Ondansetron |
| Hypotension | Rehydrate before sedation when volume depleted \[31\] | Fluid bolus, smaller propofol doses. A push-dose pressor if persistent† |
| Ketamine recovery agitation | Quiet, dim room | Midazolam IV titrated†. Midazolam 0.03 mg/kg at induction cut it from 25% to 8% in adults (NNT 6) \[43\]. No benefit shown in children \[31\] |
| Fentanyl chest wall rigidity | Seen with rapid, large doses† | Naloxone. Succinylcholine and ventilation if we cannot ventilate† |
| Etomidate myoclonus | Brief and self-limited† | Midazolam if it blocks the procedure† |

Anticholinergics with ketamine "are associated with more frequent adverse events and are no longer recommended" (moderate certainty) \[31\]. Sedating within about 30 minutes of an opioid dose modestly raises cardiopulmonary risk (moderate certainty) \[31\].

**Recovery and discharge** \[31\]

| Criterion | ACEP 2026 wording |
| --- | --- |
| Airway and breathing | "No longer at risk for airway obstruction or respiratory depression" |
| Mental status | "Alert and at age-appropriate baseline levels of consciousness" |
| Vital signs | "Normal or baseline" |
| Oral intake | No evidence that a drink test is needed |
| Disposition | "To the care of a responsible adult, with appropriate verbal and written care instructions" |

In 1,341 pediatric sedations, 92% of adverse effects happened during the procedure, and serious ones came at a median of 2 minutes after the last dose. Discharge about 30 minutes after the final dose looked safe if nothing had gone wrong. This was a ketamine, midazolam and fentanyl series and not a propofol study \[44\].

## The ER pharmacist's role

Both topics run on verbal orders and cabinet overrides, and that is exactly where a bedside pharmacist catches errors. In four US ERs, 32.7% of 364 intercepted errors came from verbal orders and 44.2% were wrong doses \[116\].

**What we own and what we catch**

| Situation | What the pharmacist owns | Common error to catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agitation, any | Cause-matched drug choice. Oral offered first. One syringe, labeled, with the dose said out loud | Reflex "B52" for everyone. Benzodiazepine for the drunk patient. Antipsychotic for withdrawal |
| Droperidol | 2.5 mg/mL vials, 5 to 10 mg is 2 to 4 mL. ECG once the patient is safe | Holding the dose for an ECG that cannot be done. Stacking QT drugs |
| Olanzapine IM | 10 mg vial plus 2.1 mL sterile water gives about 5 mg/mL. Use within 1 hour. Its own syringe \[60\] | Mixed in a syringe with lorazepam or haloperidol. Third dose inside the label interval. No orthostatic check |
| Ziprasidone IM | 1.2 mL sterile water gives 20 mg/mL \[62\] | Picked when speed matters |
| Haloperidol | Lactate for acute use, IM | Decanoate pulled by mistake. IV push with no monitor. Given in Parkinson disease |
| Ketamine | The right vial: 10, 50 and 100 mg/mL exist. 100 mg/mL for IM, and it "must be diluted" for IV \[65\]. Weight estimated, volume double checked | Tenfold errors. Nine inadvertent pediatric ER overdoses were 5 to 100 times the intended dose \[106\]. The IM dose given IV |
| Propofol | Drawn up aseptically, labeled with the time. Label allows 12 hours from spiking, including in a syringe \[80\] | Reused vial. Unlabeled white syringe (looks the same as liposomal bupivacaine and clevidipine†) |
| Ketamine plus propofol in one syringe | Counts as immediate-use compounding. Start within 4 hours†, sooner by local policy | Mixing ratio not stated. No label |
| Any sedation | Weight-based doses pre-calculated, including the rescue drugs. Naloxone, flumazenil, ondansetron, succinylcholine at the bedside | Reversal agents still in the cabinet. Full adult doses in the 85-year-old |
| Restraint | Medication ordered alongside. Standard dose for the cause, documented as treatment | PRN or standing chemical restraint orders, which CMS prohibits \[8\] |

Haloperidol, lorazepam and diphenhydramine were physically compatible in one syringe for 4 hours in a bench study (no chemical stability data) \[120\]. We found no compatibility source for droperidol with midazolam in one syringe.

**Supply watch** (FDA shortage database, November 13, 2026) \[113\]

| Status | Drugs |
| --- | --- |
| In shortage | Lorazepam injection (since 2018), midazolam (since 2020), etomidate (since October 2022), dexmedetomidine, fentanyl, rocuronium |
| No FDA shortage entry | Ketamine, droperidol, propofol, olanzapine, ziprasidone. ASHP may list ketamine and propofol† |
| To be discontinued (FDA, 2026) | Benztropine injection (Fresenius Kabi, January), haloperidol decanoate 50 mg/mL (Fresenius Kabi, January), one succinylcholine presentation (Fresenius Kabi, July) |
| Effectively unavailable | Physostigmine. Short-acting aripiprazole injection |

**Does a pharmacist at the bedside matter?**

| Study | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| Amini 2013 \[114\] | Time to sedation after intubation 9 vs 28 minutes with a pharmacist present |
| Robey-Gavin 2016 \[115\] | Analgesia after intubation rose from 20% to 49% |
| Ernst 2012 \[117\] | Patients with a recorded error in resuscitations: 3% with a pharmacist vs 30% without |
| PHARM-EM 2021 \[118\] | 13,984 interventions over 917 shifts, with cost avoidance of $8,214 per shift |

No study isolates pharmacist impact on ER procedural sedation or adult agitation, so these are borrowed from intubation and resuscitation. ASHP's emergency medicine pharmacist guideline lists both as bedside roles† \[119\].

**Equity check.** Black patients had 1.38 times the adjusted odds of IM chemical sedation in 94,204 ER mental health visits \[122\]. A standard agitation scale and a cause-matched order set are essentially our tools for making the same presentation get the same drug.

No correction or newer policy was found in PubMed through November 2026.

- **ACEP 2026 sedation guideline \[30\], \[31\].** Read in full, both parts. Quotes are direct.
- **ACEP 2021 hyperactive delirium report \[11\].** Not opened. The definition and the 2023 withdrawal of the 2009 white paper are quoted through secondary sources. The report's own treatment steps are not in this handout.

- **Scales.** SAT and AMSS ranges were confirmed. RASS and BARS anchors are as commonly cited.
- **Rescue doses.** The laryngospasm sequence, the treatment of fentanyl rigidity, etomidate myoclonus and emergence reactions, and the hypotension step are standard teaching and were not read in a primary source. ACEP 2026 prints no rescue doses.
- **Onset and duration columns** in the sedation chart are standard reference values and were not confirmed in labels, except where a label is cited.
- **Oral agitation doses** (olanzapine, risperidone, lorazepam), low-dose haloperidol and quetiapine in older adults, diphenhydramine for dystonia, and the pregnancy row are standard practice values that were not sourced.
- **Ketamine.** The under 3 months and schizophrenia contraindications are from the 2011 guideline through a secondary source. The 2004 version was read in full and agrees on age.
- **Pediatric agitation doses** are omitted. The 2019 consensus table could not be read directly.
- **Monitoring after agitation sedation.** No guideline sets intervals. The table is a synthesis of labels, trial adverse event rates and the UK guideline.
- **Physostigmine, shortages and discontinuations.** FDA database entries were read on November 13 to 16, 2026. ASHP pages could not be opened, so ASHP status and the physostigmine stock report are second hand. The rivastigmine regimen is from a poison center page.
