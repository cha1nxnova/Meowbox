## 1. ACEP Clinical Policy 2014 (Godwin et al): critical questions and Level A/B/C recommendations; is there a newer ACEP policy?

### Takeaway
The 2014 policy asked four questions (fasting, capnography, personnel, six drugs) and is still the only ACEP PSA document with Level A/B/C grades; it has not been revised. It is now supplemented, and for practice largely overtaken, by the ACEP 2019 consensus guideline and its 2026 two-part Delphi update.

## 2. ACEP 2019 "Unscheduled Procedural Sedation" guideline and its 2026 update: key statements, definitions, fasting risk stratification

### Takeaway
The 2019 guideline (Green et al) defined "unscheduled" sedation as distinct from elective sedation; the 2026 two-part update supersedes it and is the current ACEP position. Every section of the 2026 update carries the label "STRONG CONSENSUS" (at least 90% of panel agreeing), with GRADE symbols on evidence statements.

### Cited Findings
**2019 citation.** Green SM, Roback MG, Krauss BS, Miner JR, Schneider S, Kivela PD, Nelson LS, Chumpitazi CE, Fisher JD, Gesek D, Jackson B, Kamat P, Kowalenko T, Lewis B, Papo M, Phillips D, Ruff S, Runde D, Tobin T, Vafaie N, Vargo J, Walser E, Yealy DM, O'Connor RE. Unscheduled Procedural Sedation: A Multidisciplinary Consensus Practice Guideline. Ann Emerg Med. 2019;73(5):e51-e65. doi:10.1016/j.annemergmed.2019.02.022. PMID 31029297. [verified] — [PubMed 31029297](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31029297/)
- Abstract: ACEP "organized a multidisciplinary effort to create a clinical practice guideline specific to unscheduled, time-sensitive procedural sedation, which differs in important ways from scheduled, elective procedural sedation"; it covers "background and rationale, and issues relating to staffing, practice, and quality improvement." [verified] — [PubMed 31029297](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31029297/)
- **Superseded:** the 2026 document "represents an update of the 2019 multidisciplinary clinical guidelines". Literature search window 10 Aug 2018 to 7 Feb 2026. Next update planned "in six to eight years". [verified] — [Part 1 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-1-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)

**2026 method and grade wording** [verified] — [Part 1 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-1-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- Anonymous Delphi; 22-member panel (20 active). "Consensus agreement was defined as at least 80% of members choosing 'Agree' or 'Strongly Agree,' while strong consensus required at least 90%."
- Evidence quality by GRADE: "Very low (⊕), Low (⊕⊕), Moderate (⊕⊕⊕), or High (⊕⊕⊕⊕)". No rating is given to statements based on theory, tradition, experience or consensus.
- Endorsed by AAPD, ACMT, ASGE, ICAPS, SCCM, SIR, Society for Pediatric Sedation, and by AAEM, ACOEP, AACEM, CAEP, EMRA, ENA, SAEM; "strongly supported" by AAOMS. The American Society of Anesthesiologists is not among the organizations listed.

**Definitions (2026 Part 1, Figure 1)** [verified] — [Part 1 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-1-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- Unscheduled procedural sedation: "provided to facilitate time-sensitive (e.g., emergent, urgent) diagnostic or therapeutic procedures that, to optimize patient outcomes, must be performed within a short timeframe unsuitable for elective procedure scheduling."
- Procedural sedation (ICAPS definition): "the administration of one or more pharmacological agents to facilitate a diagnostic or therapeutic procedure while targeting a state during which airway patency, spontaneous respiration, protective airway reflexes, and hemodynamic stability are preserved, while alleviating anxiety and pain."
- Scope: "all emergency department (ED) sedation is unscheduled". The guideline excludes general anesthesia, minimal sedation, and sedation without a procedure, "e.g., agitation or behavioral disturbance".

**"Notable changes" listed in the 2026 update** [verified] — [Part 1 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-1-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf); [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- Part 1: Delphi method with GRADE; "More accurate definition of procedural sedation"; competencies derived using Competency-Based Medical Education.
- Part 2: "Algorithm for risk-stratifying oral intake prior to procedural sedation"; "Sedation care for of patients taking glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists"; "Deemphasis of the role of American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status in risk assessment"; "Routine use of capnography for moderate, deep, and dissociative sedation whenever feasible".

**Staffing, competencies, privileging (2026 Part 1, all STRONG CONSENSUS)** [verified] — [Part 1 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-1-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- "the provision of procedural sedation should not be considered a fixed part of any single specialty, but rather endorsed as its own multidisciplinary field."
- "The most common and important rescue interventions required during procedural sedation are airway repositioning and bag mask ventilation." These, with oral and nasal airway placement, "should be the primary focus of rescue training".
- Second person: "It is highly recommended that the practitioner responsible for oversight of the sedation encounter be assisted by a second practitioner (e.g., nurse, respiratory therapist) whose primary duty is continuous patient monitoring and documentation."
- One clinician doing both: "the sedation practitioner may also perform the procedure, provided the procedure can be immediately halted should an adverse event require urgent attention." A rigid rule requiring a separate sedation practitioner for deep sedation "would effectively prohibit compassionate sedation care".
- Nurse drug administration: "Qualified nurses should be permitted to administer all medications used for unscheduled procedural sedation while under the direct bedside supervision of the ordering practitioner, with the ordering practitioner specifying the dosing and timing."
- Privileging "should be based on documented competence ... and not based upon specialty or discipline." ACLS/PALS completion "does not assure appropriate sedation competencies"; residency training in emergency medicine or critical care "supersedes them".
- Quality monitoring: each practitioner accountable to an organized QA program; adverse events defined by intervention (e.g., "assisted ventilation for apnea") not threshold (e.g., "apnea for >30 s"); recommended tool is TROOPS.

**Fasting and aspiration risk stratification (2026 Part 2)** [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- "Urgent and emergent procedures should not be delayed solely based upon fasting times."
- "Common concerns about aspiration during procedural sedation vastly exceed actual risk (⊕⊕⊕⊕)."
- "There is no evidence that compliance with elective fasting guidelines decreases the risk of aspiration, emesis, or other adverse events during procedural sedation (⊕⊕⊕)."
- "the duration of fasting does not predict gastric volume (⊕⊕⊕)."
- Largest study (139,140 pediatric sedations): aspiration 0.97 per 10,000 in fasting-compliant vs 0.79 per 10,000 in noncompliant children.
- Moderate aspiration risk: "practitioners should refer for prompt anesthesia care when available. If unavailable, strongly consider dissociative sedation, as ketamine uniquely preserves protective airway reflexes." "No aspiration has been reported with ketamine as the sole agent ... except in dated reports involving compromised neonates (⊕⊕⊕)."
- The guideline adopts the **ICAPS fasting algorithm** (its Figure 3; read from the figure image) [verified]:

  | | Negligible risk | Mild risk (any factor) | Moderate risk (any factor) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Patient factors | none of those listed | severe systemic disease; moderate obesity (BMI 30 to 39, or 85th to 95th percentile in a child); age 12 months or less; hiatal hernia | severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life; severe obesity (BMI 40 or more, or 95th percentile or more in a child); obstructive sleep apnea; airway abnormalities (micrognathia, macroglossia, laryngomalacia); hyperemesis; esophageal disorders (gastroparesis, achalasia, atresia, stricture, tracheoesophageal fistula); bowel obstruction (ileus, pseudo-obstruction, pyloric stenosis, intussusception) |
  | Procedure / sedation factors | none | upper endoscopy; bronchoscopy; propofol as principal sedative | anticipated need for assisted ventilation or other advanced airway management |
  | **Elective:** clear liquids | unrestricted | unrestricted | about 2 hours; "Refer for anesthesia care" |
  | **Elective:** breast milk | unrestricted | about 2 hours | about 4 hours |
  | **Elective:** food, formula, non-human milk | about 2 hours | about 4 hours | about 6 hours |
  | **Urgent or emergent** | "No delay based upon fasting time" | "No delay based upon fasting time" | "No delay based upon fasting time. Anaesthesia care if available; if not consider ketamine as sole sedative agent." |

  Notes on the figure: clear liquids = water, pulp-free juice, clear tea, black coffee, prepared carbohydrate fluids; "Fasting intervals are not absolute, with exceptions permissible when the volumes of oral intake are minor, or the fasting time reasonably close."
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists (new in 2026)** [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf):
  - FDA labeling (2024) notes "rare postmarketing reports of pulmonary aspiration in patients ... requiring general anesthesia or deep sedation."
  - "no cases have been reported for deep or other levels of sedation" in the peer-reviewed literature.
  - In 377,476 adults under operative anesthesia, aspiration pneumonia did not differ by GLP-1 use (Chen et al, JAMA Netw Open 2025;8:e250081).
  - "sedation care should also proceed in usual fashion for most adult patients (⊕). There are insufficient data to advise the care of children."
  - If judged higher risk: consider anesthesia referral; if not an option, "the use of ketamine alone appears a logical choice".

### Inferences
- A pharmacist-facing summary of ACEP's 2026 position: do not hold ER sedation for NPO time; stratify aspiration risk by the ICAPS factors; propofol as principal sedative is itself a "mild" aspiration risk factor, and ketamine monotherapy is the fallback when risk is moderate and anesthesia is unavailable.
- Because capnography and the oral-intake algorithm are both listed as "notable changes", the 2019 text was weaker on both. I did not read the 2019 full text, so the exact 2019 wording is not quoted here.

### Gaps
- 2019 full text not retrieved (paywalled). Only the abstract and the 2026 description of it were read; individual 2019 statements are not quoted.
- Supplemental figures and tables of the 2026 guideline (list of systematic reviews by drug, patient survey appendix, TROOPS figure) were not extracted.

## 3. ASA 2018 moderate-sedation guideline, ASA 2023 fasting update, continuum of depth of sedation, ASA physical status

### Takeaway
ASA defines four depths (minimal, moderate, deep, general anesthesia) by responsiveness, airway, ventilation and circulation; ACEP adds dissociative sedation as a separate state and now tells practitioners to judge safety by ventilatory adequacy, not responsiveness. ASA elective fasting intervals (2 h clear liquids, 4 h breast milk, 6 h formula/non-human milk/light meal) are the reference standard that ER evidence does not support enforcing.

### Cited Findings
**ASA continuum** (Statement on Continuum of Depth of Sedation; original approval 13 Oct 1999, last amended 23 Oct 2024) [verified] — [ASA statement](https://www.asahq.org/standards-and-practice-parameters/statement-on-continuum-of-depth-of-sedation-definition-of-general-anesthesia-and-levels-of-sedation-analgesia)

| | Minimal (anxiolysis) | Moderate ("conscious sedation") | Deep | General anesthesia |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Responsiveness | Normal response to verbal stimulation | Purposeful response to verbal or tactile stimulation | Purposeful response following repeated or painful stimulation | Unarousable even with painful stimulus |
| Airway | Unaffected | No intervention required | Intervention may be required | Intervention often required |
| Spontaneous ventilation | Unaffected | Adequate | May be inadequate | Frequently inadequate |
| Cardiovascular function | Unaffected | Usually maintained | Usually maintained | May be impaired |

- "Reflex withdrawal from a painful stimulus is NOT considered a purposeful response." [verified] — same ASA statement
- Rescue rule: "Individuals administering Moderate Sedation/Analgesia ('Conscious Sedation') should be able to rescue patients who enter a state of Deep Sedation/Analgesia, while those administering Deep Sedation/Analgesia should be able to rescue patients who enter a state of General Anesthesia." [verified] — same ASA statement
- "It is not appropriate to continue the procedure at an unintended level of sedation." [verified] — same ASA statement
- **Dissociative sedation (ACEP):** "A trance-like cataleptic state induced by the dissociative drug ketamine characterized by profound analgesia and amnesia, with retention of protective airway reflexes, spontaneous respirations, and cardiopulmonary stability." Airway focus: "The airway may require repositioning. Effective spontaneous ventilation is essentially always maintained." [verified] — [Part 2 PDF, Figure 2](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- Ketamine thresholds stated by ACEP 2026: dissociation at "approximately 1.5 to 2 mg/kg intravenously (IV) in children and 1 to 1.5 mg/kg IV in teenagers and adults"; analgesia at about 0.2 to 0.3 mg/kg IV; "hallucinatory distortion" at about 0.6 to 0.7 mg/kg IV; more ketamine beyond the threshold "does not enhance or deepen sedation". [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- ACEP 2026 on depth: "Procedural sedation practice should focus on ventilatory adequacy for safety and patient responsiveness to optimize patient comfort". "There is no evidentiary or pharmacological basis for designating common procedural sedation agents as 'intended' or 'not intended' for general anesthesia, or for restricting them on this basis." (The second sentence is aimed at the ASA 2018 guideline, its reference 18.) [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- Targeting a depth is unreliable: ABEM, citing Schick A, Driver B, Moore JC, Fagerstrom E, Miner JR (Acad Emerg Med 2019;26(4):364-374), states that "targeting one versus another does not reliably result in the intended level of sedation." [secondary] — [ABEM Key Advance PDF](https://www.abem.org/key-advances_practice-advance_-procedural-sedation-adult)

**ASA 2018 guideline citation.** Practice Guidelines for Moderate Procedural Sedation and Analgesia 2018: A Report by the American Society of Anesthesiologists Task Force on Moderate Procedural Sedation and Analgesia, the American Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons, American College of Radiology, American Dental Association, American Society of Dentist Anesthesiologists, and Society of Interventional Radiology. Anesthesiology. 2018;128(3):437-479. doi:10.1097/ALN.0000000000002043. PMID 29334501. [verified citation; no abstract available] — [PubMed 29334501](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29334501/)
- It covers moderate sedation only; ACEP 2026 notes "Some guidelines omit deep or dissociative sedation", citing it. [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)

**ASA 2023 fasting citation.** Joshi GP, Abdelmalak BB, Weigel WA, Harbell MW, Kuo CI, Soriano SG, Stricker PA, Tipton T, Grant MD, Marbella AM, Agarkar M, Blanck JF, Domino KB. 2023 American Society of Anesthesiologists Practice Guidelines for Preoperative Fasting: Carbohydrate-containing Clear Liquids with or without Protein, Chewing Gum, and Pediatric Fasting Duration-A Modular Update of the 2017 American Society of Anesthesiologists Practice Guidelines for Preoperative Fasting. Anesthesiology. 2023;138(2):132-151. doi:10.1097/ALN.0000000000004381. PMID 36629465. [verified] — [PubMed 36629465](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36629465/)
- It is a modular update covering only "ingestion of carbohydrate-containing clear liquids with or without protein, chewing gum, and pediatric fasting duration"; the 2017 intervals otherwise stand. [verified] — [PubMed 36629465](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36629465/)
- ASA intervals for healthy patients before elective procedures: clear liquids 2 h, breast milk 4 h, infant formula 6 h, non-human milk 6 h, light meal 6 h, full/fatty meal 8 h. [secondary] — [Kaiser Permanente fasting table](https://thrive.kaiserpermanente.org/care-near-you/northern-california/santarosa/?p=770); [Becker's ASC summary](https://www.beckersasc.com/anesthesia/asa-updates-guidelines-for-preoperative-fasting)

**ASA physical status and risk**
- ICAPS/ACEP wording maps to ASA classes: "Severe systemic disease" (ASA III) is a mild aspiration risk factor; "Severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life" (ASA IV) is a moderate one. [verified] — [Part 2 PDF, Figure 3](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- "Patients of all ages with severe underlying systemic disease are at greater risk of adverse events (⊕⊕⊕⊕)." [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- Size of the effect (139,140 children, Beach et al, Anesthesiology 2016;124:80-88): major complications 9.10 per 10,000 with ASA above II versus 4.61 per 10,000 with ASA I or II; absolute difference 4.49 per 10,000; number needed to treat 2,227. [verified as reported in the 2026 guideline] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- ACEP 2026 conclusion: these data "provide no evidentiary justification for using ASA III versus I or II as a stipulated trigger for altered sedation planning such as requiring anesthesia consultation or care, or delaying sedation (⊕⊕⊕)." The ASA score "has poor predictive value and interrater agreement, and cannot reliably guide individual patient decisions (⊕⊕⊕)"; it "should be deemphasized or discontinued." [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- Aspiration deaths: none reported in children or healthy adults (ASA 1 or 2); "just nine reported deaths in adults of ASA physical status 3 or above" in the post-1984 literature, eight during upper GI endoscopy. [verified] — [PubMed 31792941](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31792941/); [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)

### Inferences
- Older teaching ("ASA III or higher needs anesthesia consult") is contradicted by the 2026 ACEP guideline, which asks for clinical judgment and frailty appraisal instead. Risk does rise with ASA class, but the absolute rise is small.
- ASA (2018) and ACEP (2026) disagree on whether propofol, ketamine and etomidate warrant anesthesia-level care. A hospital policy may follow either; the handout should name the conflict.

### Gaps
- [recalled] ASA class definitions: I healthy; II mild systemic disease; III severe systemic disease; IV severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life; V moribund, not expected to survive without the operation; VI brain-dead organ donor; "E" suffix for emergency. Not re-read on asahq.org.
- [recalled] ASA 2018 recommendations: continuous capnography "unless precluded or invalidated by the nature of the patient, procedure, or equipment"; continuous pulse oximetry with alarms; blood pressure before sedation and every 5 minutes once moderate sedation is established; a designated individual other than the proceduralist present to monitor, who may do minor interruptible tasks once the patient is stable; supplemental oxygen unless contraindicated; when drugs "intended for general anesthesia" (propofol, ketamine, etomidate) are used, provide care consistent with general anesthesia. The Anesthesiology full text was not opened; none of this wording is confirmed.
- [recalled] ASA 2023 recommendation content: healthy adults may drink carbohydrate-containing clear liquids until 2 hours before elective procedures; do not delay elective procedures in healthy adults who are chewing gum; evidence insufficient to recommend 1-hour over 2-hour clear-liquid fasting in children. Only the scope was verified.

## 4. Pre-sedation assessment (airway, OSA, obesity, age) and who should go to anesthesia / the OR

### Takeaway
ACEP 2026 asks for a focused history, oral-intake history and airway/pulmonary exam, lists graded risk factors, and explicitly drops Mallampati as a required element. Anesthesia referral is reserved for patients judged at unusually high risk on clinical grounds, or with moderate aspiration risk, when acuity allows.

### Cited Findings (all [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf) unless noted)
- Pre-sedation evaluation components: assess the procedure (pain, duration, motion control), comfort needs, timeliness, history, oral intake, physical exam, then overall risk. Urgent conditions "may require streamlining this process."
- **Mallampati:** "should not be required as part of the airway evaluation for procedural sedation as no evidence supports its impact on clinical outcomes." It "poorly predicts both difficult bag mask ventilation and endotracheal intubation, is unreliably assessed, and often cannot be obtained in younger children (⊕⊕⊕⊕)." Supporting paper: Green SM, Roback MG. Ann Emerg Med. 2019;74:251-259.
- Physical exam targets: "airway abnormalities (eg, micrognathia, macroglossia, laryngomalacia, tonsillar hypertrophy), short neck, or severe obesity."
- **Adults:** obesity "modestly greater risk of airway and respiratory adverse events (⊕⊕)"; obstructive sleep apnea "conflicting evidence (⊕)"; "Although elderly adults are at greater risk of adverse events, most remain appropriate candidates for sedation (⊕⊕⊕)."
- **Highest-risk adults:** "Patients with moderate to severe congestive heart failure, pulmonary edema, valvular disease, and/or acute myocardial infarction present a particularly high risk, and may tolerate only minimal sedation if at all."
- **Children:**
  - Known or suspected OSA: "approximately five-times greater risk of airway and respiratory adverse events (⊕⊕)."
  - About double the risk with moderate to severe obesity (⊕⊕⊕), preterm birth under 37 weeks (⊕⊕), current or recent upper respiratory infection (⊕⊕⊕), autism spectrum disorder (⊕⊕), and young age (under 1 or under 2 years, ⊕⊕⊕).
  - "Procedural sedation is generally avoided in children <6 months of age (⊕⊕)."
- **Procedure:** "upper endoscopy and bronchoscopy, carry greater risk of airway and respiratory adverse events (⊕⊕⊕⊕)."
- Frailty is named as a plausible risk marker to appraise.
- Opioid use disorder: "non-opioid sedation regimens would appear more likely to be successful (⊕)."
- Pregnancy: "insufficient evidence to guide sedative agent selection in pregnancy (⊕)." Breastfeeding: normal breastfeeding after ketamine or propofol; also after single doses of fentanyl or midazolam, "with extra caution when babies are aged 6 weeks or less."
- Unscheduled sedation is not riskier than elective: "serious adverse event profiles are similar during unscheduled and elective procedural sedation (⊕⊕⊕)."
- **When to refer:** "For patients judged at unusually high risk based upon such clinical judgment, consider referral for anesthesia care when patient acuity allows, recognizing the delays required to arrange an operating room, anesthesia services, and an operating surgeon or proceduralist." Moderate aspiration risk factors are the other stated trigger (section 2 table).
- Dosing in obesity: "For obese patients, most drug doses should be adjusted based on ideal body weight rather than actual weight."
- Consent: discuss plan, risks, benefits, alternatives in the preferred language; "such consent may be verbal rather than written in many settings", but must be documented; may proceed without consent in some emergencies.
- Room and supplies: "oxygen, suction, physiological monitoring equipment, resuscitation medications, and age- and size-appropriate equipment for airway and ventilatory rescue (e.g., bag-valve mask, oral airway, nasal airway) and for IV access (⊕⊕⊕⊕). When using opioids or benzodiazepines, reversal agents must be readily available." A pre-procedure "time out" is expected, shortened or skipped in emergencies.
- IV access is preferred but not mandatory: IM ketamine without IV access has "similar safety (⊕⊕⊕⊕)"; expertise to start an IV must be immediately available.
- AAP/AAPD 2019 asks for "a focused airway examination for large (kissing) tonsils or anatomic airway abnormalities that might increase the potential for airway obstruction". [verified] — [PubMed 31138666](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31138666/)
- Alternatives first: ABEM lists hematoma blocks and regional nerve blocks as options "to reduce risks of adverse events." [secondary] — [ABEM Key Advance PDF](https://www.abem.org/key-advances_practice-advance_-procedural-sedation-adult)

### Inferences
- LEMON and STOP-BANG are not named in the 2026 ACEP guideline. If the handout includes them, label them as screening aids borrowed from airway management and sleep medicine, with the note that ACEP found only low or very low certainty for OSA in adults and rejects mandatory Mallampati scoring.
- A practical referral list supported by the sources: anticipated need for assisted ventilation or advanced airway management, significant airway abnormality, severe obesity with OSA, bowel obstruction or esophageal disorder, ASA IV physiology, decompensated heart failure or acute MI, and infants under 6 months.

### Gaps
- [recalled] LEMON = Look externally, Evaluate 3-3-2, Mallampati, Obstruction/Obesity, Neck mobility. STOP-BANG = Snoring, Tired, Observed apnea, Pressure (hypertension), BMI over 35, Age over 50, Neck circumference over 40 cm, male Gender; a score of 3 or more suggests OSA risk. Not looked up; no PSA-specific validation was found in this search.

## 5. Monitoring: capnography, supplemental oxygen, pulse oximetry, blood pressure, dedicated monitor, CMS and Joint Commission

### Takeaway
Capnography detects hypoventilation before desaturation and cut hypoxia from 42% to 25% in the Deitch 2010 RCT, but the 2017 Cochrane review found no convincing reduction in clinically significant events. ACEP 2026 nonetheless recommends routine capnography whenever feasible and makes supplemental oxygen optional.

### Cited Findings
**Capnography trials**
- Deitch K, Miner J, Chudnofsky CR, Dominici P, Latta D. Does end tidal CO2 monitoring during emergency department procedural sedation and analgesia with propofol decrease the incidence of hypoxic events? A randomized, controlled trial. Ann Emerg Med. 2010;55(3):258-264. doi:10.1016/j.annemergmed.2009.07.030. PMID 19783324. [verified] — [PubMed 19783324](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19783324/)
  - Adults, propofol 1 mg/kg then 0.5 mg/kg as needed, all on 3 L/min oxygen, opioid more than 30 minutes earlier; n=132.
  - Hypoxia (SpO2 below 93%): 17/68 (25%) with capnography visible vs 27/64 (42%) blinded; P=.035; difference 17% (95% CI 1.3% to 33%).
  - "Capnography identified all cases of hypoxia before onset (sensitivity 100%; specificity 64%)"; median lead time 60 seconds (range 5 to 240).
- Wall BF, Magee K, Campbell SG, Zed PJ. Capnography versus standard monitoring for emergency department procedural sedation and analgesia. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2017;3(3):CD010698. doi:10.1002/14651858.CD010698.pub2. PMID 28334427. PMCID PMC6353146. [verified] — [PubMed 28334427](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28334427/)
  - 3 trials, 1,272 participants.
  - Oxygen desaturation RR 0.89 (95% CI 0.48 to 1.63); hypotension RR 2.36 (0.98 to 5.69; 1 trial, n=986); airway interventions RR 1.26 (0.94 to 1.69).
  - Adult subgroup: more airway interventions with capnography, RR 1.44 (1.16 to 1.79; 2 trials, n=1,118), number needed to harm 12.
  - Conclusion: "There is a lack of convincing evidence that the addition of capnography to standard monitoring in ED PSA reduces the rate of clinically significant adverse events." Evidence moderate quality.
- ACEP 2014: capnography "may be used as an adjunct" (Level B). [secondary] — [ABEM Key Advance PDF](https://www.abem.org/key-advances_practice-advance_-procedural-sedation-adult)
- ACEP 2026 (STRONG CONSENSUS) [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf):
  - "Cardiac rhythm monitoring, blood pressure assessment, capnography, and pulse oximetry with audible tone should be used routinely whenever feasible (⊕⊕⊕⊕)."
  - Capnography "provides the earliest warning of respiratory depression and apnea, and can reduce the risk of developing hypoxia (⊕⊕⊕⊕)."
  - "When using supplemental oxygen, capnography is the sole monitoring device to promptly identify impaired ventilation."
  - Abnormal readings should prompt clinicians to "reevaluate patients, prepare to provide ventilatory support and/or to administer a reversal agent, and avoid additional sedative doses until concerns resolve."
- Deitch's definition of capnographic respiratory depression: ETCO2 above 50 mm Hg, a change of 10% (2010 trial) or 10 mm Hg (2007/2008 trials) from baseline, or loss of waveform. [verified] — [PubMed 19783324](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19783324/); [PubMed 18294729](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18294729/)
- UK comparator: RCEM 2022 lists "three lead ECG, oxygen saturations, continuous capnography, and non-invasive blood pressure". [secondary] — [ABEM Key Advance PDF](https://www.abem.org/key-advances_practice-advance_-procedural-sedation-adult)

**Supplemental oxygen trials (Deitch series)**
- Deitch K, Chudnofsky CR, Dominici P. The utility of supplemental oxygen during emergency department procedural sedation and analgesia with midazolam and fentanyl: a randomized, controlled trial. Ann Emerg Med. 2007;49(1):1-8. doi:10.1016/j.annemergmed.2006.06.013. PMID 16978741. [verified] — [PubMed 16978741](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16978741/)
  - Oxygen 2 L/min vs compressed air by nasal cannula; n=80 (44 vs 36).
  - Hypoxia (SpO2 below 90%): 6 vs 5 patients; P=.97; effect size 0% (95% CI -15% to +15%).
  - Physicians recognized respiratory depression in 0 of 28 patients who met ETCO2 criteria without hypoxia.
- Deitch K, Chudnofsky CR, Dominici P. The utility of supplemental oxygen during emergency department procedural sedation with propofol: a randomized, controlled trial. Ann Emerg Med. 2008;52(1):1-8. doi:10.1016/j.annemergmed.2007.11.040. PMID 18294729. [verified] — [PubMed 18294729](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18294729/)
  - Oxygen 3 L/min vs compressed air by nasal cannula; n=110 (56 vs 54).
  - Hypoxia (SpO2 93% or less): 10 (18%) vs 15 (28%); P=.3; not significant and below the 20% target.
- Deitch K, Chudnofsky CR, Dominici P, Latta D, Salamanca Y. The utility of high-flow oxygen during emergency department procedural sedation and analgesia with propofol: a randomized, controlled trial. Ann Emerg Med. 2011;58(4):360-364.e3. doi:10.1016/j.annemergmed.2011.05.018. PMID 21680059. [verified] — [PubMed 21680059](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21680059/)
  - 100% oxygen vs compressed air at 15 L/min by nonrebreather for 5 minutes before and during; n=117 (59 vs 58).
  - Hypoxia (SpO2 below 93%): 19% vs 41%; P=.007; difference 23% (95% CI 6% to 38%).
  - Capnographic respiratory depression similar: 51% vs 48%.
- ACEP 2026 on oxygen [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf):
  - Supplemental oxygen "can decrease the frequency of hypoxemic episodes during sedation (⊕⊕⊕⊕)", but no study found fewer events needing rescue (⊕⊕).
  - Li et al (Ann Emerg Med 2024;84:473-485; 85,599 pediatric sedations): no association of supplemental oxygen with rescue interventions.
  - "its use should be considered optional and not required (⊕⊕)." Oxygen "must always be immediately available".
  - Critics' point recorded in the guideline: oxygen "renders pulse oximetry ineffective as an early warning device".

**Pulse oximetry, blood pressure, observation**
- "The sedation team must continuously assess airway patency, ventilation quality, and sedation status through direct observation and physical examination"; this needs "an unobstructed view of the airway, chest, and skin." [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- Timing: "Airway and respiratory adverse events occur most frequently during induction and the immediate post-painful procedure phases, and rarely during recovery." [verified] — same
- Blood pressure "should be assessed prior to sedation and at appropriate intervals thereafter"; no fixed interval is given. More frequent measurement for higher-risk patients and volume depletion; rehydrate before sedation when possible. [verified] — same
- Uncooperative patients: sedation "may need to begin without these devices, which should then be applied at the earliest point", "prioritizing pulse oximetry and capnography." [verified] — same
- Hypoxia definitions used in the ER trials: SpO2 below 93% (Deitch 2008/2010/2011) or below 90% (Deitch 2007). [verified] — PubMed records above

**Dedicated monitoring nurse / personnel**
- ACEP 2014 Level C: "a nurse or other qualified individual should be present for continuous monitoring of the patient, in addition to the provider performing the procedure." [secondary] — [ABEM Key Advance PDF](https://www.abem.org/key-advances_practice-advance_-procedural-sedation-adult)
- ACEP 2026: second practitioner "highly recommended"; see section 2. [verified] — [Part 1 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-1-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)

**CMS and Joint Commission**
- CMS State Operations Manual Appendix A, 42 CFR 482.52 interpretive guidelines (Transmittal 74, 2 Dec 2011) [verified] — [CMS Transmittal 74](https://www.cms.gov/regulations-and-guidance/guidance/transmittals/downloads/r74soma.pdf):
  - "CMS, consistent with ASA guidelines, does not define moderate or conscious sedation as anesthesia (71 FR 68690-1)." Minimal sedation "is also not anesthesia."
  - Deep sedation is included in monitored anesthesia care: "it is necessary that the administration of deep sedation/analgesia be delivered or supervised by a practitioner as specified in 42 CFR 482.52(a)."
  - Rescue capacity: "hospitals must ensure that procedures are in place to rescue patients whose level of sedation becomes deeper than initially intended"; rescue "requires an intervention by a practitioner with expertise in airway management and advanced life support."
  - Hospitals must set policies "based on nationally recognized guidelines"; "We encourage hospitals to address whether the sedation typically provided in the emergency department or procedure rooms involves anesthesia or analgesia."
  - Policies must "address the minimum qualifications and supervision requirements for each category of practitioner who is permitted to provide analgesia services, particularly moderate sedation." All anesthesia services are organized under one anesthesia service, and the emergency department is a listed location.
  - Surveyor probe: "Does the hospital have a system by which adverse events related to the administration of anesthesia and analgesia, including moderate sedation, are tracked and acted upon?"
- CMS language on emergency physicians (Revised Hospital Anesthesia Services Interpretive Guidelines, 14 Jan 2011), as quoted in ACEP 2026: "emergency medicine–trained physicians have very specific skill sets to manage airways and ventilation that is necessary to provide patient rescue. Therefore, these practitioners are uniquely qualified to provide all levels of analgesia/sedation." [secondary; quoted inside the ACEP guideline] — [Part 1 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-1-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- Joint Commission: treats moderate sedation under its anesthesia-related Provision of Care standards and expects pre-sedation and post-sedation assessments. [secondary; web search summaries of TJC FAQ and trade analysis] — [Joint Commission FAQ](https://www.jointcommission.org/standards/standard-faqs/ambulatory/provision-of-care-treatment-and-services-pc/000002231)
- Critique: Green SM, Norse AB, Jackson BF, Carman MJ, Roback MG. Regulatory Challenges to Emergency Medicine Procedural Sedation. Ann Emerg Med. 2021;77(1):91-102. doi:10.1016/j.annemergmed.2020.08.004. PMID 33353592. "national regulatory guidance on procedural sedation has elements that are contradictory, confusing, and out of date." [verified] — [PubMed 33353592](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33353592/)
- Effect on practice: a 2018 survey found restrictions on key aspects of sedation in 43% of California emergency departments, with deep sedation prohibited in 18%. [verified as reported in ACEP 2026] — [Part 1 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-1-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Cochrane conclusion and the ACEP 2026 recommendation are not contradictory in the facts: capnography reliably gives earlier warning and less transient hypoxia, while no ER trial was powered for serious outcomes. ACEP weighs the low cost and early warning; Cochrane weighs the absent hard-outcome benefit.
- If the unit gives routine oxygen, capnography becomes more important, because oxygen delays desaturation and so blunts pulse oximetry as a ventilation alarm.

### Gaps
- [recalled] Blood pressure every 5 minutes during moderate sedation is an ASA 2018 recommendation, and AAP/AAPD 2019 asks for vital signs at least every 10 minutes for moderate and every 5 minutes for deep sedation. ACEP 2026 gives no fixed interval. Unconfirmed.
- [recalled] Joint Commission element numbers (PC.03.01.01 planning, PC.03.01.03 pre-sedation assessment and immediate re-evaluation, PC.03.01.05 monitoring, PC.03.01.07 post-procedure care) and its requirement that those giving moderate or deep sedation be qualified to rescue one level deeper. The current standards manual was not read.
- The January 2011 CMS revision containing the emergency-physician sentences was not opened directly; the December 2011 transmittal I read does not contain them.

## 6. Adverse-event rates: adults (Bellolio 2016) and children (Bellolio 2016 BMJ Open; Bhatt 2017)

### Takeaway
In adults the commonest events are hypoxia (about 4%), vomiting (1.6%) and hypotension (1.5%); aspiration, laryngospasm and intubation each occur in roughly 1 to 4 per 1,000. In children serious adverse events run about 1.1%, lowest with ketamine alone (0.4%) and highest with propofol alone, ketamine plus fentanyl, and ketamine plus propofol.

## 7. Stepwise rescue of PSA complications

### Takeaway
ACEP 2026 says most rescues are repositioning and bag-mask ventilation, with naloxone and flumazenil as adjuncts when opioids or benzodiazepines were given; it gives no drug-dose algorithm. The specific doses the handout wants (laryngospasm, rigidity, myoclonus) come from older reviews and could not be verified in this search.

### Cited Findings
- "The hallmark of safe procedural sedation is the care team's ability to provide prompt and effective interventions". Rescue interventions "may be minor and preventative (e.g., tactile stimulation or airway repositioning) or they may be more invasive (e.g., suctioning, positive pressure ventilation)." [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- "The most common and important rescue interventions required during procedural sedation are airway repositioning and bag mask ventilation." [verified] — [Part 1 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-1-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- Required competencies include: "airway alignment maneuvers (head tilt, chin lift, jaw thrust) or placement of nasal or oral airway, or a supraglottic airway device"; "distinguish central and obstructive apnea"; "perform bag-mask ventilation"; "identify and manage hypoxia, hypotension, bradycardia, anaphylaxis, seizure, and cardiac arrest"; "summon additional resuscitation assistance". Endotracheal intubation is not obligatory for practitioners outside specialties that train in it. [verified] — [Part 1 PDF, Figure 6](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-1-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- **Hypoventilation/apnea:** on abnormal capnography, reassess, prepare ventilatory support and/or reversal, and hold further sedative doses. "naloxone may be used to reverse opioid-induced respiratory depression"; "flumazenil can be administered to reverse benzodiazepine-induced oversedation". [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- **Opioid plus sedative timing:** co-administration "appears to modestly increase adverse event risk (⊕⊕⊕)"; "Clinicians should be particularly attentive to cardiopulmonary depression when sedation occurs within approximately 30 minutes of opioid administration." Source: Bhatt M, Cheng W, Roback MG. Acad Emerg Med. 2020;27:217-227. [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- **Fentanyl/midazolam titration:** slower, less predictable onset, "risking either undersedation ... or oversedation with respiratory depression." [verified] — same
- **Laryngospasm:** epidemiology and risk factors in section 6 (33 of 34 pediatric cases with ketamine; 3.3 per 1,000 in the registry). [verified] — [PubMed 27311910](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27311910/); [PubMed 35752522](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35752522/)
- **Anticholinergics:** co-administered anticholinergics "are associated with more frequent adverse events and are no longer recommended (⊕⊕⊕)." [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- **Vomiting/aspiration:** antiemetics for "recovery nausea and vomiting should also be readily accessible"; suction must be in the room. Adult vomiting with ketamine 170.0 per 1,000 vs 16.4 overall. [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf); [PubMed 26801209](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26801209/)
- **Hypotension:** "Clinically important hypotension rarely occurs during procedural sedation in patients without serious systemic disease or acute cardiovascular compromise." Rehydrate volume-depleted patients before sedation; measure blood pressure more often in higher-risk patients; propofol hypotension in the elderly and in longer sedations is flagged as a research priority. [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- **Ketamine emergence (recovery agitation), prophylaxis:** Sener S, Eken C, Schultz CH, Serinken M, Ozsarac M. Ketamine with and without midazolam for emergency department sedation in adults: a randomized controlled trial. Ann Emerg Med. 2011;57(2):109-114.e2. doi:10.1016/j.annemergmed.2010.09.010. PMID 20970888. [verified] — [PubMed 20970888](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20970888/)
  - Double-blind 2x2 factorial; 182 adults aged 18 to 50; midazolam 0.03 mg/kg IV vs placebo; ketamine 1.5 mg/kg IV or 4 mg/kg IM.
  - Recovery agitation 8% with midazolam vs 25% with placebo; difference 17% (95% CI 6% to 28%); number needed to treat 6.
  - IV vs IM ketamine: recovery agitation 13% vs 17%; nausea/vomiting 28% vs 34%; respiratory events 0% vs 0%.
- ACEP 2026: "Adjunctive midazolam in adults, for example, modestly decreases recovery agitation with ketamine, while no similar benefit in children has been demonstrated, where recovery agitation occurs less frequently (⊕⊕⊕⊕)." [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- Adult agitation with ketamine: 164.1 per 1,000. [verified] — [PubMed 26801209](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26801209/)
- **Fentanyl chest wall rigidity:** listed by ABEM as "Chest wall rigidity (when given rapidly in large doses)". [secondary] — [ABEM Key Advance PDF](https://www.abem.org/key-advances_practice-advance_-procedural-sedation-adult)
- **Etomidate myoclonus** and **propofol injection pain**: both listed as disadvantages in ABEM's agent table (etomidate also "Respiratory depression"; propofol also hypotension and respiratory depression). [secondary] — [ABEM Key Advance PDF](https://www.abem.org/key-advances_practice-advance_-procedural-sedation-adult)
- Behavioral measures (calm environment, explanation, distraction, child life) reduce distress (⊕⊕); immobilization boards "should be avoided". [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- Rare events justify simulation: "Critical adverse events during procedural sedation are rare enough that many practitioners never encounter them in their careers." [verified] — [Part 1 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-1-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)

### Inferences
- A defensible stepwise order from the verified material: stop the stimulus and drug; reposition (head tilt, chin lift, jaw thrust); suction; oral or nasal airway; bag-mask ventilation; reversal agent if an opioid or benzodiazepine was given; supraglottic airway or intubation only if those fail; call for help early.
- Pharmacist-relevant bedside stock implied by the guidelines: naloxone, flumazenil, an antiemetic, allergy medications, resuscitation drugs, and (from the unverified laryngospasm algorithm) succinylcholine with a dose pre-calculated.

### Gaps (all [recalled]; none confirmed in this search, so cite a primary source such as the 2011 ketamine guideline, PMID 21256625, before printing)
- Laryngospasm sequence: 100% oxygen and jaw thrust with firm pressure at the laryngospasm notch (Larson's point, behind the earlobe between mandibular ramus and mastoid); continuous positive pressure by bag-mask; deepen with propofol about 0.5 mg/kg IV; succinylcholine 0.1 to 0.5 mg/kg IV for partial relief, 1 to 2 mg/kg IV with intubation if needed, or 4 mg/kg IM without IV access. The doses match the task brief but I did not read them in a source.
- Fentanyl rigidity: associated with rapid, high-dose injection; treated with naloxone, or succinylcholine and ventilation if ventilation is impossible.
- Etomidate myoclonus: frequent, brief, self-limited; midazolam if it interferes with the procedure. Incidence figures belong to the drug researcher's notes.
- Propofol injection pain: reduced by a large antecubital vein or lidocaine pretreatment (Jalota L et al, BMJ 2011;342:d1110).
- Ketamine vomiting: ondansetron prophylaxis in children lowered vomiting (Langston WT et al, Ann Emerg Med 2008;52:30-34; about 12.6% to 4.7%).
- Emergence reaction treatment: titrated IV midazolam is the usual treatment; ACEP 2026 addresses only prophylaxis.
- Naloxone and flumazenil rescue doses, and flumazenil's seizure risk in benzodiazepine-dependent patients: not sourced here.
- Hypotension treatment specifics (fluid bolus, vasopressor choice): no guideline text found.

## 8. Fasting evidence in the ER

### Takeaway
Four ER cohorts totalling more than 10,000 sedated children found no link between fasting status and adverse events, and no aspirations. ACEP 2014 (Level B), ICAPS 2020 and ACEP 2026 all say not to delay urgent sedation for fasting.

### Cited Findings
- Bhatt M, Johnson DW, Taljaard M, Chan J, Barrowman N, Farion KJ, Ali S, Beno S, Dixon A, McTimoney CM, Dubrovsky AS, Roback MG; Sedation Safety Study Group of Pediatric Emergency Research Canada. Association of Preprocedural Fasting With Outcomes of Emergency Department Sedation in Children. JAMA Pediatr. 2018;172(7):678-685. doi:10.1001/jamapediatrics.2018.0830. PMID 29800944. PMCID PMC6137504. [verified] — [PubMed 29800944](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29800944/)
  - n=6,183; median age 8.0 years; 99.7% ASA I or II.
  - Did not meet ASA fasting guidelines: 2,974 (48.1%) for solids, 310 (5.0%) for liquids.
  - "There were no cases of pulmonary aspiration."
  - Any adverse event 717 (11.6%); serious adverse events 68 (1.1%); vomiting 315 (5.1%).
  - Per additional hour of fasting, solids: any event OR 1.00 (0.98 to 1.02); serious events OR 1.01 (0.95 to 1.07); vomiting OR 1.00 (0.97 to 1.03). Liquids: same pattern.
  - Conclusion: "These findings do not support delaying sedation to meet established fasting guidelines."
- Roback MG, Bajaj L, Wathen JE, Bothner J. Preprocedural fasting and adverse events in procedural sedation and analgesia in a pediatric emergency department: are they related? Ann Emerg Med. 2004;44(5):454-459. doi:10.1016/j.annemergmed.2004.03.015. PMID 15520704. [verified] — [PubMed 15520704](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15520704/)
  - 2,085 parenteral sedations; fasting time documented in 1,555; median fast 5.1 hours.
  - Desaturation 8.1%; vomiting 7.5%; apnea 0.8%; laryngospasm 0.1%.
  - No difference across fasting strata (0 to 2, 2 to 4, 4 to 6, 6 to 8, over 8 hours). "No patients experienced clinically apparent aspiration."
- Agrawal D, Manzi SF, Gupta R, Krauss B. Preprocedural fasting state and adverse events in children undergoing procedural sedation and analgesia in a pediatric emergency department. Ann Emerg Med. 2003;42(5):636-646. doi:10.1016/s0196-0644(03)00516-x. PMID 14581915. [verified] — [PubMed 14581915](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14581915/)
  - 1,014 sedations; fasting data on 905; 509 (56%) did not meet fasting guidelines.
  - Adverse events 8.1% in those who met guidelines vs 6.9% in those who did not; emesis 1.5%; no aspiration (one-sided 97.5% CI 0% to 0.4%).
- Green SM, Roback MG, Miner JR, Burton JH, Krauss B. Fasting and emergency department procedural sedation and analgesia: a consensus-based clinical practice advisory. Ann Emerg Med. 2007;49(4):454-461. doi:10.1016/j.annemergmed.2006.08.017. PMID 17083995. [verified] — [PubMed 17083995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17083995/)
  - Purpose: "a tool to permit emergency physicians to identify prudent limits of sedation depth and timing in light of fasting status and individual patient risk factors." Now historical; replaced by the ICAPS algorithm.
- ACEP 2014, Level B: "Do not delay procedural sedation in adults or pediatrics in the ED based on fasting time. Preprocedural fasting for any duration has not demonstrated a reduction in the risk of emesis or aspiration when administering procedural sedation and analgesia." [secondary] — [ABEM Key Advance PDF](https://www.abem.org/key-advances_practice-advance_-procedural-sedation-adult)
- ICAPS 2020: Green SM, Leroy PL, Roback MG, Irwin MG, Andolfatto G, Babl FE, Barbi E, Costa LR, Absalom A, Carlson DW, Krauss BS, Roelofse J, Yuen VM, Alcaino E, Costa PS, Mason KP; International Committee for the Advancement of Procedural Sedation. An international multidisciplinary consensus statement on fasting before procedural sedation in adults and children. Anaesthesia. 2020;75(3):374-385. doi:10.1111/anae.14892. PMID 31792941. PMCID PMC7064977. [verified] — [PubMed 31792941](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31792941/)
  - "Fasting does not guarantee an empty stomach, and there is no observed association between aspiration and compliance with common fasting guidelines."
  - "The probability of clinically important aspiration during procedural sedation is negligible."
  - Risk-factor evidence table: esophageal endoscopy in adults is the only high-quality risk factor; not risk factors (moderate quality): emergency procedure, absence of fasting, upper respiratory infection in children, pregnancy.
  - "We did not include a high-risk category, because even with the most notable risk factors, the evidence suggests that aspiration remains uncommon."
  - Best incidence estimate: aspiration with procedural sedation about 1:13,914 (pediatric study); with general anesthesia about 1:7,103 in adults and 1:4,800 in children. [verified] — [ICAPS fasting page](https://proceduralsedation.org/fasting/)
- Real-world test of the ICAPS rule: Hara Y, Hagiwara Y, Inoue N. Pediatr Int. 2022;64(1):e14840. doi:10.1111/ped.14840. PMID 33991157. 857 pediatric ER sedations; adherence 95.7% to ICAPS vs 41.0% to ASA fasting times; complications 14.2% vs 15.5% (P=0.586); no aspiration. [verified] — [PubMed 33991157](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33991157/)
- ABEM focus point: "Nothing by mouth (NPO) status has not been shown to reduce the risk of adverse events." [secondary] — [ABEM Key Advance PDF](https://www.abem.org/key-advances_practice-advance_-procedural-sedation-adult)

### Inferences
- The direct ER fasting evidence is pediatric and mostly ketamine-based; adult support comes from the ICAPS review and the Green 2017 systematic review. The handout should say so when it applies "do not delay" to adults receiving propofol.
- Prolonged fasting has its own harms (irritability, dehydration, hypoglycemia; rated ⊕⊕⊕⊕ by ACEP 2026), which is the argument against "wait until NPO".

### Gaps
- No ER randomized trial of fasting versus no fasting exists in the sources read; ACEP 2026 calls for large prospective studies.
- The fasting-and-depth table from the 2007 advisory was not retrieved.

## 9. Recovery, discharge criteria and discharge instructions

### Takeaway
ACEP 2026 requires return to baseline consciousness and vital signs with no ongoing airway or respiratory risk, discharge to a responsible adult with verbal and written instructions, and no oral-intake test. Serious events after the procedure are rare; in the Newman pediatric series they almost never occurred more than 25 minutes after the last dose.

### Cited Findings
- ACEP 2026 (STRONG CONSENSUS): "patients should be monitored until they are no longer at risk for airway obstruction or respiratory depression, they are alert and at age-appropriate baseline levels of consciousness, and they have normal or baseline vital signs." [verified] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- "Many institutions use specific discharge scoring scales for this purpose and practitioners should adhere to local policies." [verified] — same
- "There is no literature supporting a need to establish a willingness or ability to take oral liquids." [verified] — same
- Discharge "should be to the care of a responsible adult, with appropriate verbal and written care instructions given to the patient and their family or caregivers." [verified] — same
- "Children normally capable of sitting upright unaided should be able to again do so before being transferred to a car or booster seat." [verified] — same
- Admitted or transferred patients need a practitioner-to-practitioner hand-off. [verified] — same
- Documentation: pre-sedation evaluation, drugs with doses and timing, adverse events and interventions, serial monitoring; may be completed after the procedure in emergencies. [verified] — same
- Newman DH, Azer MM, Pitetti RD, Singh S. When is a patient safe for discharge after procedural sedation? The timing of adverse effect events in 1367 pediatric procedural sedations. Ann Emerg Med. 2003;42(5):627-635. doi:10.1016/s0196-0644(03)00446-3. PMID 14581914. [verified] — [PubMed 14581914](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14581914/)
  - 1,341 sedation events analyzed; 184 (13.7%) adverse effects, 159 (11.9%) serious (hypoxia, stridor, hypotension).
  - 92% occurred during the procedure, 8% after.
  - Serious effects at a median of 2 minutes after the final dose (range -106 to +40 minutes).
  - Hypoxia at 26, 30 and 40 minutes occurred only in children with earlier hypoxia.
  - Conclusion: "discharge from the ED may be safe at approximately 30 minutes after final sedation medication administration if no adverse effects have occurred." The MeSH terms indicate ketamine, midazolam and fentanyl regimens; it is not a propofol study.
- Timing across the encounter: adverse events are most frequent "during induction and the immediate post-painful procedure phases, and rarely during recovery" (Boriosi JP et al, Hosp Pediatr 2022;12:930-936). [verified as cited in ACEP 2026] — [Part 2 PDF](https://www.acmt.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Part-2-Unscheduled-Procedural-Sedation.pdf)
- AAP/AAPD 2019 requires "a properly equipped and staffed recovery area, recovery to the presedation level of consciousness before discharge from medical/dental supervision, and appropriate discharge instructions." [verified] — [PubMed 31138666](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31138666/)
- ACEP policy statement: institutional guidelines should include "criteria for discharge". RCEM 2022 encourages a patient advice leaflet. [secondary] — [ABEM Key Advance PDF](https://www.abem.org/key-advances_practice-advance_-procedural-sedation-adult)

### Inferences
- The "post-painful procedure" risk window matters for pharmacists: once the stimulus (for example a reduction) ends, opioid and sedative effect is unopposed, so monitoring should not relax at that moment.
- Newman's 30-minute figure applies to children on ketamine or midazolam/fentanyl regimens with no earlier adverse event; it should not be generalized to adults or to long-acting drugs without saying so.

### Gaps
- [recalled] Aldrete score: five items (activity, respiration, circulation, consciousness, oxygenation/color) scored 0 to 2; a total of 9 or more is the usual discharge threshold. The modified Aldrete replaces color with SpO2. Not cited by ACEP 2026 and not looked up.
- [recalled] Typical written instructions: no driving or hazardous activity for 12 to 24 hours, no alcohol or other sedatives, supervision by an adult, advance diet as tolerated, return for breathing difficulty, persistent vomiting or unusual drowsiness. No guideline text was found that lists these items.
- No adult ER study of post-sedation event timing after propofol or ketamine was retrieved.

## 10. Other guideline and consensus updates 2018 to 2026; adverse-event reporting tools

### Takeaway
The major post-2019 developments are the ACEP 2026 two-part guideline, the ICAPS fasting statement (2020), the ICAPS definition (2021) and competencies (2025), and TROOPS as the recommended reporting tool. I found no SAEM GRACE guideline on PSA and no ICAPS nitrous oxide consensus statement.

- "TRACERS" in the assignment appears to be a mis-recollection of TROOPS; no tool called TRACERS turned up in the guideline text, its references or PubMed.
- The lineage of reporting tools is Quebec criteria (2009, pediatric ER, threshold-based) to World SIVA (2012, all settings, intervention-based) to TROOPS (2018, ICAPS; the one ACEP now recommends).

### Gaps
- **ICAPS nitrous oxide statement (2021):** not found. A PubMed search on the committee name returned the fasting statement, the definition editorial and TROOPS only. ACEP 2026 mentions nitrous oxide only as safe without IV access (⊕⊕⊕). Treat the premise as unconfirmed.
- **SAEM GRACE:** I found no GRACE guideline on procedural sedation; SAEM appears only as an endorser of ACEP 2026. [recalled] GRACE topics to date cover recurrent chest pain, recurrent abdominal pain, dizziness, and substance use, not PSA.
- **ACEP nurse-administered propofol/ketamine policy statement:** not retrieved as a separate document. Its substance appears in the 2023 policy statement (via ABEM) and in ACEP 2026 Part 1.
- The ESA 2018, AAP/AAPD 2019 and ASA 2018 full texts were not opened; only abstracts or citations were read.
- Has any of these been revised after June 2026? Only PubMed and one web search were checked; an AAP or ASA revision published in late 2026 could have been missed.
