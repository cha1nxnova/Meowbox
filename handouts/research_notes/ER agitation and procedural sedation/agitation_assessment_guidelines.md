## 1. ACEP Clinical Policy on severe agitation (2024): recommendations and grade wording; any newer policy, correction or update through 2026

### Takeaway
The only graded US emergency medicine guideline is the ACEP Clinical Policy approved 6 Oct 2023 and published January 2024. It answers one critical question (parenteral agents) and gives no Level A, a three-part Level B, and consensus-based Level C statements. I found no erratum, revision or replacement in PubMed through Nov 2026.

### Cited Findings
- Title carries "Approved by the ACEP Board of Directors, October 6, 2023"; published Ann Emerg Med Jan 2024;83(1):e1-e30; PubMed lists no abstract. [verified] — [PubMed 38105109](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2023.09.010)
- Critical question: "Is there a superior parenteral medication or combination of medications for the acute management of adult out-of-hospital or emergency department patients with severe agitation?" [secondary: quoted in the lead author's ACEP Now summary, 12 Feb 2024] — [ACEP Now, Thiessen](https://acepnow.com/article/aceps-new-clinical-policy-on-severe-agitation)
- Level A recommendations: none specified. [secondary] — [ACEP Now](https://acepnow.com/article/aceps-new-clinical-policy-on-severe-agitation)
- Level B recommendations (assembled from quoted fragments; fragment order is the tool's, so check exact sentence breaks against the Annals PDF): "For more rapid and efficacious treatment of severe agitation in the emergency department, use a combination of droperidol and midazolam or an atypical antipsychotic in combination with midazolam." "If a single agent must be administered, ... use droperidol or an atypical antipsychotic due to the adverse effect profile of midazolam alone." "For efficacious treatment of severe agitation in the emergency department, use ... haloperidol alone or in combination with lorazepam." [secondary] — [ACEP Now](https://acepnow.com/article/aceps-new-clinical-policy-on-severe-agitation)
- Level C recommendations: "In situations where safety of the patient, bystanders, or staff is a concern, consider ketamine (intravenous or intramuscular) to rapidly treat severe agitation in the ED" (consensus recommendation). Out-of-hospital: "No recommendations for or against the use of specific agents in the out-of-hospital setting can be made at this time" (consensus recommendation). [secondary] — [ACEP Now](https://acepnow.com/article/aceps-new-clinical-policy-on-severe-agitation)
- The policy makes no specific medication recommendation for patients older than 65 years. [secondary: search-result digest of the Guideline Central summary; page itself returned 403] — [Guideline Central](https://www.guidelinecentral.com/guideline/3303797/)
- Scope per the lead author: initial treatment of "undifferentiated agitation, in which the underlying etiology is unknown"; verbal de-escalation is first line, then oral or sublingual medication where safe. No formal numeric definition of "severe agitation" appears in the summary. [secondary] — [ACEP Now](https://acepnow.com/article/aceps-new-clinical-policy-on-severe-agitation)
- A later Annals study states that the ACEP policy "recommends injectable olanzapine and benzodiazepines be coadministered for treating severe agitation", against US labeling (which recommends against coadministration) and European labeling (not within 60 minutes). In 693 ED patients, intubation was 3.8% (21/549) with two olanzapine doses vs 3.5% (5/144) with olanzapine plus benzodiazepine (difference 0.3%, 95% CI -3.0% to 3.7%). [verified: abstract] — [Cole JB, Stang JL, Collins JD, Klein LR, DeVries PA, Smith J, Pepin LC, Fuchs RT, Driver BE. Ann Emerg Med. 2024;84(6):658-667. PMID 38878022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2024.05.006)
- ACEP level definitions (standard ACEP wording): Level A = "Generally accepted principles for patient care that reflect a high degree of clinical certainty"; Level B = "may identify a particular strategy or range of strategies that reflect moderate clinical certainty"; Level C = based on Class III evidence "or, in the absence of adequate published literature, based on expert consensus", with "consensus" placed in parentheses after the recommendation. [recalled] — no source opened this session; see Gaps.
- Earlier ACEP statements this policy effectively updates: 2017 adult psychiatric patient policy (Nazarian DJ et al. Ann Emerg Med. 2017;69:480-498), Level C that ketamine is one option for immediate sedation of the severely agitated patient; 2006 policy (Lukens TW et al. Ann Emerg Med. 2006;47:79-99), Level B benzodiazepine or conventional antipsychotic monotherapy for the undifferentiated agitated patient. [recalled] — not confirmed this session.

## 2. Project BETA (AAEP, 2012): medical evaluation, verbal de-escalation, psychopharmacology by cause, restraint; AAEP updates since

### Takeaway
Project BETA is a six-paper expert consensus (no evidence grades) built on four ideas: de-escalate first, treat the most likely cause, do a targeted medical work-up, and minimize restraint. It has not been formally revised for adults; the 2019 pediatric consensus and a 2020 implementation review are the only AAEP-linked follow-ups I found.

### Cited Findings
**Medical evaluation (Nordstrom 2012)**
- Agitation defined as "an extreme form of arousal that is associated with increased verbal and motor activity." [verified] — [Nordstrom 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6863)
- Triage in a medical setting: brief history and vital signs; "Oxygenation level and blood sugar level should also be obtained if possible"; "Unless immediate intervention is indicated, de-escalation should be attempted"; if the patient must be restrained, "oxygenation level and finger stick blood glucose should be done immediately if previously it could not be obtained." [verified] — [Nordstrom 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6863)
- "The goal of medication is to calm the patient enough to allow for a thorough evaluation." [verified] — [Nordstrom 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6863)
- Yield of history vs exam: history sensitivity 94%, physical examination 51% for detecting medical problems in psychiatric patients (citing Olshaker). [verified as quoted by BETA; Olshaker not opened] — [Nordstrom 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6863)
- Work-up by provisional category: general medical condition (targeted labs, neuroimaging or lumbar puncture as appropriate); intoxication/withdrawal (variable; fuller work-up for older adults, comorbidity, unknown ingestion); undifferentiated agitation "should be presumed to be from a general medical condition until proven otherwise"; known psychiatric disease with typical presentation, normal vitals and normal mentation, "little to no testing may be considered." [verified] — [Nordstrom 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6863)
- Minimum routine set: "complete set of vital signs, blood glucose measurement (finger stick), and determination of oxygenation level"; physical exam should be "focused, unclothed, but gowned" covering heart, lungs, abdomen, skin and neurologic system. [verified] — [Nordstrom 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6863)
- Consensus conclusions, verbatim: "1. Routine laboratory testing is not indicated; rather, directed testing should be based on the most likely diagnosis..." "2. New-onset agitation should be presumed to be agitation from a general medical condition." "3. There should be a high level of suspicion of agitation from a medical condition in a patient with concerning past medical history, such as immunosuppression, or when onset is outside the normal age ranges of psychiatric disease." [verified] — [Nordstrom 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6863)

**Verbal de-escalation (Richmond 2012)**
- Three-step approach: "First, the patient is verbally engaged; then a collaborative relationship is established; and, finally, the patient is verbally de-escalated out of the agitated state." [verified] — [Richmond 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6864)
- Four objectives: "(1) ensure the safety of the patient, staff, and others in the area; (2) help the patient manage his emotions and distress and maintain or regain control of his behavior; (3) avoid the use of restraint when at all possible; and (4) avoid coercive interventions that escalate agitation." [verified] — [Richmond 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6864)
- The 10 domains (headings verbatim): I Respect Personal Space; II Do Not Be Provocative; III Establish Verbal Contact; IV Be Concise; V Identify Wants and Feelings; VI Listen Closely to What the Patient Is Saying; VII Agree or Agree to Disagree; VIII Lay Down the Law and Set Clear Limits; IX Offer Choices and Optimism; X Debrief the Patient and Staff. [verified] — [Richmond 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6864)
- Practical details: keep "at least 2 arm's lengths of distance"; both parties must be able to exit; hands visible and unclenched, stand at an angle, avoid staring; repeat the message ("may have to repeat his message a dozen or more times"); "fogging" = agree with the truth, in principle, or with the odds; limit setting "must be reasonable and done in a respectful manner". [verified] — [Richmond 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6864)
- Time: "verbal de-escalation frequently can be successful in less than 5 minutes"; a dozen repetitions "would take 10 minutes at the most." [verified] — [Richmond 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6864)
- Team: "the de-escalation team should consist of 4 to 6 team members" (nurses, clinicians, technicians, police/security). [verified] — [Richmond 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6864)
- Medication within de-escalation: offer choices, let the request come from the patient if possible, offer oral vs parenteral choice; "when verbal attempts to de-escalate fail, more coercive measures such as restraints or injectable medication may be necessary to ensure safety but always as a last resort." [verified] — [Richmond 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6864)
- BARS at triage: "Any score other than a 4 should trigger an evaluation by a clinician." [verified] — [Richmond 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6864)

**Psychopharmacology by cause (Wilson 2012)**
- General recommendations, verbatim: "The use of medication as a restraint (ie, to restrict movement) should be discouraged." "Nonpharmacologic approaches, such as verbal de-escalation and reducing environmental stimulation (quiet room, low lighting), should be attempted, if possible, before medications are administered." "Medication should be used to calm patients, not to induce sleep." "Patients should be involved in the process of selecting medication to whatever extent possible." "If the patient is able to cooperate with taking oral medications, these are preferred over intramuscular preparations." [verified] — [Wilson 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6866)
- Stimulant/recreational drug intoxication: "benzodiazepines are generally considered first-line agents"; add a second-generation antipsychotic if amphetamine-related psychosis. [verified] — [Wilson 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6866)
- Alcohol (CNS depressant) intoxication: medication "should be used sparingly if at all"; "benzodiazepines should be avoided because of the potential to compound the risk of respiratory depression. Thus, antipsychotics are preferred. Haloperidol has the longest track record". Olanzapine or risperidone "may be a reasonable alternative". [verified] — [Wilson 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6866)
- Alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal: "a benzodiazepine is the agent of choice"; a chronic alcohol user with tachycardia, diaphoresis, tremor and low or undetectable alcohol level "should be presumed to be due to withdrawal". [verified] — [Wilson 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6866)
- Psychosis in known psychiatric illness: "antipsychotics are preferred over benzodiazepines"; second-generation agents preferred over haloperidol; oral risperidone has the strongest oral evidence; if not cooperative, "intramuscular ziprasidone or intramuscular olanzapine is preferred"; if the first antipsychotic dose is insufficient, add a benzodiazepine such as lorazepam instead of more antipsychotic. [verified] — [Wilson 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6866)
- Delirium (not from alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal or sleep deprivation): correct the cause; "second-generation antipsychotics are the preferred agents. Haloperidol is also acceptable in low doses. Benzodiazepines should be generally avoided because they can exacerbate the delirium." [verified] — [Wilson 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6866)
- Undifferentiated/complex agitation, non-delirious: "In patients who do not display psychosis ... a benzodiazepine is recommended as first-line treatment. An antipsychotic is recommended in patients who are displaying psychotic features." [verified] — [Wilson 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6866)
- If haloperidol is used, "consider administering it with a benzodiazepine to reduce extrapyramidal side effects" (EPS 20% haloperidol alone vs 6% with lorazepam in the cited study). [verified as quoted by BETA] — [Wilson 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6866)
- SUPERSEDED items in BETA 2012: (a) "it is prudent for clinicians to avoid using droperidol for agitation"; (b) olanzapine plus benzodiazepines "should be avoided"; (c) aripiprazole, quetiapine, clozapine not first line (quetiapine: orthostatic hypotension in the ED). Items (a) and (b) conflict with the ACEP 2024 Level B recommendation. [verified text; supersession is my reading] — [Wilson 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6866); [ACEP Now](https://acepnow.com/article/aceps-new-clinical-policy-on-severe-agitation)
- BETA predates ketamine use for agitation: a 2020 review notes ketamine was "not included in the Project BETA guidelines". [verified: abstract] — [Miller CWT, Hodzic V, Weintraub E. West J Emerg Med. 2020;21(4):841-848. PMID 32726254](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2020.4.45779)

**Restraint and seclusion (Knox 2012)**
- Algorithm in prose: unless actively violent, verbal de-escalation first; offer medication; "If the patient is an immediate danger to others, restraint is indicated. If the patient is not a danger to others, seclusion should be considered"; restraint if the patient would be a danger to self in seclusion; "medication should be administered to calm a patient who has been placed in restraints"; monitor all restrained or secluded patients; minimize time. [verified] — [Knox 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6867)
- Restraint framed as "treatment failures", not treatment (Pennsylvania state hospital program quote). Staff need at least annual training in de-escalation and management of aggressive behavior; debrief staff and patient. [verified] — [Knox 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6867)

**Updates since 2012**
- Roppolo 2020 summarizes BETA principles as "verbal de-escalation as a first-line treatment for agitation; pharmacotherapy that treats the most likely etiology of the agitation; appropriate psychiatric evaluation; appropriate treatment of associated medical conditions; and minimization of physical restraint/seclusion." [verified] — [Roppolo 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/emp2.12138)
- Roppolo 2020 restraint technique: "five trained individuals", one per extremity and one at the head; then supine with head of bed elevated, restraints tied to the bed frame and not the side rails, "one arm upward and the other downward"; "Medications are necessary whenever physical restraint of the patient is required". [verified] — [Roppolo 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/emp2.12138)
- A correction to Roppolo 2020 exists (2023); I did not read what was corrected. [verified: existence only] — [Correction, PMID 37360224](https://doi.org/10.1002/emp2.13002)

### Inferences
- A workable stepwise approach supported by these sources: (1) scene safety and enough staff; (2) rapid red-flag screen with vitals, glucose, oxygen saturation; (3) verbal and environmental de-escalation; (4) offer oral medication matched to likely cause; (5) parenteral medication if needed (ACEP 2024 for agent choice); (6) restraint or seclusion only as last resort, with medication, monitoring and earliest release; (7) full work-up once calm; (8) debrief.
- BETA recommendations are consensus statements with no grades; the handout should label them "consensus (ungraded)".

- No adult "BETA 2.0" or AAEP revision found in PubMed through Nov 2026; I did not search the AAEP website directly.
- BETA dosing table and algorithm figures are images and were not read.

## 3. ACEP 2021 Task Force report on "hyperactive delirium with severe agitation"; why the term changed; AMA/ACEP positions 2021 to 2023

### Takeaway
ACEP replaced "excited delirium" with the descriptive term "hyperactive delirium with severe agitation" in a task force report approved 23 June 2021, and in October 2023 withdrew approval of its 2009 white paper. AMA (June 2021), APA, ACMT (2023) and NAME (2023) all rejected "excited delirium" as a diagnosis or cause of death, mainly because it lacks diagnostic criteria and has been used to justify force and sedation in police encounters.

### Cited Findings
- Report title: "ACEP Task Force Report on Hyperactive Delirium with Severe Agitation in Emergency Settings", approved by the ACEP Board 23 June 2021; it states the condition "is a life-threatening constellation of signs and symptoms with numerous causes". [secondary: search-result digest of the ACEP PDF; PDF returned 403] — [ACEP Task Force Report PDF](https://www.acep.org/siteassets/new-pdfs/education/acep-task-force-report-on-hyperactive-delirium-final.pdf)
- Definition attributed to the 2021 report: hyperactive delirium "describes a condition of altered mental status distinguished by disordered thinking and psychomotor agitation, often accompanied by a hyperadrenergic state." [secondary: University of Texas System Police Training Bulletin 035, 11 Apr 2024, citing ACEP 2021] — [UT System TB035](https://www.utsystem.edu/sites/default/files/offices/police/policies/TB035HyperactiveDeliriumwith-SevereAgitation041124.pdf)
- ACEP 12 Oct 2023 statement ("ACEP Reaffirms Positions on Hyperactive Delirium"): ACEP "does not recognize the use of the term 'excited delirium' and its use in clinical settings"; it withdrew approval of the 2009 White Paper Report on Excited Delirium Syndrome; it recognizes hyperactive delirium syndrome with severe agitation as "a potentially life-threatening clinical condition characterized by a combination of vital sign abnormalities (e.g., elevated temperature and blood pressure), pronounced agitation, altered mental status, and metabolic derangements." [secondary: UT bulletin quoting ACEP 2023; ACEP page returned 403] — [UT System TB035](https://www.utsystem.edu/sites/default/files/offices/police/policies/TB035HyperactiveDeliriumwith-SevereAgitation041124.pdf); [ACEP page](https://www.acep.org/news/acep-newsroom-articles/aceps-position-on-hyperactive-delirium)
- 2009 ACEP white paper had concluded excited delirium syndrome "is a real syndrome of uncertain etiology." [secondary: quoted in ACMT statement] — [Stolbach AI et al. J Med Toxicol. 2023;19(3):310-312. PMC10293509](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10293509)
- AMA policy, press release 14 June 2021: current evidence does not support "excited delirium" as an official diagnosis; denounces "'excited delirium' as a sole justification for law enforcement use of excessive force"; underscores "the importance of emergency physician-led oversight of medical emergencies in the field"; opposes sedative/hypnotic and dissociative drugs "including ketamine" used on agitated persons in out-of-hospital settings solely for a law enforcement purpose. [verified: AMA press release read via fetch tool] — [AMA press release](https://www.ama-assn.org/press-center/ama-press-releases/new-ama-policy-opposes-excited-delirium-diagnosis)
- ACMT position (published 22 June 2023): "Because the term 'excited delirium' is used in multiple distinct contexts with various underlying causes and has become associated with racism, it is time to discontinue the use of this term. The term 'hyperactive delirium with agitation' more accurately describes the undifferentiated presentation of patients with disorientation who appear aggressive or have vital signs suggestive of excessive adrenergic activity. When possible, the underlying cause of death should be listed on the death certificate." [verified: PMC full text via fetch tool; quote reassembled from fragments] — [Stolbach AI, Dargan PI, Greller HA, Hamilton RJ, Johnson-Arbor K, Murray BP, Ovakim D, Tormoehlen L, Nelson LS, et al. J Med Toxicol. 2023;19(3):310-312](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13181-023-00944-4)
- NAME ("Excited Delirium Statement 3/2023") and the APA position statement are cited by ACMT as also abandoning the term as a diagnosis/cause of death. [secondary] — [ACMT statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10293509)
- Clinical weight of delirium features: in Miner 2018, delirium characteristics were present in 23% of agitated ED patients, and clinical events requiring intervention occurred in 26% with delirium symptoms vs 13% without (OR 1.6, 95% CI 1.2 to 2.4). [verified: abstract] — [Miner 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2018.06.001)
- Prehospital consensus 2024 (NAEMSP, IAFC, IACP): framework for coordinated law enforcement and EMS response to acute behavioral emergencies with severe agitation. [verified: abstract] — [Levy MK, Tan DK, McArdle DQ, McEvoy M, Kupas DF, Beltran G, Miller DL. Prehosp Emerg Care. 2024;28(8):1058-1062. PMID 39264840](https://doi.org/10.1080/10903127.2024.2402530)
- UK equivalent term is "acute behavioural disturbance" (ABD); RCEM states ABD "should not be considered a diagnosis or syndrome, but rather a clinical picture", and that excited delirium is "a contested diagnosis, which is not recognised in the UK". [verified: RCEM PDF text] — [RCEM Best Practice Guideline, ABD in EDs, Version 4, April 2025, minor edits April 2026](https://rcem.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Acute-Behavioural-Disturbance-in-Emergency-Departments-April-2026.pdf)

### Inferences
- For the handout: use "hyperactive delirium with severe agitation" as a descriptive presentation (not a diagnosis or cause of death), stress hyperthermia, acidosis and exertion under restraint as the lethal pathway, and treat it as a resuscitation problem requiring rapid sedation and cooling.

- The ACEP 2021 report itself was not opened, so its recommended approach (agent choices, doses, monitoring, cooling) is not quoted here. Author list and specific treatment statements are not confirmed.
- Exact AMA policy number/title in PolicyFinder not retrieved.
- State laws restricting the term (for example California) were not researched.

## 4. Agitation scales used in the ER, cut-offs, and which scale each key trial used

### Takeaway
ER agitation trials mostly use bedside observational scales anchored at 0 = calm: AMSS (Hennepin trials), SAT (Australian and UK practice), RASS (ketamine trials), and a 6-level sedation scale (Hong Kong/Melbourne trials). BARS is the scale Project BETA favors for triage; PANSS-EC is a psychiatric research scale used in drug-registration trials and is impractical in the undifferentiated ER patient.

### Cited Findings
- SAT (Sedation Assessment Tool), 7 points from +3 to -3, as reproduced by RCEM: +3 "Combative, violent, out of control / Continual loud outbursts"; +2 "Very anxious and agitated / Loud outbursts"; +1 "Anxious/restless / Normal/talkative"; 0 "Awake and calm/cooperative / Speaks normally"; -1 "Asleep but rouses if name is called / Slurring or prominent slowing"; -2 "Responds to physical stimulation / Few recognizable words"; -3 "No response to stimulation / None". Source reference given: Calver L, Stokes B, Isbister G. Emerg Med Australas. 2011;23:732-740. [verified in RCEM appendix; Calver paper not opened] — [RCEM 2026](https://rcem.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Acute-Behavioural-Disturbance-in-Emergency-Departments-April-2026.pdf)
- SAT cut-off in guidance: RCEM reserves ketamine or droperidol for "severe agitation (a Sedation Assessment Tool score of 2 or 3)". [verified] — [RCEM 2026](https://rcem.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Acute-Behavioural-Disturbance-in-Emergency-Departments-April-2026.pdf)
- AMSS (Altered Mental Status Scale): "an ordinal agitation scale from -4 (coma) to 0 (normal) to +4 (most agitated)"; agitation defined as +1 or higher. [verified: abstract] — [Boes SA, Cole JB, Puskarich MA, Miner JR, Knack SKS, Prekker ME, Driver BE. Acad Emerg Med. 2025;32(10):1054-1064. PMID 40272385](https://doi.org/10.1111/acem.70045)
- AMSS in Miner 2018 prevalence study: agitation = "altered mental status score greater than 1"; followed every 5 minutes until score less than 1. [verified: abstract] — [Miner 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2018.06.001)
- AMSS, Klein 2018 (IM midazolam vs olanzapine vs ziprasidone vs haloperidol): primary outcome proportion adequately sedated at 15 minutes, "Altered Mental Status Scale <1". [verified: abstract] — [Klein LR, Driver BE, Miner JR, Martel ML, et al. Ann Emerg Med. 2018;72(4):374-385. PMID 29885904](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2018.04.027)
- AMSS, Martel 2021 (droperidol vs ziprasidone vs lorazepam RCT): primary outcome "proportion of patients adequately sedated (AMSS ≤ 0) at 15 minutes": droperidol 16/25 (64%), ziprasidone 10 mg 7/28 (25%), ziprasidone 20 mg 11/31 (35%), lorazepam 9/31 (29%). [verified: abstract] — [Martel ML, Driver BE, Miner JR, Biros MH, et al. Acad Emerg Med. 2021;28(4):421-434. PMID 32888340](https://doi.org/10.1111/acem.14124)
- AMSS, Cole 2021 (IM droperidol vs olanzapine): time to AMSS ≤ 0, 16 vs 17.5 minutes. [verified: abstract] — [Cole JB, Stang JL, DeVries PA, Martel ML, et al. Ann Emerg Med. 2021;78(2):274-286. PMID 33846015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2021.01.005)
- AMSS, Cole 2016 (prehospital ketamine vs haloperidol): paramedics trained in AMSS; time to adequate sedation 5 vs 17 minutes. [verified: abstract] — [Cole JB, Moore JC, Nystrom PC, Orozco BS, et al. Clin Toxicol (Phila). 2016;54(7):556-562. PMID 27102743](https://doi.org/10.1080/15563650.2016.1177652)
- RASS, Barbic 2021 (IM ketamine vs midazolam plus haloperidol): inclusion "Richmond Agitation Score (RASS) ≥+3"; adequate sedation "RASS ≤-1"; median 5.8 vs 14.7 minutes. [verified: abstract] — [Barbic D, Andolfatto G, Grunau B, Scheuermeyer FX, et al. Ann Emerg Med. 2021;78(6):788-795. PMID 34353650](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2021.05.023)
- Six-level sedation scale, Chan 2021 (IM midazolam vs olanzapine vs haloperidol, Hong Kong): "Sedation levels were measured on a 6-level validated scale". [verified: abstract] — [Chan EW, Lao KSJ, Lam L, Tsui SH, et al. EClinicalMedicine. 2021;32:100751. PMID 33681744](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2021.100751)
- Taylor 2017 (IV midazolam-droperidol vs droperidol vs olanzapine): primary outcome proportion adequately sedated at 10 minutes (difference 25.0% and 25.4% favoring the combination). The abstract does not name the scale. [verified: abstract] — [Taylor DM, Yap CYL, Knott JC, Taylor SE, et al. Ann Emerg Med. 2017;69(3):318-326.e1. PMID 27745766](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2016.07.033)
- Chan 2013 (IV droperidol or olanzapine adjunct to midazolam): primary outcome time to sedation; scale not named in abstract. [verified: abstract] — [Chan EW, Taylor DM, Knott JC, Phillips GA, et al. Ann Emerg Med. 2013;61(1):72-81. PMID 22981685](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2012.07.118)
- Lin 2021 (ketamine vs haloperidol plus lorazepam): primary outcome sedation within 5 minutes; scale not named in abstract. [verified: abstract] — [Lin J, Figuerado Y, Montgomery A, Lee J, et al. Am J Emerg Med. 2021;44:306-311. PMID 32340820](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajem.2020.04.013)
- BARS (Behavioural Activity Rating Scale): AAEP "does not consider one agitation rating scale to be better than another, although we find the BARS is easy to use reliably"; it "does not require the participant/patient to answer questions". BETA triage rules: BARS 1 = immediate transfer to a medical ED; BARS 2, 3 or 7 = immediate transfer to ED or psychiatric emergency service with medical capability; BARS 5 or 6 = "mild to moderate agitation", try de-escalation; 4 = normal. [verified] — [Nordstrom 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6863); [Richmond 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6864)
- BARS anchors used in the Parkland order set: "BARS 5 = mild agitation (eg, pacing, restless, intrusive behavior, easily annoyed or angered, confused, physically distressed but is redirectable)"; "BARS 6 = moderate agitation (eg, signs of aggression but no violent, continuously pacing/restless, confused/unable to cooperate, needs continuous redirection)"; "BARS 7 = severe agitation (eg, striking staff or other patients, harming or repeatedly threatening to harm self or others, violence, destroying property, not redirectable and not responding to de-escalation". [verified] — [Roppolo 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/emp2.12138)
- BARS original 7-point wording: 1 difficult or unable to rouse; 2 asleep but responds normally to verbal or physical contact; 3 drowsy, appears sedated; 4 quiet and awake (normal level of activity); 5 signs of overt (physical or verbal) activity, calms down with instructions; 6 extremely or continuously active, not requiring restraint; 7 violent, requires restraint. Source: Swift RH et al. J Psychiatr Res. 2002;36:87-95. [recalled] — not opened; consistent with the BETA text above.
- RASS: 10 points, +4 combative, +3 very agitated, +2 agitated, +1 restless, 0 alert and calm, -1 drowsy to -5 unarousable. Source: Sessler CN et al. Am J Respir Crit Care Med. 2002;166:1338-1344. Built for the ICU. [recalled] — not opened.
- SAS (Riker Sedation-Agitation Scale): 1 to 7; 4 calm and cooperative, 5 agitated, 6 very agitated, 7 dangerous agitation. Source: Riker RR et al. Crit Care Med. 1999;27:1325-1329. ICU scale. [recalled] — not opened.
- PANSS-EC (PEC): five items (excitement, tension, hostility, uncooperativeness, poor impulse control), each 1 to 7, total 5 to 35; registration trials typically required a total of 14 or more with at least one item of 4 or more, and defined response as a 40% or greater fall. Used in IM olanzapine, ziprasidone, aripiprazole, inhaled loxapine and sublingual dexmedetomidine trials. Needs an interviewable patient. [recalled] — not opened.
- "BAM": I found no ER agitation scale by that abbreviation; the request appears to mean BARS (Behavioural Activity Rating Scale). [verified: absence in sources read]
- Uptake: a 2025 analysis reported US statewide EMS protocols recommending a standardized agitation score rose from 10% in 2018 to 29% in 2025. [secondary: search digest] — [PMC13286584](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13286584/)

### Inferences
- Scale thresholds differ, so "adequate sedation" is not interchangeable across trials: AMSS ≤ 0 (awake and calm counts) vs RASS ≤ -1 (drowsy required) vs SAT fall of 2 points or score ≤ 0 (DORM-style definitions, recalled).
- For bedside use a 0-anchored observational scale (AMSS, SAT or RASS) fits the ER; BARS suits triage/non-medical staff.

### Gaps
- Original validation papers for RASS, SAS, BARS, PANSS-EC, AMSS (Martel 2005) and SAT (Calver 2011) were not opened; descriptors for all but SAT and the AMSS range are from memory.
- Scales for Taylor 2017, Chan 2013, Lin 2021 and the DORM trials (Isbister 2010; Calver 2015) not confirmed from full text.

## 5. Red flags that agitation is medical, not psychiatric

### Takeaway
Project BETA is the citable source: treat new-onset or undifferentiated agitation as medical until proven otherwise, and look for abnormal vitals, impaired attention/awareness, intoxication or withdrawal, toxin exposure, head injury, neurologic disease, and onset after age 45 without psychiatric history.

### Cited Findings
- "New onset of agitation in a person older than 45 years with no psychiatric history would suggest a medical etiology, since most psychiatric disorders have an earlier onset." [verified] — [Nordstrom 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6863)
- "Abnormal vital signs, abnormal physical examination results, overt signs of alcohol or drug intoxication or withdrawal, evidence of exposure to toxins, and decreased awareness with attentional problems are all indicative of a medical etiology." Also head injury or history of stroke, cancer, parkinsonism, multiple sclerosis. [verified] — [Nordstrom 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6863)
- Known psychiatric disease presenting differently from prior episodes should raise suspicion of a medical cause. [verified] — [Nordstrom 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6863)
- Medical causes listed in text: head trauma; meningitis, encephalitis, sepsis; renal or hepatic encephalopathy; hypoxia; metabolic derangement including hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia ("easily reversible"); thyrotoxicosis; postictal state; toxic levels of psychiatric or antiseizure drugs; environmental toxins; neuroleptic malignant syndrome ("lead pipe" rigidity) vs serotonin syndrome (myoclonus, hyperreflexia); alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal ("the most medically concerning" withdrawal syndromes). [verified] — [Nordstrom 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6863)
- Delirium clue: fluctuating awareness and attention; "visual hallucinations are a common symptom in delirium, especially in elderly patients"; delirium "indicates an underlying medical etiology." [verified] — [Nordstrom 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6863); [Wilson 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6866)
- Roppolo 2020 "red flags (eg, abnormal vital signs, trauma, and abnormal neurologic exam)". [verified] — [Roppolo 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/emp2.12138)
- Epidemiologic support: among physically restrained ED adults, chief complaints were 45.4% medical, 33.7% alcohol or drug use, 12.3% psychiatric, 8.5% trauma. [verified: abstract] — [Wong AH, Taylor RA, Ray JM, Bernstein SL. Ann Emerg Med. 2019;73(2):183-192. PMID 30119940](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2018.06.020)
- Non-drug or non-alcohol causes carried higher odds of a clinical event needing intervention (nondrug cause OR 3.5, 95% CI 2.3 to 5.6; drug other than alcohol OR 1.7, 95% CI 1.1 to 2.9). [verified: abstract] — [Miner 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2018.06.001)
- RCEM investigations for severe cases include blood gas with glucose, ECG, temperature, and correction of electrolyte, glucose and acid-base abnormalities. [verified: RCEM text, partial] — [RCEM 2026](https://rcem.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Acute-Behavioural-Disturbance-in-Emergency-Departments-April-2026.pdf)

### Inferences
- Minimum bedside screen for every agitated ER patient that the sources support: full vital signs including temperature, pulse oximetry, finger-stick glucose, brief attention/orientation check, signs of trauma, toxidrome exam, medication and substance history from collateral sources.

### Gaps
- BETA Tables 2 to 4 (the formal red-flag lists) are images; the list above is from the running text, so the exact table wording is not quoted.
- I did not confirm the commonly taught mnemonic lists (for example FIND ME) against a primary source.

## 6. Restraint and seclusion: CMS 42 CFR 482.13(e), Joint Commission, harms data, ACEP policy statement

### Takeaway
The CMS time limits requested are correct as stated and unchanged in the eCFR as of 1 Jan 2026: orders for violent or self-destructive behavior are limited to 4 h (adults), 2 h (ages 9 to 17), 1 h (under 9), renewable to 24 h, with a face-to-face evaluation within 1 hour; PRN and standing orders are prohibited. A medication counts as a restraint only when it "is not a standard treatment or dosage for the patient's condition."

### Cited Findings
**CMS Conditions of Participation, 42 CFR 482.13 (all verbatim from eCFR, version 2026-01-01)** — [eCFR 482.13](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-482.13)
- (e): "Restraint or seclusion may only be imposed to ensure the immediate physical safety of the patient, a staff member, or others and must be discontinued at the earliest possible time." Patients have the right to be free from restraint or seclusion "imposed as a means of coercion, discipline, convenience, or retaliation by staff." [verified]
- (e)(1)(i)(B) drug as restraint: "A drug or medication when it is used as a restriction to manage the patient's behavior or restrict the patient's freedom of movement and is not a standard treatment or dosage for the patient's condition." [verified]
- (e)(1)(ii): "Seclusion is the involuntary confinement of a patient alone in a room or area from which the patient is physically prevented from leaving. Seclusion may only be used for the management of violent or self-destructive behavior." [verified]
- (e)(2)-(3): only when less restrictive interventions are ineffective; must be "the least restrictive intervention that will be effective". [verified]
- (e)(5)-(7): requires an order from "a physician or other licensed practitioner"; orders "must never be written as a standing order or on an as needed basis (PRN)"; attending physician consulted as soon as possible if not the orderer. [verified]
- (e)(8)(i): each order for violent or self-destructive behavior "may only be renewed in accordance with the following limits for up to a total of 24 hours: (A) 4 hours for adults 18 years of age or older; (B) 2 hours for children and adolescents 9 to 17 years of age; or (C) 1 hour for children under 9 years of age". (e)(8)(ii): after 24 hours the practitioner "must see and assess the patient" before a new order. Preamble: "Unless superseded by State law that is more restrictive". [verified]
- (e)(8)(iii): non-violent restraint orders "may be renewed as authorized by hospital policy." [verified]
- (e)(9): "must be discontinued at the earliest possible time, regardless of the length of time identified in the order." [verified]
- (e)(10): monitoring by physician, other licensed practitioner or trained staff "at an interval determined by hospital policy." (CMS sets no fixed interval such as every 15 minutes.) [verified]
- (e)(12): "the patient must be seen face-to-face within 1 hour after the initiation of the intervention" by a physician, other licensed practitioner, or trained registered nurse, to evaluate "(A) The patient's immediate situation; (B) The patient's reaction to the intervention; (C) The patient's medical and behavioral condition; and (D) The need to continue or terminate the restraint or seclusion." (e)(14): if done by a trained RN, consult the attending or responsible practitioner as soon as possible afterward. [verified]
- (e)(15): simultaneous restraint and seclusion only if "continually monitored" face-to-face by assigned trained staff or by trained staff using "both video and audio equipment" in close proximity. [verified]
- (e)(16) documentation: the 1-hour evaluation; behavior and intervention; alternatives or less restrictive interventions attempted; the condition or symptoms that warranted use; response and rationale for continued use. [verified]
- (f) training content includes nonphysical intervention skills, least restrictive choice, recognizing distress "(for example, positional asphyxia)", monitoring "respiratory and circulatory status, skin integrity, vital signs", and first aid/CPR certification. [verified]
- (g) death reporting: report to CMS "no later than the close of business on the next business day" each death in restraint or seclusion, within 24 hours after removal, or within 1 week where restraint or seclusion reasonably contributed. Deaths with only soft two-wrist restraints and no seclusion go in an internal log within seven days. [verified]

**Joint Commission**
- BETA (2012) states the CMS conditions "have been endorsed by The Joint Commission (TJC)". [verified] — [Knox 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6867)
- Joint Commission removed the term "licensed independent practitioner" from hospital restraint and seclusion standards effective 15 March 2020. [secondary: search digest of AAPA news] — [AAPA](https://aapa.org/news-central/2020/03/joint-commission-removes-licensed-independent-practitioner-term-from-restraint-and-seclusion-standards)
- New and revised restraint and seclusion requirements effective 1 Jan 2025 apply to behavioral health care and human services organizations (R3 Report Issue 44), not to hospitals. [secondary: search digest] — [Joint Commission R3 Report 44](https://jointcommission.org/standards/r3-report/r3-report-issue-44-new-and-revised-restraint-and-seclusion-requirements-for-behavioral-health-care)
- Hospital standards historically sat at PC.03.05.01 through PC.03.05.19 for deemed-status hospitals, and the hospital manual was restructured for 2026. [recalled] — numbering and current text not confirmed; see Gaps.

**ACEP policy statement "Use of Patient Restraints"**
- Version found: approved/revised February 2020 (earlier revisions 2014, 2001, 2000). Text reported: "The method of restraint should be the least restrictive necessary for the protection of the patient and others." "When appropriate and safe, verbal de-escalation and standard treatment of underlying medical or psychiatric conditions should be attempted before restraints." Staff "should be properly trained in de-escalation, trauma informed care, the appropriate use and application of restraints and in the monitoring of patients in restraint and seclusion"; "the use of a specific medication does not by itself constitute a restraint." [secondary: search-result digest of the ACEP PDF; PDF returned 403] — [ACEP policy statement](https://www.acep.org/siteassets/new-pdfs/policy-statements/use-of-patient-restraints.pdf)

**Harms and usage data**
- Restraint orders in 0.5% of adult ED visits (4,661 of 956,153; 5 EDs, 2013 to 2015); median age 47; 66.7% men; 91.1% arrived by ambulance. [verified: abstract] — [Wong 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2018.06.020)
- Among agitated ED patients at one urban county ED, 84% (969/1,146) were physically restrained and 72% (829/1,146) received IM sedation. [verified: abstract] — [Miner 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2018.06.001)
- Restrained patients are more likely to be admitted to psychiatric hospital and have longer stays (BETA, citing earlier studies). [verified as stated by BETA] — [Richmond 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6864)
- Seclusion used in 25.6% of EDs; in another ED survey 30% used physical restraint alone and 30% restraint plus drugs; prehospital restraint involved assault on EMS personnel in 28%. [verified as stated by BETA; underlying surveys not opened] — [Knox 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6867)
- Restraints "carry an elevated risk of injury to patients and staff, are experienced as coercion or aggression, and can lead to psychological trauma"; "most physical assaults occur during the containment process". [verified] — [Roppolo 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/emp2.12138)
- Equity: unhoused adults had restraint orders in 5.7% of visits vs 1.5% for housed patients (adjusted OR 1.45, 95% CI 1.36 to 1.54; 8 hospitals, 2013 to 2021). [verified: abstract] — [Robinson L, Ryus CR, Nath B, Kumar A, Desai R, Shah D, Faustino IV, Wong AH. PLoS One. 2025;20(3):e0319286. PMID 40080507](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0319286)
- Fatal cases: Italian series of 7 deaths with hyperactive delirium with severe agitation; 5 occurred immediately after manual restraint, psychostimulants in 5. [verified: abstract] — [Margari A, Tavone A, Cardinale AN, et al. Leg Med (Tokyo). 2026;82:102823. PMID 41759402](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.legalmed.2026.102823)
- RCEM: "continued exertion under restraint can contribute to poor outcomes (likely due to increasing catecholamine levels, worsening hyperthermia, and metabolic acidosis)"; "Prolonged restraint should prompt consideration of rapid tranquilisation"; "Do not attempt to restrain patients with insufficient numbers of staff"; assign a safety person to monitor the patient. [verified] — [RCEM 2026](https://rcem.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Acute-Behavioural-Disturbance-in-Emergency-Departments-April-2026.pdf)
- Zun LS, prospective ED restraint complication study (J Emerg Med. 2003;24:119-124), complication rate about 7%. [recalled] — not opened; BETA cites Zun only for restraint being used more in EDs than inpatient units.

### Inferences
- Pharmacist-relevant point: a sedative given at a standard dose to treat the patient's agitation-causing condition is treatment, not "chemical restraint", under the CMS definition; the same drug given purely to restrict movement is a restraint and triggers the (e) requirements. Hospital policy decides how this is documented.
- CMS does not set a monitoring frequency; any "every 15 minutes" rule is hospital policy or state law.

### Gaps
- Joint Commission hospital standard numbers and current wording for 2026 were not verified; check the current hospital accreditation manual.
- ACEP restraint policy statement not opened; I could not confirm whether it was revised or reaffirmed after February 2020.
- No pooled incidence of restraint-related injury or death in US EDs was found; the 7% figure is from memory.
- State laws stricter than CMS were not researched.

## 7. Epidemiology: share of ED visits with agitation; staff injury and workplace violence

### Takeaway
The best-sourced prevalence is 2.6% of ED visits at one urban county ED (Miner 2018); it is a single-center figure, not a national estimate. Violence toward staff is common and under-reported, with assault in about one in five agitated-patient encounters in the same system.

### Cited Findings
- Prevalence 2.6% (1,146 of 43,838 screened patients), prospective, urban Level I trauma center; delirium characteristics in 0.6% of all patients (260/43,838), 23% of agitated patients. Sedatives used: olanzapine 39%, droperidol 20%, haloperidol 20%, benzodiazepine 6%, ketamine 5%. [verified: abstract] — [Miner JR, Klein LR, Cole JB, Driver BE, Moore JC, Ho JD. Ann Emerg Med. 2018;72(4):361-370. PMID 30031556](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annemergmed.2018.06.001)
- In 4,609 agitated encounters (AMSS +1 or higher) in an ED intoxication unit: assault (threat or violent act) in 20.3% (95% CI 19.1% to 21.5%); violent act in 7.9%; threat in 17.4%; verbal abuse in 38.8%. Formal reporting: 0.5% of verbal abuse and 61.9% of violent acts. Intoxication present in 89.1%. [verified: abstract] — [Boes 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/acem.70045)
- ACEP 2018 poll of more than 3,500 emergency physicians: "nearly half had been physically assaulted while at work, with 60% occurring within the past year." A 2016 survey of 119 EM residents: 66% physically assaulted by patients; 16.8% had prior violence-prevention training. [verified as stated in Roppolo; polls not opened] — [Roppolo 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/emp2.12138)
- 2010 Emergency Nurses Association study: more than half of emergency nurses verbally or physically threatened at work in the preceding 7 days; National ED Safety Study: at least 25% of ED staff felt safe "sometimes," "rarely," or "never." [verified as stated in BETA; originals not opened] — [Wilson 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6866)
- Survey of 242 ED workers at 5 hospitals: about 48% had been physically assaulted. [verified as stated in BETA] — [Knox 2012](https://doi.org/10.5811/westjem.2011.9.6867)
- After BETA-based order set and training at Parkland (ED volume over 240,000 visits/year): pre-implementation survey, 28% of 50 responding residents assaulted; 18 months after, assaults on 5 of 66 residents, one in the last 12 months. Uncontrolled before/after quality-improvement data. [verified] — [Roppolo 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/emp2.12138)

### Inferences
- The widely repeated "1.7 million agitated ED visits per year" style figure was not found in any source I opened; do not print a national count without a source.

### Gaps
- No nationally representative US estimate of agitation prevalence in ED visits was found.
- ACEP 2022 workplace violence poll and Bureau of Labor Statistics injury data were not retrieved.

## 8. 2024 to 2026 guidelines or consensus documents on ED agitation worth citing; pediatric note

### Takeaway
Since the ACEP 2024 policy, I found no new US adult ED agitation guideline from ACEP, AAEP or APA. The notable recent documents are the UK RCEM guideline (Version 4, 2025/2026), the 2024 NAEMSP/IAFC/IACP prehospital consensus, and a pending NICE update; the 2019 AAEP pediatric BETA remains the pediatric reference.

### Cited Findings
- RCEM Best Practice Guideline, Acute Behavioural Disturbance in Emergency Departments, Version 4 (dated April 2025, minor edits April 2026). Summary recommendations include: "Early sedation should be considered in severe cases of ABD, including where prolonged activity or restraint has taken place" and "In most cases of ABD requiring parenteral sedation, intramuscular ketamine or droperidol are recommended agents." Suggested doses: "Ketamine 4mg/kg IM (or titrate to effect IV) OR Droperidol 5-10mg IM"; if unavailable, "midazolam (5-10mg IM), lorazepam (4mg IM) or haloperidol (5mg IM +/- 2mg lorazepam IM) could be considered." Consensus-based, UK practice; not a US standard. [verified] — [RCEM 2026](https://rcem.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Acute-Behavioural-Disturbance-in-Emergency-Departments-April-2026.pdf)
- RCEM uses the BETA de-escalation domains and offers oral sedatives "as part of a verbal and environmental de-escalation strategy", calling this "an important step in establishing that you are using the least restrictive practice". [verified] — [RCEM 2026](https://rcem.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Acute-Behavioural-Disturbance-in-Emergency-Departments-April-2026.pdf)
- NICE is reviewing its violence and aggression guidance (in-development reference GID-NG10432; current guideline NG10); RCEM says it will update once NICE publishes. Status as of Nov 2026 not confirmed. [verified: as stated in RCEM document] — [RCEM 2026](https://rcem.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Acute-Behavioural-Disturbance-in-Emergency-Departments-April-2026.pdf)
- NAEMSP/IAFC/IACP consensus statement on law enforcement and EMS collaboration in acute behavioral emergencies (2024). [verified: abstract] — [Levy 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/10903127.2024.2402530)
- Older adults: a 2026 analysis found only 11 of 34 statewide EMS protocols addressed agitated older adults. [secondary: search digest] — [Lachs et al. J Am Geriatr Soc. 2026](https://agsjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jgs.70466?af=R)
- SCCM 2025 guideline on pain, anxiety, agitation/sedation, delirium and sleep in adult ICU patients exists but is ICU-focused. [secondary: search digest] — [Guideline Central](https://www.guidelinecentral.com/guideline/25064/)
- Pediatric BETA (AAEP with AACAP emergency child psychiatry committee; Delphi, 17 experts): "there should be a multimodal approach to managing agitation in the ED, and that etiology of agitation should drive choice of treatment." It notes 6 to 10% of youth presenting for psychiatric care require restraint.
