## 1. The 2024 ADA/EASD/JBDS/AACE/DTS consensus report: diagnostic criteria for DKA, HHS and mixed DKA/HHS

### Takeaway
DKA now requires all three of "D" (glucose >=200 mg/dL [11.1 mmol/L] OR known diabetes regardless of glucose), "K" (ketones, preferably beta-hydroxybutyrate >=3.0 mmol/L) and "A" (metabolic acidosis); the glucose threshold dropped from >250 mg/dL in 2009, explicitly to capture euglycemic (often SGLT2-inhibitor) DKA. HHS requires all four of severe hyperglycemia, hyperosmolality, absence of significant ketonemia and absence of acidosis; patients who meet both sets of criteria are "mixed DKA/HHS".

### Cited Findings
- "The diagnosis of DKA should be based on the three criteria described in Fig. 2a. All three components must be present to make this diagnosis." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024 (Diabetologia PMC11343900 full text).
- "In this consensus report, we have defined hyperglycaemia as a diagnostic criterion for DKA from >13.9 mmol/l (250 mg/dl) to either a glucose value of ≥11.1 mmol/l (200 mg/dl) or a prior history of diabetes irrespective of the presenting glucose value. Hyperglycaemia and/or diabetes must be accompanied by two additional criteria—elevated ketones and metabolic acidosis—for the diagnosis of DKA to be established." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Euglycemic DKA definition: "Approximately 10% of patients with DKA present with euglycaemic DKA, which is defined as plasma glucose levels <11.1 mmol/l (200 mg/dl) in the presence of ketosis and metabolic acidosis criteria of DKA described in Fig. 2." Causes listed: "exogenous insulin injection, reduced food intake, pregnancy, or impaired gluconeogenesis due to alcohol use, liver failure and/or SGLT2 inhibitor therapy." "In recent years, the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in those with type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes has accounted for the majority of cases of euglycaemic DKA." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Rationale for the change: "In recognition of the wider range of glucose levels at presentation with DKA, the criteria for diagnosis of DKA have been changed to encompass a lower glucose value of >11.1 mmol/l (200 mg/dl) and a prior history of diabetes (irrespective of the glucose level)." (Note the internal inconsistency: ">11.1" here vs "≥11.1" elsewhere and in Table 2.) [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Severity table "D" row (Table 2) lists the D criterion as "Glucose ≥11.1 mmol/l (200 mg/dl)" for mild, moderate and severe, labelled "'D': history of diabetes or elevated glucose level". "K" row: "β-Hydroxybutyrate 3.0–6.0 mmol/l" (mild, moderate), "β-Hydroxybutyrate >6.0 mmol/l" (severe). [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024, Table 2.
- HHS: "HHS is a state of significant hyperglycaemia and hyperosmolality in the absence of severe ketonaemia and metabolic acidosis. The diagnosis of HHS should be based on the four criteria presented in Fig. 2b. All four components must be present to make the diagnosis." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- "Clinical overlap between DKA and HHS has been reported in more than one-third of people with hyperglycaemic crises. Although most people with HHS have an admission pH ≥7.30 and a bicarbonate level ≥18 mmol/l, mild ketonaemia may be present." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Mixed DKA/HHS operational definition (in the HHS insulin section): "If HHS is present with no ketosis or with mild or moderate ketonaemia (blood β-hydroxybutyrate ≥1.0 to <3.0 mmol/l or urine ketones <2+) and without acidosis (pH ≥7.3 and bicarbonate ≥18 mmol/l), then a fixed-rate intravenous insulin infusion should be started at 0.05 U kg−1 h−1. If significant ketonaemia is present (i.e., β-hydroxybutyrate ≥3.0 mmol/l, ketonuria ≥2+, pH <7.30 or bicarbonate <18 mmol/l), which represents mixed DKA/HHS, then a fixed-rate intravenous insulin infusion should be started at 0.1 U kg−1 h−1." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- The numeric HHS criteria (glucose, osmolality formula, ketone and acid-base cut-offs) and the exact "K" and "A" diagnostic thresholds are in Fig. 2 of the consensus, which is an image in both PMC copies (legend only: "The diagnosis criteria of (a) DKA and (b) HHS"). See later bullets for confirmation from secondary reproductions. [verified that the values are figure-only]

- **DKA (all three required):** [verified, via Table 16.1]
  - Hyperglycemia ("D"): "Glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or prior history of diabetes"
  - Ketosis ("K"): "β-Hydroxybutyrate concentration ≥3.0 mmol/L or urine ketone strip 2+ or greater"
  - Acidosis ("A"): "pH <7.3 and/or bicarbonate concentration <18 mmol/L"
- **HHS (all four required):** [verified, via Table 16.1]
  - Hyperglycemia: "Plasma glucose ≥600 mg/dL (33.3 mmol/L)"
  - Hyperosmolarity: "Calculated effective serum osmolality >300 mOsm/kg (calculated as [2 × Na+ (mmol/L) + glucose (mmol/L)]" "or total serum osmolality >320 mOsm/kg [2 × Na+ (mmol/L) + glucose (mmol/L) + urea (mmol/L)]"
  - Absence of significant ketonemia: "β-Hydroxybutyrate concentration <3.0 mmol/L OR urine ketone strip less than 2+"
  - Absence of acidosis: "pH ≥7.3 and bicarbonate concentration ≥15 mmol/L"
- Unit conversion for US labs (not stated in the guideline; standard chemistry): glucose mg/dL ÷ 18 = mmol/L; BUN mg/dL ÷ 2.8 = urea mmol/L. So effective osmolality in US units = 2×Na + glucose/18; total = 2×Na + glucose/18 + BUN/2.8. [recalled — standard conversion factors, not quoted from the guideline]
- Note the HHS acidosis cut-off (bicarbonate ≥15 mmol/L) differs from the DKA acidosis cut-off (<18 mmol/L) and from the consensus HHS insulin-dosing text (which uses "bicarbonate ≥18 mmol/l" to define HHS without acidosis). A patient with bicarbonate 15-17.9 mmol/L, glucose ≥600 mg/dL, effective osmolality >300 and BHB <3.0 could meet the HHS criteria while also meeting the DKA "A" criterion (but not "K"). See Gaps. ## 2. Evidence grades: the 2024 consensus vs the ADA Standards of Care 2026 (Sections 6 and 16), and what is new in 2026

### Takeaway
The 2024 consensus report gives **no evidence grades**; it is explicitly "the panel's collective analysis, evaluation and expert opinion." Graded statements come from the ADA Standards of Care. In 2026, Section 6 was renamed to include "Hyperglycemic Crises" (Recs 6.21 [C] and 6.22 [B], prevention/education), and Section 16 (Recs 16.16 [A] management, 16.17 [B] discharge education) added Tables 16.1 and 16.2 carrying the consensus diagnostic criteria and clinical presentation. Neither section grades severity, ICU, or resolution criteria; those come only from the ungraded consensus.

### Cited Findings
- Consensus methodology: literature searches "in PubMed using medical subject headings to identify human studies published in English between 1 January 2009 and 1 June 2023 ... The guidance represents the panel's collective analysis, evaluation and expert opinion." No GRADE or ADA A/B/C/E letters appear anywhere in the text or tables. [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Consensus on its own evidence base: "To date, clinical recommendations for the management of DKA and HHS are largely based on consensus and opinion rather than rigorous outcomes research." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- **ADA SOC 2026 Rec 16.16 (grade A):** "Manage diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state (HHS) by administering intravenous fluids, insulin, and electrolytes by closely monitoring during treatment, ensuring timely and bridged transition to maintenance subcutaneous insulin administration, and identifying and treating the precipitating cause." [verified, via PMC HTML in fragments; wording reassembled] — ADA SOC 2026 Section 16 (doi:10.2337/dc26-S016).
- **ADA SOC 2026 Rec 16.17 (grade B):** "The discharge planning process should include education on the recognition, prevention, and management of DKA and/or HHS for all individuals affected by or at high risk for these events to prevent recurrence and readmission." [verified, fragments reassembled] — ADA SOC 2026 Section 16.
- **ADA SOC 2026 Rec 16.11 (grade A)** (SGLT2 inhibitors in hospital): "It is recommended that use of a sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor be initiated or continued during hospitalization if indicated for heart failure, providing there are no contraindications." [verified, fragments reassembled] — ADA SOC 2026 Section 16. ## 3. Severity grading, disposition (ED vs step-down vs ICU) and resolution criteria: 2024 consensus vs ADA 2009

### Takeaway
The 2024 consensus keeps a mild/moderate/severe DKA scale (now anchored on beta-hydroxybutyrate, pH, bicarbonate and mental status; anion gap dropped) and attaches a suggested level of care: mild = regular/observation unit, moderate = step-down/intermediate, severe = ICU. Severe DKA, any HHS, critical-illness precipitants, or altered mental status go to the ICU; uncomplicated mild-moderate DKA can stay in the ED/step-down, including with subcutaneous rapid-acting insulin. DKA is resolved at ketones <0.6 mmol/L plus venous pH ≥7.3 or bicarbonate ≥18 mmol/L (glucose <200 mg/dL "ideally"); anion gap and urine ketones are no longer resolution criteria. HHS resolution (no prior consensus definition) is osmolality <300 mOsm/kg, corrected hyperglycemia/glucose <250 mg/dL, urine output >0.5 mL/kg/h and improved cognition.

### Cited Findings
- **2024 Table 2, "DKA classification and suggested level of care by severity: mild, moderate or severe"** (verbatim cells) [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024:
  - "'D': history of diabetes or elevated glucose level": "Glucose ≥11.1 mmol/l (200 mg/dl)" in all three columns.
  - "'K': ketonaemia": Mild "β-Hydroxybutyrate 3.0–6.0 mmol/l"; Moderate "β-Hydroxybutyrate 3.0–6.0 mmol/l"; Severe "β-Hydroxybutyrate >6.0 mmol/l".
  - "'A': acidosis": Mild "pH >7.25 to <7.30 or bicarbonate 15–18 mmol/l"; Moderate "pH 7.0–7.25" and "Bicarbonate 10 to <15 mmol/l"; Severe "pH <7.0" and "Bicarbonate <10 mmol/l".
  - "Mental status": Mild "Alert"; Moderate "Alert/drowsy"; Severe "Stupor/coma".
  - "Suggested level of care": Mild "Regular or observation nursing unit"; Moderate "Step-down unit or intermediate care unit"; Severe "ICU".
  - Footnote: "Not all variables need to be fulfilled to be defined as either mild, moderate or severe, and the admission site and level of care are ultimately a clinical decision."
- Purpose of grading: "This categorisation may be clinically useful for guiding the location where an individual is assigned to receive care (e.g., emergency department, intensive care unit [ICU] or step-down unit) and for identifying patients with mild DKA who are candidates for subcutaneous insulin dosing rather than intravenous insulin infusion." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Disposition: "Most people with uncomplicated mild or moderate DKA can be treated in the emergency department or a step-down unit if close nursing supervision and monitoring are available ... ICU admission in people with mild DKA has also been associated with more laboratory testing and higher hospitalisation costs. In contrast, individuals with severe DKA or HHS, or those with critical illness as the precipitating cause (e.g., myocardial infarction, gastrointestinal bleeding, sepsis) or with altered mental status should be treated in the ICU, as outlined in Table 2." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Subcutaneous insulin in the ED: "Patients with uncomplicated mild or moderate DKA may be treated with subcutaneous rapid-acting insulin analogues ... every 1–2 h ... This treatment can be delivered in emergency departments and step-down units without the need for ICU care." "The use of rapid-acting subcutaneous insulin analogues is not recommended for the treatment of severe and complicated DKA or with HHS." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Initial ED work-up: "If DKA or HHS is suspected, initial samples should be taken for glucose, serum electrolytes, venous blood gases, complete blood count, and blood or urine ketone levels. Volume status can be assessed with vital sign parameters ... an electrocardiogram should be performed to assess for evidence of biochemically induced repolarisation abnormalities, such as peaked T waves from hyperkalaemia and ischaemia." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Monitoring cadence: "Capillary blood glucose testing should be performed during treatment every 1–2 h using a hospital-calibrated glucose meter, and blood should be drawn every 4 h for determination of electrolytes, phosphate, creatinine, β-hydroxybutyrate and venous pH until resolution of DKA. In patients with HHS, in addition to measuring glucose, creatinine and electrolytes, serum osmolarity should be measured every 4 h." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- **DKA resolution (2024):** "Resolution of DKA is defined as achieving plasma ketone <0.6 mmol/l and venous pH ≥7.3 or bicarbonate ≥18 mmol/l. Ideally, the blood glucose concentration should also be <11.1 mmol/l (200 mg/dl). The anion gap should not be used as a criterion, as it may be misleading because of the presence of hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis caused by large volumes of 0.9% sodium chloride solution. Because β-hydroxybutyrate is converted into acetoacetate as the acidosis improves, urinary ketone measurement should be avoided as a criterion of DKA resolution." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- **HHS resolution (2024):** "While there is no consensus on the definition for resolution of HHS, we consider HHS to be resolved when the measured or calculated serum osmolality falls to <300 mOsm/kg, hyperglycaemia has been corrected, urine output is >0.5 ml kg−1 h−1, cognitive status has improved and the blood glucose is <13.9 mmol/l (250 mg/dl)." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Anion gap demoted: "An anion gap >12 mmol/l indicates the presence of a high anion gap metabolic acidosis consistent with DKA ... Although the anion gap is not recommended as a first-line diagnostic or resolution criterion for these reasons, it may still have some utility in resource settings where ketone measurement is unavailable." Mixed acid-base disorders "are present in about one-third of those presenting with DKA". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- **ADA 2009 (Kitabchi) Table 1, for comparison** (values read from the PMC copy through a summarizer that would only paraphrase the table; spot-check against the PDF before quoting as verbatim) [verified-paraphrase]:
  - Mild DKA: plasma glucose >250 mg/dL; arterial pH 7.25–7.30; serum bicarbonate 15–18 mEq/L; urine and serum ketones positive (nitroprusside); anion gap >10; alert.
  - Moderate DKA: glucose >250 mg/dL; pH 7.00 to <7.24; bicarbonate 10 to <15 mEq/L; anion gap >12; alert/drowsy.
  - Severe DKA: glucose >250 mg/dL; pH <7.00; bicarbonate <10 mEq/L; anion gap >12; stupor/coma.
  - HHS: glucose >600 mg/dL; pH >7.30; bicarbonate >18 mEq/L; ketones small; effective serum osmolality >320 mOsm/kg; anion gap variable; stupor/coma.
  - 2009 formula (verbatim): "Effective serum osmolality: 2[measured Na+ (mEq/l)] + glucose (mg/dl)/18." [verified]
- **ADA 2009 resolution** (verbatim fragments): "Criteria for resolution of ketoacidosis include a blood glucose <200 mg/dl and two of the following criteria" ... "a venous pH >7.3, and a calculated anion gap ≤12 mEq/l." (The third criterion, serum bicarbonate ≥15 mEq/L, was not returned verbatim by the summarizer [recalled].) HHS: "Resolution of HHS is associated with normal osmolality and regain of normal mental status." [verified fragments] — ADA 2009.
- ADA 2009 on ICU: severe DKA/HHS "should be managed with intravenous regular insulin in the ICU." [verified fragment] — ADA 2009.

## 4. UK JBDS-IP guidelines (DKA revised March 2023; HHS 2022/2023) and how they differ from the US consensus

### Takeaway
JBDS DKA uses the same D-K-A structure but a stricter acidosis cut-off (bicarbonate <15.0 mmol/L, not <18) and glucose >11.0 mmol/L; it defines severity by "red flag" markers that trigger consultant review and Level 2/HDU referral (ketones >6.0, bicarbonate <5.0, pH <7.0, admission K+ <3.5, GCS <12, SpO2 <92%, SBP <90, HR >100 or <60, anion gap >16) rather than a three-tier scale. JBDS HHS describes HHS clinically (osmolality ≥320 mOsm/kg using 2Na + glucose + urea, glucose ≥30 mmol/L, ketones ≤3.0, pH >7.3, bicarbonate ≥15) and warns against diagnosing on biochemistry alone; it has its own HDU triggers (e.g., osmolality >350, Na >160, pH <7.1) and uses the 2.4 mmol/L-per-5.5 mmol/L sodium correction.

### Cited Findings
- **JBDS DKA citation (guideline PDF read this session):** Joint British Diabetes Societies for Inpatient Care (JBDS-IP). The Management of Diabetic Ketoacidosis in Adults. JBDS 02. Revised March 2023. https://abcd.care/sites/default/files/site_uploads/JBDS_Guidelines_Current/JBDS_02_DKA_Guideline_with_QR_code_March_2023.pdf. Journal summary: Dhatariya KK; Joint British Diabetes Societies for Inpatient Care. The management of diabetic ketoacidosis in adults—An updated guideline from the Joint British Diabetes Society for Inpatient Care. *Diabet Med.* 2022;39(6):e14788. doi:10.1111/dme.14788. PMID 35224769 (abstract read). [verified]
- JBDS DKA diagnostic criteria (verbatim): "All of these must be present to make the diagnosis / The 'D' – a blood glucose concentration of >11.0 mmol/L or known to have diabetes mellitus / The 'K' – a capillary or blood ketone concentration of >3.0 mmol/L or significant ketonuria (2+ or more on standard urine sticks) / The 'A' – a bicarbonate concentration of <15.0 mmol/L and/or venous pH <7.3". [verified] — JBDS DKA 2023, p.10.
- JBDS on the ADA definition: "The ADA definition is slightly different, and it also uses the anion gap as part of the diagnostic criteria to judge severity (2). The most common equation to calculate anion gap is ([Na+] + [K+]) − ([Cl−] + [HCO3−]). There has been a call to update the ADA guideline (3)." (Written before the 2024 consensus; note JBDS anion gap includes K+.) [verified] — JBDS DKA 2023.
- JBDS severity (verbatim): "The presence of one or more of the following may indicate severe DKA: • Blood ketones over 6.0 mmol/L • Bicarbonate level below 5.0 mmol/L • Venous/arterial pH below 7.0 • Hypokalaemia on admission (under 3.5 mmol/L) • GCS less than 12 or abnormal AVPU scale • Oxygen saturation below 92% on air (assuming normal baseline respiratory function) • Systolic BP below 90 mmHg • Pulse over 100 or below 60 bpm • Anion gap above 16 [Anion Gap = (Na+ + K+) – (Cl- + HCO3-)]". [verified] — JBDS DKA 2023, p.23.
- JBDS escalation (verbatim): "If the individual exhibits any of these signs, resuscitation and treatment should be started without delay, and an intensive monitoring regimen put in place. Depending on local circumstances individuals who fulfil the criteria for severity or who require intensive monitoring should be reviewed by a consultant physician and considered for swift referral to a Level 2/HDU (High Dependency Unit) environment, or if the individual has failed to improve after initial resuscitation measures." Also: "If intravenous access cannot be obtained request critical care support immediately." [verified] — JBDS DKA 2023.
- JBDS DKA resolution (verbatim, body text): "Resolution of DKA is defined as ketones less than 0.6 mmol/L and venous pH over 7.3 (do not use bicarbonate as a surrogate at this stage because the hyperchloraemic acidosis associated with large volumes of 0.9% sodium chloride will lower bicarbonate levels)". (The summary algorithm Box also says "Continue FRIII until ketones less than 0.6 mmol/L, venous pH >7.3 and/or venous bicarbonate over 18 mmol/L"; an example local-trust monitoring chart in Appendix 3 uses "pH>7.3 and/or blood ketones<0.3 mmoll/L" — an internal inconsistency in example material, not the guideline definition.) [verified] — JBDS DKA 2023.
- JBDS treatment targets that double as "failure to improve" triggers: blood ketones fall ≥0.5 mmol/L/h, venous bicarbonate rise ≥3.0 mmol/L/h, capillary glucose fall ≥3.0 mmol/L/h; "If not improving ... seek senior advice". [verified] — JBDS DKA 2023 (algorithm and Section on treatment targets).
- JBDS settled practice points (no longer "controversial"): "1. Measure venous rather than arterial bicarbonate and pH 2. Blood ketone meters should be used for point of care testing 3. 0.9% sodium chloride solution is the recommended fluid of choice on the general medical ward ... 4. Subcutaneous long-acting analogue/human insulin should be continued 5. Insulin should be administered as a FRIII calculated on body weight 6. Do not use a priming (bolus) dose of insulin". [verified] — JBDS DKA 2023. (Contrast: 2024 consensus allows a 0.1 U/kg IV/IM bolus "if a delay in obtaining venous access is anticipated".)
- JBDS euglycemic DKA (verbatim): "This is the development of DKA in people known to have diabetes but where the glucose is normal, or not particularly raised ... This condition is treated in exactly the same way as hyperglycaemic DKA." "If DKA occurs with SGLT inhibitor use, they should be stopped. A 'Yellow Card' should also be completed." DKA in pregnancy "can occur with only very modest elevation of glucose levels, i.e. <13.9mmol/l, ('euglycaemic DKA')". [verified] — JBDS DKA 2023.
- JBDS differential (verbatim): "Ketoacidosis without a raised glucose in a person with alcoholism is virtually diagnostic of alcoholic ketoacidosis ... If alcoholic ketoacidosis is suspected, then ß-hydroxybutyrate should be measured and not urine ketones, because acetoacetate production can be supressed in alcoholic ketoacidosis." Starvation ketosis: "Ketone concentrations can rise to over 6 mmol/L ... acid base and electrolyte disturbances are often minimal". [verified] — JBDS DKA 2023.
- JBDS DKA scope: "For 16-18 year olds Use this guideline if managed by the adult diabetes team but if managed by the paediatric team follow: BSPED DKA Guidelines". [verified] — JBDS DKA 2023 cover. (Pediatric pointer: children are covered by ISPAD/BSPED guidance, handled by another researcher.)
- **JBDS HHS citation:** Mustafa OG, Haq M, Dashora U, et al.; Joint British Diabetes Societies (JBDS) for Inpatient Care Group. Management of Hyperosmolar Hyperglycaemic State (HHS) in Adults: An updated guideline from the Joint British Diabetes Societies (JBDS) for Inpatient Care Group. *Diabet Med.* 2023;40(3):e15005. doi:10.1111/dme.15005. PMID 36370077. PMCID PMC10107355 (full text read; epub 21 Dec 2022). [verified]
- JBDS HHS defining features (abstract, verbatim): "Clinical features of HHS include marked hypovolaemia, osmolality ≥320 mOsm/kg using [(2×Na+) + glucose+urea], marked hyperglycaemia ≥30 mmol/L, without significant ketonaemia (≤3.0 mmol/L), without significant acidosis (pH >7.3) and bicarbonate ≥15 mmol/L." [verified] — Mustafa 2023.
- JBDS HHS on diagnosis (verbatim): "International guidelines vary as to the precise definition of HHS, but there are characteristic features that differentiate it from other hyperglycaemic states such as DKA. Defining HHS by osmolality alone is inappropriate without considering other clinical features". "HHS should not be diagnosed using biochemical parameters alone. However, the blood glucose is markedly raised (usually ≥30 mmol/L), as is the osmolality (usually ≥320 mOsm/kg)." [verified] — Mustafa 2023.
- JBDS HHS osmolality formula choice (verbatim): "The best approximation to measured osmolality can be calculated using the formula [(2×Na+) + glucose + urea] ... as urea is an ineffective osmolyte, it can be omitted from the equation to allow calculation of tonicity (or effective osmolality). This is of the greatest importance when someone is hyponatraemic since tonicity indicates risk of cerebral oedema". "If frequent measurement of osmolality is not practical, osmolality should be calculated using the formula [(2×Na+) + glucose + urea]. This is the formula used throughout this guideline." [verified] — Mustafa 2023.
- JBDS mixed DKA/HHS (verbatim): "In HHS there is usually no significant ketosis/ketonaemia (blood ketones ≤3.0 mmol/L), although a mild acidosis (pH <7.3, bicarbonate >15.0 mmol/L) may accompany those affected by acute kidney injury or severe sepsis." Figure 4: "Treat as mixed DKA/HHS if all the following criteria are met: marked hypovolaemia, marked hyperosmolality (osmolality ≥320 mOsm/kg), pH <7.3, bicarbonate <15 mmol/L and blood ketones >3.0 mmol/L. Start a Fixed Rate Intravenous Insulin Infusion (FRIII) and IV fluids immediately and treat according to DKA pathway using 0.1 units/kg/h." [verified] — Mustafa 2023.
- **JBDS HHS HDU/senior-review triggers (verbatim):** "The presence of one or more of the following should prompt discussion about the need for admission to a High‐Dependency Unit/Level 2 environment. Immediate senior review by a clinician skilled in the management of HHS should be considered: • Measured or calculated Osmolality >350 mOsm/kg • Sodium >160 mmol/L • Venous/arterial pH <7.1 • Hypokalaemia (<3.5 mmol/L) or hyperkalaemia (≥6 mmol/L) on admission • Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) <12 or abnormal AVPU (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) scale • Oxygen saturation <92% on air (assuming normal baseline respiratory function) • Systolic blood pressure < 90 mm Hg • Pulse >100 or <60 bpm • Urine output <0.5 ml/kg/h • Serum creatinine >200 μmol/L and/or acute kidney injury • Hypothermia • Macrovascular event such as myocardial infarction or stroke • Other serious co‐morbidity". [verified] — Mustafa 2023.
- JBDS HHS resolution (Table 3, verbatim): "Criteria for resolution of HHS: Holistic assessment of the following: 1) Clinical and cognitive status is back to the pre‐morbid state 2) Osmolality <300 mOsm/kg 3) Hypovolaemia has been corrected (urine output ≥0.5 ml/kg/h) 4) Blood glucose <15 mmol/L". "What's new? ... We introduced a formal definition of resolution of HHS and audit standards." [verified] — Mustafa 2023.
- JBDS HHS insulin timing (verbatim): "An FRIII should not be started as part of the initial treatment unless significant ketonaemia is present (>3.0 mmol/L or urine ketones >2+). In all other circumstances, intravenous fluids should be administered first, and an FRIII only started once the glucose has stopped falling." [verified] — Mustafa 2023. (The 2024 consensus notes this view: "Some have recommended that insulin be withheld until glucose has stopped dropping".)
- JBDS HHS precipitants (verbatim): "The most common precipitating factors for HHS include: infections, discontinuation/omission of antidiabetic medications, cardiovascular events, pancreatitis and drugs (corticosteroids, thiazides, sympathomimetic agents and conventional antipsychotics)." Mortality: "In the United States, it accounts for only 13% of hyperglycaemia‐related emergency admissions but has a higher mortality than DKA ... reported series suggest mortality may have improved though remains high at between 15% and 20%." Cognition: "Alterations in cognitive status are more common when the osmolality rises >330 mOsm/kg." Severe hypovolaemia: "tachycardia (pulse > 100 bpm) and/or hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 100 mm Hg)". [verified] — Mustafa 2023.
- JBDS HHS lactate/ketones: "Serum lactate and ketones must also be checked, usually using venous blood gases (VBG) and point of care testing. The former can indicate lactic acidosis related to sepsis, for example and the latter will exclude significant ketonaemia (β‐hydroxybutyrate <1.0 mmol/L)." [verified] — Mustafa 2023.

## 5. Diagnostic labs and their pitfalls (ketones, anion gap, VBG vs ABG, corrected Na, osmolality, K+, phosphate, lactate, lipase, WBC, pseudohyponatremia, creatinine)

### Takeaway
Measure blood beta-hydroxybutyrate (BHB; point-of-care or lab), not urine nitroprusside ketones, for both diagnosis and resolution. A venous gas is enough (mean arterial-venous pH difference about 0.03). Anion gap is now secondary (consider albumin correction if used). Potassium is usually normal or high on arrival despite a total-body deficit of 3-6 mmol/kg; insulin is held if K+ <3.5 mmol/L (2024; it was <3.3 mEq/L in 2009). Know the artifacts: Jaffe creatinine is falsely raised by acetoacetate, amylase and lipase rise without pancreatitis, WBC 10,000-15,000 is typical, and chylomicronemia causes pseudohyponatremia. True hyperglycemic (translocational) hyponatremia is corrected by 1.6 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL (consensus) or 2.4 (Hillier; JBDS).

### Cited Findings
- **Ketones (2024):** "Assessment of ketonaemia can be performed semiquantitatively by the nitroprusside reaction in urine or serum, which measures acetoacetic acid (but not β-hydroxybutyrate, the main ketoacid produced in DKA), or quantitatively by direct measurement of β-hydroxybutyrate in blood from capillary point-of-care testing (POCT) or in the hospital laboratory. Both types of ketones have similar diagnostic sensitivity, but measuring β-hydroxybutyrate in blood is more specific for detecting DKA than measuring acetoacetate in urine." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- "Reliance on urine ketone testing can underestimate the severity of ketonaemia early in the course of DKA because of a lag in the formation of acetoacetate, and conversely overestimate its severity later in the course of DKA when β-hydroxybutyrate is being cleared and converted into acetoacetate. In addition, several sulfhydryl drugs (e.g., captopril) and medications such as valproate can give false-positive nitroprusside urine tests. Thus, for diagnosis and monitoring of the response to therapy, we recommend direct measurement of venous or capillary β-hydroxybutyrate". "Blood concentrations of β-hydroxybutyrate ≥3.0 mmol/l correlate well with acid–base changes, with >90% sensitivity and specificity for the diagnosis of DKA". "However, there is concern about how accurate POCT instruments are compared with laboratory instruments for measuring β-hydroxybutyrate levels ≥5 mmol/l." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- ADA 2009 on nitroprusside: "this assay does not recognize the presence of β-hydroxybutyrate." [verified fragment] — ADA 2009.
- Review: "During DKA, beta-hydroxybutyrate is the predominant ketone in blood. As DKA resolves, beta-hydroxybutyrate is oxidized to acetoacetate, which is the predominant ketone in the urine. Because of this lag, a urine ketone test might be increasing even as DKA is resolving." [verified, abstract] — Huang J, Yeung AM, Bergenstal RM, et al. Update on measuring ketones. *J Diabetes Sci Technol.* 2024;18(3):714-726. doi:10.1177/19322968231152236. PMID 36794812. PMCID PMC11089855.
- **Anion gap (2024):** "The anion gap is calculated by subtracting the major measured anions (chloride and bicarbonate) from the major measured cation (sodium). An anion gap >12 mmol/l indicates the presence of a high anion gap metabolic acidosis consistent with DKA." Hyperchloremic normal-gap acidosis during recovery "may delay transition back to subcutaneous insulin if mistaken for persistent DKA". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. (JBDS includes K+: "[Anion Gap = (Na+ + K+) – (Cl- + HCO3-)]", severity cut-off >16. [verified])
- **Albumin correction (not in any of the guidelines read):** "adjusted anion gap = observed anion gap + 0.25 x ([normal albumin] [observed albumin]), where albumin concentrations are in g/L; if given in g/dL, the factor is 2.5." [verified, abstract] — Figge J, Jabor A, Kazda A, Fencl V. Anion gap and hypoalbuminemia. *Crit Care Med.* 1998;26(11):1807-1810. doi:10.1097/00003246-199811000-00019. PMID 9824071. (Fourth author from memory [recalled]; first three verified.)
- **Venous vs arterial:** 44 ED DKA episodes: "The mean difference between arterial and venous pH values was 0.03 (range 0.0 to 0.11)"; "Venous blood gas measurements accurately demonstrate the degree of acidosis of adult ED patients presenting with diabetic ketoacidosis." [verified, abstract] — Brandenburg MA, Dire DJ. Comparison of arterial and venous blood gas values in the initial emergency department evaluation of patients with diabetic ketoacidosis. *Ann Emerg Med.* 1998;31(4):459-465. doi:10.1016/s0196-0644(98)70254-9. PMID 9546014. The 2024 consensus specifies "venous blood gases" in the initial work-up and "venous pH" for resolution; JBDS: "Measure venous rather than arterial bicarbonate and pH" and "Venous blood gas can be used safely". [verified] Note: the 2009 severity table used *arterial* pH. [verified-paraphrase]
- **Corrected sodium:** 2024 consensus (HHS fluid section): "(a reduction of 5.6 mmol/l [100 mg/dl] of glucose will result in a 1.6 mmol/l rise in sodium concentration). The initial rise in serum sodium is not an indication to give hypotonic fluids". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. JBDS HHS: "a fall in blood glucose of 5.5 mmol/L will result in a 2.4 mmol/L rise in sodium ... If the inevitable rise in serum sodium is much greater than 2.4 mmol/L for each 5.5 mmol/L fall in blood glucose this would suggest insufficient fluid replacement"; worked example "corrected Na+ = 122 + (2.4 × 4) = 131.6"; "the rate of fall of serum sodium should not exceed 10 mmol/L in 24 h." [verified] — Mustafa 2023. ADA 2009: "serum sodium may be corrected by adding 1.6 mg/dl to the measured serum sodium" ... "for each 100 mg/dl of glucose above 100 mg/dl" (the "mg/dl" for sodium is a unit error in the original; mEq/L intended). [verified fragments]
  - Katz MA. Hyperglycemia-induced hyponatremia—calculation of expected serum sodium depression. *N Engl J Med.* 1973;289(16):843-844. doi:10.1056/NEJM197310182891607. PMID 4763428. (Origin of the 1.6 factor; no abstract available, so the 1.6 value is attributed via Hillier's abstract: "the commonly used correction factor of a 1.6 meq/L decrease in serum sodium concentration for every 100 mg/dL increase in plasma glucose concentration".) [verified citation; content secondary]
  - Hillier: "Overall, the mean decrease in serum sodium concentration averaged 2.4 meq/L for every 100 mg/dL increase in glucose concentration ... Up to 400 mg/dL, the standard correction of 1.6 worked well, but if the glucose concentration was >400 mg/dL, a correction factor of 4.0 was better." (6 healthy volunteers, somatostatin + dextrose clamp.) [verified, abstract] — Hillier TA, Abbott RD, Barrett EJ. Hyponatremia: evaluating the correction factor for hyperglycemia. *Am J Med.* 1999;106(4):399-403. doi:10.1016/s0002-9343(99)00055-8. PMID 10225241.
- **Osmolality:** 2024/ADA 2026 HHS criteria use effective osmolality "[2 × Na+ (mmol/L) + glucose (mmol/L)]" >300 mOsm/kg or total "[2 × Na+ (mmol/L) + glucose (mmol/L) + urea (mmol/L)]" >320 mOsm/kg [verified, Table 16.1]; ADA 2009: "Effective serum osmolality: 2[measured Na+ (mEq/l)] + glucose (mg/dl)/18" with HHS threshold >320 [verified/verified-paraphrase]; JBDS uses total (2Na + glucose + urea) ≥320 and notes "Urea is not an effective osmolyte but including it in the calculation is important in the hyperosmolar state, as it is one of the indicators of severe dehydration." [verified] — Mustafa 2023. ADA 2009 also tied coma to osmolality: altered mentation "in the absence of definitive elevation of effective osmolality (≥320 mOsm/kg)" should prompt a search for other causes [verified fragment]; JBDS: "Alterations in cognitive status are more common when the osmolality rises >330 mOsm/kg." [verified]
- **Potassium:** "Despite experiencing a total-body potassium depletion of 3–6 mmol/kg due to long-standing osmotic diuresis, emesis and hyperaldosteronism, most patients with DKA present with normal or high serum potassium levels ... This is because metabolic acidosis and insulin deficiency cause the movement of potassium from the intracellular to the extracellular compartment." "Low-normal or low potassium levels (<3.5 mmol/l) are present on admission in 5–10% of patients with DKA; in such cases, potassium replacement should begin at a rate of 10 mmol/h, and insulin therapy should be delayed until the potassium level increases to >3.5 mmol/l". "To prevent hypokalaemia, potassium replacement should be started after serum levels fall below 5.0 mmol/l to maintain a potassium level of 4–5 mmol/l". "we recommend measuring serum potassium 2 h after starting insulin administration and every 4 h thereafter". Table 3: hypokalemia (<3.5) in "∼55% of DKA and 51% of HHS patients"; severe ≤2.5 mmol/L associated with "adjusted OR 4.9 [95% CI 1.3, 18.8]" for inpatient mortality. [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
  - ADA 2009 threshold differed: "insulin treatment should be delayed until potassium concentration is restored to >3.3 mEq/l." Also: "Despite total-body potassium depletion, mild-to-moderate hyperkalemia is common." [verified fragments] — ADA 2009.
  - JBDS: "Hypokalaemia on admission (under 3.5 mmol/L)" is a severe-DKA marker; HHS: K+ ≥6.0 → "Senior review ICU/outreach"; <3.5 → "Senior review as additional potassium is required (via central line in high dependency unit)". [verified]
- **Phosphate:** "Whole-body losses can be up to 1.0 mmol/kg; however, unless there is evidence of muscle weakness, such as respiratory or cardiac compromise with the phosphate <1.0 mmol/l, routine administration of phosphate is not indicated ... When necessary, 20–30 mmol of potassium phosphate can be added to replacement fluids." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. ADA 2009: "serum phosphate is often normal or increased at presentation." [verified fragment]
- **Lactate:** consensus states fluid resuscitation "increases tissue/organ perfusion (which decreases lactate formation)" [verified]; JBDS HHS: "Serum lactate and ketones must also be checked, usually using venous blood gases (VBG) and point of care testing. The former can indicate lactic acidosis related to sepsis" [verified]. No guideline read gives a lactate cut-off for DKA.
- **Amylase/lipase:** "Hyperamylasemia has been reported in 21–79% of patients with DKA"; "lipase could also be elevated in DKA in the absence of pancreatitis." [verified fragments] — ADA 2009. Consensus on abdominal pain: "Nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain are common in DKA (>50%) but are uncommon in HHS ... Further clinical evaluation is necessary if this complaint is not resolved with the resolution of dehydration and metabolic acidosis." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- **WBC:** "leukocytosis with cell counts >25,000 mm3 may designate infection and require further evaluation" (counts of 10,000–15,000/mm3 typical of DKA itself, per summarizer paraphrase). [verified fragment] — ADA 2009.
- **Pseudohyponatremia:** pseudohyponatremia "may occur in DKA in the presence of severe chylomicronemia." [verified fragment] — ADA 2009.
- **Creatinine artifact (not addressed in the 2024 consensus, ADA SOC or JBDS texts read):** "At presentation, the Jaffé assay gave falsely high values of plasma creatinine (median 99 micromol L(-1)), in contrast to the PAP+ (median 60.5 micromol L(-1)) and HPLC assays (median 67.5 micromol L(-1)) ... the positive error by the Jaffé method correlated with the acetoacetate concentration (r = 0.79, P < 0.0001)"; "beta-hydroxybutyrate caused no additional interference"; "Enzymatic assays lack this interference." [verified, abstract] — Kemperman FA, Weber JA, Gorgels J, et al. The influence of ketoacids on plasma creatinine assays in diabetic ketoacidosis. *J Intern Med.* 2000;248(6):511-517. doi:10.1046/j.1365-2796.2000.00768.x. PMID 11155144. Supporting lab study (pediatric samples): Jaffe methods "much more susceptible to interference by acetone and acetoacetate" — Feldman-Kiss D, Li D, Cleve R, et al. *Pediatr Nephrol.* 2022;37(6):1347-1353. doi:10.1007/s00467-021-05324-0. PMID 34757480. [verified, abstract]
- **ECG:** "an electrocardiogram should be performed to assess for evidence of biochemically induced repolarisation abnormalities, such as peaked T waves from hyperkalaemia and ischaemia." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- **Differential of ketosis (2024):** "starvation ketosis, alcoholic ketoacidosis, and ketosis of pregnancy and hyperemesis ... The diagnosis of starvation ketosis is suggested by a history of dietary intake of <2090 kJ/day (500 kcal/day)". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.

## 6. Precipitating causes, their frequencies, and the euglycemic DKA definition

### Takeaway
In US series, insulin omission (41-60%) outranks infection (14-16%) and new-onset diabetes (17-24%) as the DKA trigger; worldwide, infection is the commonest (14-58%). HHS is driven mainly by infection (30-60%; UTI and pneumonia), plus stroke, MI, pancreatitis and drugs. Drug triggers named in the consensus include SGLT2 inhibitors, glucocorticoids, antipsychotics and immune checkpoint inhibitors (JBDS adds thiazides and sympathomimetics). Euglycemic DKA is DKA with glucose <200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L); it accounts for about 10% of DKA, mostly SGLT2-inhibitor related. Cost-related insulin rationing (up to 20% of US insulin users) is named as a DKA risk.

### Cited Findings
- Consensus Table 1 "Precipitating causes of DKA in adults by region" (data are %; adapted from Dhatariya et al.), **USA row:** New-onset diabetes "17.2–23.8"; Infection "14.0–16.0"; Insulin omission "41.0–59.6"; Other "9.7–18.0"; Unknown "3.0–4.2". UK row: 6.1 / 44.6 / 19.7 / 10.9 / 18.7. [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- "Between 6% and 21% of adults present with DKA as their initial diagnosis of type 1 diabetes ... Worldwide, infection is the most common precipitating factor for DKA, occurring in 14–58% of cases. Other acute conditions that may precipitate DKA include stroke, alcohol and substance use, pancreatitis, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction and trauma". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Insulin omission: "The omission of insulin therapy, often in the setting of psychological and socioeconomic factors, is a major cause of DKA ... discontinuation of insulin therapy to account for more than two-thirds of all DKA admissions" (urban minority cohort). "In patients with recurrent DKA, up to 75% of the admissions have been attributed to insufficient use of insulin therapy". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. ADA 2009: insulin omission "accounts for more than half of DKA admissions in inner-city and minority populations." [verified fragment]
- Cost-related rationing: "insulin rationing—defined as skipping insulin doses, using less insulin than prescribed or delaying the purchase of insulin to save money—has been reported in up to 20% of people treated with insulin. Cost-related insulin rationing is most commonly reported in non-Hispanic Black, middle-income, and underinsured or uninsured populations and has been associated with increased risk of DKA." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. ADA SOC 2026 Table 6.8 lists "Insulin rationing" as a risk factor. [verified]
- Drugs: "Glucocorticoids may precipitate acute and sustained hyperglycaemia by countering insulin action. Antipsychotic medications may also raise DKA risk, although the precise mechanism is uncertain. Approximately 1–2% of patients receiving checkpoint inhibitors develop new-onset autoimmune diabetes ... DKA was present at diagnosis in 69.7%" (systematic review, 278 patients). [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- SGLT2 inhibitors: "SGLT2 inhibitor-associated DKA occurs in approximately 4% of people with type 1 diabetes; the risk can be 5–17 times higher than in people with type 1 diabetes not treated with SGLT2 inhibitors ... DKA is uncommon in people with type 2 diabetes treated with SGLT2 inhibitors, with an estimated incidence of 0.6–4.9 events per 1000 patient-years". RR in T2D: "2.46 (95% CI 1.16, 5.21)" (RCT meta-analysis) and "1.74 (95% CI 1.07, 2.83)" (observational). Risk factors: "very-low-carbohydrate diets and prolonged fasting, dehydration, excessive alcohol intake and the presence of autoimmunity". "35% of people treated with SGLT2 inhibitors presenting with DKA had glucose levels <11.1 mmol/l (200 mg/dl) ... in another series, 71% ... had glucose levels ≤13.9 mmol/l (250 mg/dl)." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Euglycemic DKA definition (2024): "plasma glucose levels <11.1 mmol/l (200 mg/dl) in the presence of ketosis and metabolic acidosis criteria of DKA"; ~10% of DKA; SGLT2 inhibitors account for "the majority of cases". Management (Table 4): "In euglycaemic DKA, 5–10% dextrose should be added to intravenous fluid or started at the same time as the 0.9% sodium chloride. SGLT2 inhibitors should be stopped on admission." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. ADA SOC 2026 Section 16 repeats the ~10% figure and the <200 mg/dL definition. [verified] JBDS uses a looser definition ("glucose is normal, or not particularly raised") and starts 10% glucose because glucose is <14 mmol/L. [verified]
- Pumps: "In pump users presenting with DKA, the most common precipitating factors are management error and underlying infection; these are more common precipitating causes than device malfunction". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Ketosis-prone T2D: "over half of Black/African American and Hispanic/Latino adults with newly diagnosed diabetes presenting with unprovoked DKA have type 2 diabetes". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- HHS precipitants (2024): "Infection is the major precipitating factor in 30–60% of patients with HHS, with urinary tract infections and pneumonia being the most common. Other common precipitating causes of HHS include acute cerebrovascular events, acute myocardial infarction, surgery, acute pancreatitis, and the use of drugs that affect carbohydrate metabolism ... corticosteroids, sympathomimetic agents and antipsychotic drugs". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. ADA 2009: "The most common precipitating factor in the development of DKA and HHS is infection." [verified fragment]
- Cocaine: not named in the 2024 consensus (which says "alcohol and substance use"), JBDS or ADA SOC text read. Cocaine as an independent DKA risk factor is from older US inner-city cohort data [recalled; not verified this session].

## 7. Epidemiology and outcomes (US focus)

### Takeaway
US DKA admissions fell slightly in 2000-2009, then rose about 6% a year (2009-2014), and ED plus inpatient DKA and HHS rates kept rising to 2015. In-hospital DKA case-fatality is now under 1% (0.4% in 2014; 0.20% in T1D to 1.04% in T2D). HHS mortality is 5- to 10-fold higher. Mixed DKA/HHS carries the highest mortality (8% vs 5% HHS vs 3% DKA in one academic cohort). About 10-20% of patients are readmitted, up to 22% within 30 days; about 40% of 30-day readmissions are recurrent DKA, half within 2 weeks. No national US DKA/HHS hospitalization series beyond about 2020 was found this session.

## Gaps and conflicts

**Internal inconsistencies in the 2024 consensus**
- DKA glucose threshold is written "≥11.1 mmol/l (200 mg/dl)" in the text and Table 2 but ">11.1 mmol/l (200 mg/dl)" in the rationale sentence. ADA SOC 2026 Table 16.1 uses "≥200 mg/dL". Use ≥200 mg/dL.
- HHS acid-base: Fig. 2/Table 16.1 require "bicarbonate concentration ≥15 mmol/L", but the narrative says "most people with HHS have an admission pH ≥7.30 and a bicarbonate level ≥18 mmol/l", and the insulin section defines HHS "without acidosis (pH ≥7.3 and bicarbonate ≥18 mmol/l)" and mixed DKA/HHS as bicarbonate <18. A hyperosmolar patient with bicarbonate 15-17.9 mmol/L and BHB <3.0 falls in a grey zone: HHS by Fig. 2, but treated as mixed by the insulin text.
- Mixed DKA/HHS has no figure-level definition. The consensus uses an operational definition (in a hyperosmolar patient: BHB ≥3.0, ketonuria ≥2+, pH <7.30 or bicarbonate <18). JBDS requires all of osmolality ≥320, pH <7.3, bicarbonate <15 and ketones >3.0. Pasquel 2020 used "DKA criteria plus elevated effective osmolality".
- Phosphate threshold: the consensus says replace when there is weakness "with the phosphate <1.0 mmol/l" (≈3.1 mg/dL), which would include most patients. The traditional US threshold is <1.0 mg/dL (≈0.32 mmol/L) [recalled from ADA 2009; not verified this session]. A possible unit error; check before printing.
- Pasquel 2020 numbers quoted differently: the consensus gives the mixed-vs-HHS aOR as "2.7 [95% CI 1.5, 4.9]" and the hypoglycemia aOR as "4 [95% CI 1.4, 16.8]" (Table 3, while the text says "4.8-fold"); the Pasquel abstract gives aOR 2.7 (95% CI 1.4, 4.9) versus isolated crises and aOR 4.8 (1.4, 16.8). Cite Pasquel directly.

**Disagreements between societies**
- DKA acidosis cut-off: bicarbonate <18 mmol/L (US) vs <15.0 mmol/L (JBDS).
- DKA resolution: US allows "venous pH ≥7.3 **or** bicarbonate ≥18 mmol/l" with ketones <0.6; JBDS uses ketones <0.6 **and** venous pH >7.3 and says not to use bicarbonate (hyperchloremia). JBDS's own algorithm box says "and/or venous bicarbonate over 18 mmol/L", and its example chart (Appendix 3) says ketones <0.3 mmol/L.
- HHS resolution glucose: <13.9 mmol/L (250 mg/dL) in the US vs <15 mmol/L (≈270 mg/dL) in JBDS. HHS osmolality definition: effective >300 or total >320 (US) vs total ≥320 only (JBDS). HHS glucose: ≥600 mg/dL (33.3 mmol/L) in the US vs "usually ≥30 mmol/L" (≈540 mg/dL) in JBDS.
- Sodium correction: 1.6 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL (US consensus; Katz) vs 2.4 per 5.5 mmol/L (JBDS; Hillier). Hillier found the relationship nonlinear (4.0 above 400 mg/dL). Neither guideline gives a corrected-Na formula in its diagnostic criteria.
- Insulin bolus: the US allows 0.1 U/kg IV/IM "if a delay in obtaining venous access is anticipated"; JBDS says "Do not use a priming (bolus) dose of insulin".
- Potassium threshold for holding insulin: >3.5 mmol/L (2024 consensus) vs >3.3 mEq/L (ADA 2009, superseded). Many US order sets may still use 3.3.
- SGLT2 inhibitors in hospital: ADA SOC 2026 Rec 16.11 (A) supports initiating or continuing for heart failure during hospitalization; the consensus says stop on admission in SGLT2i-associated DKA and does not routinely recommend restarting after DKA in T2D. These are not strictly contradictory (different populations), but a handout should state both.
- Anion gap formula: Na − (Cl + HCO3), normal ≤12 (US) vs (Na + K) − (Cl + HCO3), severity >16 (JBDS).

**Values not confirmed verbatim this session**
- ADA 2009 Table 1 values (glucose >250; pH 7.25-7.30 / 7.00-<7.24 / <7.00; HCO3 15-18 / 10-<15 / <10; AG >10 / >12 / >12; HHS osmolality >320, HCO3 >18) came from a summarizer that would only paraphrase. Spot-check against the PDF (doi:10.2337/dc09-9032) before quoting. Whether the 2009 statement carried ADA evidence grades was not checked [recalled: it did].
- ADA SOC 2026 recommendation wording (16.11, 16.16, 16.17, 6.21, 6.22) and Table 16.1 cells were read through a web-fetch summarizer returning fragments, because the publisher blocks PMC XML/full-text download. The grade letters and numbers are consistent across two separate fetches, but the exact punctuation should be checked against the PDF. The 2024 consensus Fig. 2 was not viewed directly (image only); its values are taken from Table 16.1, which the Summary of Revisions says was added to carry the DKA/HHS diagnostic criteria.
- Evidence-grade changes between ADA SOC 2025 and 2026 are not itemized ("levels of evidence for several recommendations have been updated, these changes are not outlined"). The 2025 grades for 16.14/16.15 (A/B) match 2026's 16.16/16.17 (A/B).
- Cocaine as a DKA precipitant and its frequency: not found in the documents read [recalled only].
- Albumin-corrected anion gap and the acetoacetate creatinine artifact are supported by primary lab studies (Figge 1998; Kemperman 2000) but are not mentioned in any guideline read.
- The Katz 1973 paper has no abstract; the 1.6 factor is attributed through Hillier's abstract.

- No published study (through Nov 2026) was found that quantifies how the 2024 criteria (glucose ≥200 or known diabetes; BHB-based) reclassify ED patients compared with the 2009 criteria. An ACEP25 abstract on this topic exists but was not read.
- No DKA/HHS guideline from ACEP, SCCM, ESICM or the Endocrine Society was found. This was a search-based absence; society websites were not checked directly.
