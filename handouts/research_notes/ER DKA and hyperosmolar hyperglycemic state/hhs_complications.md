## 1. HHS management targets: fluids first, rate limits for osmolality, glucose and sodium, insulin timing and dose, dextrose, resolution, recovery time

### Takeaway
Both the 2024 US-led consensus and JBDS 2023 use the same rate limits (osmolality fall 3.0-8.0 mOsm/kg/h; sodium fall no more than 10 mmol/L in 24 h; glucose fall no more than about 5 mmol/L/h, which the consensus renders as 5-6.7 mmol/L/h or 90-120 mg/dL/h). They differ mainly on insulin timing: JBDS says give 0.9% saline alone first and start a fixed-rate insulin infusion (FRIII) at 0.05 U/kg/h only once glucose stops falling (unless ketones >3.0 mmol/L), whereas the consensus says start FRIII 0.05 U/kg/h in HHS without significant ketonemia and only mentions the fluids-first approach as something "some have recommended." Dextrose thresholds also differ (JBDS: glucose <14 mmol/L [about 250 mg/dL]; consensus text: DKA rule of <13.9 mmol/L [250 mg/dL]; ADA 2009: 300 mg/dL for HHS, now superseded).

### Cited Findings

**Rate-of-change targets**
- Consensus (verbatim): "In patients with HHS, the usual time to resolve hyperglycaemia is between 8 and 10 h and the decline should not exceed 5–6.7 mmol l−1 h−1 (90–120 mg dl−1 h−1) to prevent cerebral oedema. Similarly, the rate of decline of serum sodium should not exceed 10 mmol/l in 24 h and the rate of fall in osmolality should be no greater than 3.0–8.0 mOsm kg−1 h−1 to minimise the risk of neurological complications [117]." (Ref 117 is the JBDS HHS guideline.) [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- JBDS (verbatim): "If the IV fluids and FRIII are managed appropriately, the fall in serum osmolality should be within the target range of 3.0–8.0 mOsm/kg/h. If the rate is faster than this, it increases the risk of neurological complications such as cerebral oedema and osmotic demyelination." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS (verbatim): "Overall, the rate of fall of serum sodium should not exceed 10 mmol/L in 24 h. A safe rate of fall of plasma glucose should not be more than 5 mmol/h." And in the insulin section: "To prevent the serum osmolality falling too quickly, the plasma glucose should ideally fall by no more than 5 mmol/L/h." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023. (5 mmol/L/h = 90 mg/dL/h; conversion is arithmetic, not quoted.)
- JBDS abstract aims (verbatim): "Aims of the therapy are to improve clinical status/replace fluid losses by 24 h, gradual decline in osmolality (3.0–8.0 mOsm/kg/h to minimise the risk of neurological complications), blood glucose 10–15 mmol/L in the first 24 h, prevent hypoglycaemia/hypokalaemia and prevent harm (VTE, osmotic demyelination, fluid overload, foot ulceration)." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS goals of therapy list (verbatim): "Gradual normalisation of the osmolality; Safe replacement of fluid and electrolyte losses; Safe and gradual normalisation of blood glucose; Prevention of arterial or venous thrombosis; Prevention of potential complications, for example, cerebral oedema, osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS); Prevention of foot ulceration". [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS: "The key parameter in HHS is osmolality. Sodium and glucose are the main contributors to this, and too rapid changes are dangerous because large fluid shifts can lead to neurological complications, in particular cerebral oedema and osmotic demyelination. Because these parameters are interrelated, we advise that they are plotted on a graph or tabulated to permit appreciation of the rate of change". [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS rationale: HHS "requires less aggressive fluid resuscitation and slower glucose‐lowering than DKA" because "the brain tissue of those who develop HHS, particularly in those who are older, is at higher risk of injury due to rapid shifts in sodium, water and glucose." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS initial glucose target: "An initial target glucose of between 10 and 15 mmol/L is a reasonable goal until the person is eating and drinking normally, and then an individual target glucose (if appropriate 6–10 mmol/L) should be set". (10-15 mmol/L = 180-270 mg/dL by arithmetic.) [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.

**Sodium behavior during treatment (the expected rise)**
- Consensus: "Initial fluid replacement will lower the glucose concentration and osmolality, causing a shift of water into the intracellular space, which may result in a rise in serum sodium (a reduction of 5.6 mmol/l [100 mg/dl] of glucose will result in a 1.6 mmol/l rise in sodium concentration). The initial rise in serum sodium is not an indication to give hypotonic fluids, and the administration of 0.45% sodium chloride is indicated only if osmolality is not declining despite adequate positive fluid balance and appropriate insulin administration." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- JBDS uses a different factor: "This inevitably results in a rise in serum sodium (a fall in blood glucose of 5.5 mmol/L will result in a 2.4 mmol/L rise in sodium). This is not necessarily an indication to give hypotonic solutions. A rising sodium is only a concern if the osmolality is not declining concurrently. If the inevitable rise in serum sodium is much greater than 2.4 mmol/L for each 5.5 mmol/L fall in blood glucose this would suggest insufficient fluid replacement." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023. (Conflict: 1.6 vs 2.4 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL; see Gaps.)
- JBDS on switching to 0.45% saline: "if the osmolality is no longer declining despite adequate fluid replacement with 0.9% sodium chloride solution and an adequate rate of fall of plasma glucose is not being achieved, then 0.45% sodium chloride solution should be substituted. There are no data to justify using fluids that are less concentrated than 0.45% sodium chloride solution." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS worked example on hyponatremic patients and ODS risk: a person with "Na+ 122 mmol/L, glucose 13 mmol/L, urea 23 mmol/L has a calculated osmolality of 280 mOsm/kg and an effective osmolality of 257 mOsm/kg ... So the person with the raised urea has a much lower effective osmolality and is therefore at a greater risk of osmotic demyelination should correction of the hyponatraemia be too fast." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.

**Fluids: type, volume, rate**
- Consensus (all crises): "In adults with DKA or HHS without renal or cardiac compromise, we recommend starting the administration of isotonic saline or balanced crystalloid solutions at an initial rate of 500–1000 ml/h during the first 2–4 h ... Fluid replacement should correct estimated deficits within the first 24–48 h. However, caution should be used when rapidly replacing fluids in those at high risk of fluid overload, including older adults, pregnant individuals, and people with heart or kidney disease or other serious comorbidities." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Consensus: "Older adults with DKA or HHS, as well as individuals with heart failure or end-stage kidney disease on dialysis, should be treated cautiously with smaller boluses of isotonic or crystalloid solutions (e.g., 250 ml boluses) and should undergo frequent assessment of haemodynamic status [131]. In such patients, the use of a standard fluid replacement protocol may be associated with treatment-related complications, including volume overload, need for mechanical ventilation and longer length of stay". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Consensus: "Mean plasma glucose concentrations have been reported to drop by approximately 2.8–3.9 mmol l−1 h−1 (50–70 mg dl−1 h−1) solely in response to intravenous fluid administration in the absence of insulin [2]. This rate of decrease may be even more pronounced in HHS." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- JBDS fluid choice: "the initial fluid replacement of choice should be 0.9% sodium chloride solution with potassium added as required"; "0.9% sodium chloride solution should be used as the principal fluid to restore circulating volume and reverse dehydration because it is relatively hypotonic compared to the serum in someone with HHS." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023. (Contrast: the consensus allows balanced crystalloid as an equal first option; JBDS HHS does not mention balanced crystalloids.)
- JBDS volume goal: "The aim of treatment should be to replace approximately 50% of estimated fluid loss within the first 12 h and the remainder in the following 12 h. However, this will in part be determined by the initial severity, degree of renal impairment and co‐morbidities such as heart failure, which may limit the speed of correction." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS Table 1 typical losses (adapted from Kitabchi & Nyenwe): water 100–220 mL/kg (6–13 L for 60 kg; 10–22 L for 100 kg); Na+ 5–13 mmol/kg; Cl− 5–15 mmol/kg; K+ 4–6 mmol/kg (240–360 mmol for 60 kg). [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS on deceptive appearance: "often the person with HHS may not look as dehydrated as they are, because the hypertonicity leads to preservation of intravascular volume". [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS: "There are almost no data on the benefits or risks of particular fluid replacement regimens in HHS. Controversies persist around the speed and type of fluid replacement". [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS mixed DKA/HHS fluid target: "IV fluid replacement should aim to achieve a positive balance of 3–6 L during the first 12 h and the remaining replacement of estimated fluid loss during the following 12 h, although complete normalisation of biochemistry may take up to 72 h." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023. (In the PMC text this sentence sits in the mixed DKA/HHS Figure 4 paragraph.)
- JBDS care-pathway hourly fluid rates (e.g., first-hour litre of 0.9% saline) are in Figures 1-2, which are images in PMC; see later bullets from the JBDS PDF if obtained, otherwise Gaps.

**Insulin: when and how much**
- JBDS (verbatim): "Use of the previous version of this guideline has confirmed that fluid replacement alone will lower glucose concentrations. An FRIII should not be started as part of the initial treatment unless significant ketonaemia is present (>3.0 mmol/L or urine ketones >2+). In all other circumstances, intravenous fluids should be administered first, and an FRIII only started once the glucose has stopped falling. The risk of adding insulin at the start of treatment will lead to larger osmotic shifts leading to neurological complications. In addition, adding IV insulin too early will also potentially lead to circulatory collapse". [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS dose: "Once the glucose has ceased to fall following initial fluid resuscitation, reassessment of fluid intake and evaluation of renal function must be undertaken. Insulin may be started at this point. As with DKA, an FRIII is preferred, though generally lower doses are required. The recommended initial insulin dose is an FRIII given at 0.05 units/kg/h. If an FRIII is already in place, the infusion rate can be increased by 1.0 unit/h." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS mechanism of harm from early insulin: "Insulin treatment prior to adequate fluid replacement may result in cardiovascular collapse because water will move out of the intravascular space, resulting in a reduction in intravascular volume (a consequence of insulin‐mediated glucose uptake and a diuresis from urinary glucose excretion)." Also "There is also higher risk of hypokalaemia and hypoglycaemia." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- Internal JBDS inconsistency: the abstract says FRIII "should be commenced once osmolality stops falling with fluid replacement unless there is ketonaemia", while the body text says "once the glucose has stopped falling". [verified, both read] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- Consensus (verbatim): "Few studies have assessed the optimal insulin regimen in HHS. If the individual is already being treated with basal insulin, it should be continued at the usual dose and adjusted as needed. If HHS is present with no ketosis or with mild or moderate ketonaemia (blood β-hydroxybutyrate ≥1.0 to <3.0 mmol/l or urine ketones <2+) and without acidosis (pH ≥7.3 and bicarbonate ≥18 mmol/l), then a fixed-rate intravenous insulin infusion should be started at 0.05 U kg−1 h−1. If significant ketonaemia is present (i.e., β-hydroxybutyrate ≥3.0 mmol/l, ketonuria ≥2+, pH <7.30 or bicarbonate <18 mmol/l), which represents mixed DKA/HHS, then a fixed-rate intravenous insulin infusion should be started at 0.1 U kg−1 h−1 [117]." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Consensus on fluids-first: "Some have recommended that insulin be withheld until glucose has stopped dropping, with initial fluid administration alone to prevent a rapid fall in osmolality [117]." (Reported, not endorsed or rejected.) [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Consensus: "The use of rapid-acting subcutaneous insulin analogues is not recommended for the treatment of severe and complicated DKA or with HHS." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Consensus does not say in its text whether an IV insulin bolus should be given in HHS; its bolus statement is DKA-specific ("an insulin bolus (0.1 U/kg) ... if a delay in obtaining venous access is anticipated"). [verified absence in text; Fig. 4 not readable]

**Dextrose**
- JBDS (verbatim): "Glucose infusion (5% or 10%) should be started once blood glucose is <14 mmol/L and run alongside FRIII and other fluid replacement. Choice of the glucose fluid concentration (5% or 10%) depends on the individual patient factors (e.g., risk of fluid overloads) and/or institutional factors (e.g. cost and availability)." (14 mmol/L = about 252 mg/dL.) [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- Consensus text gives the dextrose trigger only in DKA language: "once the plasma glucose concentration is <13.9 mmol/l (250 mg/dl), replacement fluids should be modified to contain 5–10% dextrose"; and Table 3 (hypoglycemia mitigation, applies to "DKA and HHS"): "When glucose levels are reduced to <13.9 mmol/l (250 mg/dl), it is advised to reduce the insulin infusion rate to 0.05 U kg−1 h−1 and replacement fluids should be modified to contain 5–10% dextrose to prevent hypoglycaemia." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. Whether Fig. 4 (HHS arm) uses 300 mg/dL could not be read (image). See Gaps.
- ADA 2009 (superseded) used 300 mg/dL for HHS: see ADA 2009 bullet below if confirmed.

**Resolution and recovery time**
- Consensus HHS resolution (verbatim): "While there is no consensus on the definition for resolution of HHS, we consider HHS to be resolved when the measured or calculated serum osmolality falls to <300 mOsm/kg, hyperglycaemia has been corrected, urine output is >0.5 ml kg−1 h−1, cognitive status has improved and the blood glucose is <13.9 mmol/l (250 mg/dl) [12, 117]." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- JBDS Table 3 (verbatim): "Criteria for resolution of HHS: Holistic assessment of the following: 1) Clinical and cognitive status is back to the pre‐morbid state 2) Osmolality <300 mOsm/kg 3) Hypovolaemia has been corrected (urine output ≥0.5 ml/kg/h) 4) Blood glucose <15 mmol/L". Also: "a normal glucose or sodium concentration in isolation is not sufficient to say that the episode has resolved." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- Time course: consensus "usual time to resolve hyperglycaemia is between 8 and 10 h" [verified]; JBDS "Complete normalisation of electrolytes and osmolality may take up to 72 h" and "Complete correction of electrolyte and osmolality abnormalities may take up to 72 h" [verified]; JBDS care pathway phases "0–60 min, 1–6, 6–12, 12–24 and 24–72 h" [verified]. JBDS recovery: "IV insulin can usually be discontinued once they are eating and drinking but IV fluids may be required for longer if intake is inadequate ... switching from insulin to the appropriate oral or subcutaneous glucose‐lowering drugs should be considered after a period of stability. This may take weeks or months." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- Level of care: consensus "individuals with severe DKA or HHS, or those with critical illness as the precipitating cause (e.g., myocardial infarction, gastrointestinal bleeding, sepsis) or with altered mental status ... should be treated in the ICU". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. JBDS HDU triggers (osmolality >350, Na >160, pH <7.1, K <3.5 or ≥6, GCS <12, SpO2 <92%, SBP <90, HR >100 or <60, UO <0.5 mL/kg/h, creatinine >200 µmol/L or AKI, hypothermia, macrovascular event, other serious comorbidity). [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.

**Potassium in HHS (JBDS table differs from consensus)**
- JBDS Table 2 "Suggested potassium replacement regimen in HHS" (K+ in first 24 h → replacement per litre): "≥6.0 Senior review ICU/outreach; 5.5–5.9 Nil; 3.5–5.5 40 mmol/L; <3.5 Senior review as additional potassium is required (via central line in high dependency unit)". [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- Consensus (all crises): "potassium replacement should be started after serum levels fall below 5.0 mmol/l to maintain a potassium level of 4–5 mmol/l ... For most patients with DKA, 20–30 mmol of potassium per litre of intravenous fluid is sufficient"; if K <3.5 mmol/L, "potassium replacement should begin at a rate of 10 mmol/h, and insulin therapy should be delayed until the potassium level increases to >3.5 mmol/l". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- JBDS: "Those on diuretics may be profoundly hypokalaemic to start with. It is also worth noting that if acute kidney injury is present, hyperkalaemia may be present owing to reduced clearance and therefore closer monitoring is required." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.

**Phosphate and magnesium in HHS**
- Consensus: "There is scarce data on phosphate deficiency or the effects of phosphate replacement in HHS, so we recommend a similar approach to phosphorus replacement" (i.e., replace only if phosphate <1.0 mmol/L with weakness/respiratory/cardiac compromise; "20–30 mmol of potassium phosphate can be added to replacement fluids"). [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- JBDS: "Hypophosphataemia and hypomagnesaemia are common in HHS. There are no data looking at the use of replacement of either of these in HHS." IV magnesium "should only be considered if severely deplete and symptomatic, for example, ECG changes or neurological manifestations". [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.

- Source: **JBDS-HHS PDF 2022** = Joint British Diabetes Societies for Inpatient Care Group (writing group: Mustafa OG, Haq M, Dashora U, Castro E, Dhatariya KK). The Management of Hyperosmolar Hyperglycaemic State (HHS) in Adults. JBDS 06. February 2022 (PDF created 3 Feb 2022, 46 pp). https://abcd.care/sites/default/files/site_uploads/JBDS_Guidelines_Current/JBDS_06_The_Management_of_Hyperosmolar_Hyperglycaemic_State_HHS_%20in_Adults_FINAL_0.pdf (downloaded and converted to text this session). The separate 2-page "HHS care pathway in adults 2022" PDF (JBDS_06_HHS_care_pathway_in_adults_2022.pdf) is image-only and could not be text-read. No PMID (grey literature).
- 0-60 min (verbatim): "T=0 at time intravenous fluids are commenced ... Commence IV 0.9% sodium chloride – 1 litre to run over 1 hour • Consider more rapid replacement if systolic blood pressure (SBP) < 90 mmHg • Caution in the elderly where too rapid rehydration may precipitate heart failure but insufficient may fail to reverse acute kidney injury". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- 0-60 min insulin (verbatim): "ONLY commence insulin infusion quickly in the following circumstances: • If there is HHS and ketonaemia (blood ketones 3ß-hydroxybutyrate >1.0 - ≤3.0 mmol/L or urine ketones < 2+) and not acidotic (venous pH >7.3 and bicarbonate >15.0 mmol/L) then use 0.05 units/kg/hr OR • If there is significant ketonaemia (3ß-hydroxybutyrate >3.0 mmol/L) or ketonuria (≥ 2+) with a pH <7.3 and bicarbonate <15 mmol/L (i.e. mixed DKA and HHS) and use the DKA guideline at 0.1 units/kg/hr". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022. (Note: this PDF pathway lets mild ketonemia >1.0 mmol/L trigger early FRIII at 0.05 U/kg/h, whereas the 2023 Diabet Med body text says no early FRIII "unless significant ketonaemia is present (>3.0 mmol/L or urine ketones >2+)". The PDF's sample chart (Appendix 1) also says "ONLY commence intravenous insulin therapy IF patient has significant ketonaemia (blood ketones >3.0 mmol/L or ketonuria (urine ketones >2+)". So the JBDS documents are not fully self-consistent on the 1.0-3.0 mmol/L band.)
- 0-60 min investigations (verbatim list): capillary and venous plasma glucose; urea and electrolytes; "Measured or calculated serum osmolality [(2xNa+) + glucose + urea]. Until the urea is available, calculate using (2xNa+ + glucose)"; "Venous blood gas (VBG) for: pH, bicarbonate, lactate, and electrolytes"; capillary blood ketones; full blood count; blood cultures; ECG; chest x-ray; urinalysis and culture; CRP (if indicated). Assessment includes "Mini Mental State assessment for cognitive assessment", "Assess for contraindications of low molecular weight heparin (LMWH)", "Assess foot risk score – assume high risk if the person is obtunded or uncooperative • Ensure heels are off-loaded". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- 1-6 h fluids (verbatim): "Using 0.9% sodium chloride solution aim to give a further 0.5-1 litre/hr depending on clinical assessment of dehydration vs. risk of precipitating heart failure. The target is to achieve positive fluid balance of 2-3 litres by 6 hours". "Fluid replacement should be adjusted for those who are <50 kg in body weight or with pre-existing heart and renal disease. More cautious fluid replacement is necessary e.g. 0.25 ml/kg/hr as recommended by NICE". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022. (The "0.25 ml/kg/hr" figure is as printed; it is implausibly low for resuscitation and may be a typo, see Gaps.)
- 1-6 h insulin (verbatim): "ONLY start IV insulin once fluid replacement is adequate and glucose concentrations have plateaued. Starting an IV insulin infusion too early could result in circulatory collapse • If positive fluid balance is adequate, commence low dose IV insulin as a fixed rate intravenous insulin infusion (FRIII) at 0.05 units/kg/hr or if already running, increase rate to 0.1 units/kg/hr if glucose concentrations are not falling". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- 1-6 h decision rules (verbatim): "If plasma Na+ increasing but osmolality declining at appropriate rate, continue 0.9% sodium chloride • If plasma Na+ increasing AND osmolality increasing (or declining at less than 3.0 mOsm/kg/hr), check fluid balance. If positive balance is inadequate, then increase the rate of infusion of 0.9% sodium chloride solution • If the osmolality is increasing and fluid balance adequate, then consider switching to 0.45% sodium chloride at same infusion rate • If osmolality falling at rate exceeding 8.0 mOsm/kg/hr consider reducing infusion rate of IV fluids and/or insulin (if already commenced)". Body text adds: "(or > 3 mOsm/kg/hr in those at high risk of developing cerebral oedema)". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- Hypoglycemia avoidance (verbatim): "Aim to keep BG 10-15 mmol/L in first 24 hours • If BG falls below 14 mmol/L commence 5% or 10% glucose at 125 ml/hr AND CONTINUE 0.9% sodium chloride solution". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- 6-12 h: "Continue IV fluid replacement to achieve positive balance of 3-6 litres by 12 hours". 12-24 h: "Do not expect biochemistry to have normalised by 24 hrs (sodium and osmolality are likely to be raised)"; "Adjust insulin infusion rate hourly by 1 unit/hr increments or decrements to achieve desired BG". 24 h to day 3: "Continue LMWH until day of discharge (consider extended treatment in those deemed very high risk. This may require specialist advice)"; "Daily foot checks"; "Encourage early mobilisation". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- Glucose fall: the PDF body states both "a safe rate of fall of plasma glucose is between 4.0 and 6.0 mmol/hr" and "the plasma glucose should ideally fall by no more than 5 mmol/L/hr" and "a fall of glucose at a rate of up to 5.0 mmol/L per hour is ideal". Rationale: "because most people with HHS are insulin sensitive there is a risk of lowering the osmolality precipitously." [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- Example regimen (Appendix 1 "Sample Insulin and Fluid Prescription Chart", an example local chart, not a mandated protocol): "0.9% sodium chloride 1 litre (no KCl) Over 1st hour; 1 litre (check K+) Over next 2 hours; 1 litre Over next 2 hours; 1 litre Over next 4 hours; 1 litre Over next 4 hours" (Box 5 rates: 1000 mL/h x1 h, 500 mL/h x2 h, 500 mL/h x2 h, 250 mL/h x4 h, 250 mL/h x4 h, 166 mL/h x6 h); "CAUTION: Slower in young people aged 18-25 years, elderly, pregnant, heart or renal failure"; "Once CBG<14.0 mmol/L Give 10% Glucose to run at 125 ml/hr AND Continue Saline"; "Re-assessment of cardio-vascular status at 12 hours is mandatory"; FRIII "calculated on 0.05 units/kg body weight" (weight-band table: 50-59 kg 2.5 U/h; 60-69 kg 3 U/h; 70-79 kg 3.5 U/h; 80-89 kg 4 U/h; 90-99 kg 4.5 U/h; 100-109 kg 5 U/h ... 130-139 kg 6.5 U/h; "<50 kg or >140 kg: seek senior advice"); "ALWAYS continue subcutaneous intermediate* or basal insulin"; potassium "Add potassium as per guidance below EXCEPT for the first Saline (1 hour) bag": ">5.5 mmol/L None; 3.5 – 5.5 mmol/L 40 mmol KCl per litre; <3.5 mmol/L 40 mmol KCl per litre (senior review as additional potassium needs to be given)". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- Summary principle (verbatim): "assessment of complications of treatment e.g. fluid overload, cerebral oedema or CPM / osmotic demyelination syndrome (as indicated by a deteriorating conscious level) must be undertaken frequently (every 1-2 hours)." [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- Summary principle (verbatim): "prophylactic LMWH is required in most people" and "everyone should be assumed to be at high risk of foot ulceration, particularly if obtunded or uncooperative. The heels should be appropriately protected and daily foot checks undertaken". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- Summary: "The predominant cause of HHS is usually a chest or urinary tract infection". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.

## 2. Mixed DKA-HHS: definition, frequency, mortality, how treatment differs

### Takeaway
Mixed DKA/HHS (DKA criteria plus hyperosmolality) is common (27% of 1,211 US crises in PASQUEL 2020; overlap in "more than one-third" per the consensus) and carries the highest in-hospital mortality (8% vs 5% HHS vs 3% DKA). Both the consensus and JBDS say to treat the ketoacidosis immediately with the DKA insulin dose (FRIII 0.1 U/kg/h started with fluids, no fluids-only lead-in), while respecting HHS osmolality/sodium rate limits. The two societies define "mixed" with different acid-base cut-offs (consensus: BHB ≥3.0 mmol/L, ketonuria ≥2+, pH <7.30 OR bicarbonate <18; JBDS: osmolality ≥320, pH <7.3, bicarbonate <15 AND ketones >3.0).

### Cited Findings
- Consensus definition (in the HHS insulin paragraph): "If significant ketonaemia is present (i.e., β-hydroxybutyrate ≥3.0 mmol/l, ketonuria ≥2+, pH <7.30 or bicarbonate <18 mmol/l), which represents mixed DKA/HHS, then a fixed-rate intravenous insulin infusion should be started at 0.1 U kg−1 h−1". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Consensus on overlap frequency: "Clinical overlap between DKA and HHS has been reported in more than one-third of people with hyperglycaemic crises [50]. Although most people with HHS have an admission pH ≥7.30 and a bicarbonate level ≥18 mmol/l, mild ketonaemia may be present." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. (Same "more than one-third" wording appears in ADA 2009 [verified].)
- JBDS definition (verbatim): "Treat as mixed DKA/HHS if all the following criteria are met: marked hypovolaemia, marked hyperosmolality (osmolality ≥320 mOsm/kg), pH <7.3, bicarbonate <15 mmol/L and blood ketones >3.0 mmol/L. Start a Fixed Rate Intravenous Insulin Infusion (FRIII) and IV fluids immediately and treat according to DKA pathway using 0.1 units/kg/h. IV fluid replacement should aim to achieve a positive balance of 3–6 L during the first 12 h and the remaining replacement of estimated fluid loss during the following 12 h, although complete normalisation of biochemistry may take up to 72 h." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS on mechanism and individualization: mixed states are "likely to reflect a relative insulin deficiency due to beta cell exhaustion as a result of temporary glucotoxicity and excess counter‐regulatory hormone production. These individuals may require a modification of this treatment guideline to take into account which aspect predominates. If a predominant diagnosis is unclear (HHS vs. mixed HHS/DKA), early specialist input should be sought ... However, if ketone concentrations are high (blood ketones >3.0 mmol/L), then the DKA protocol may be appropriate". Also: "In HHS there is usually no significant ketosis/ketonaemia (blood ketones ≤3.0 mmol/L), although a mild acidosis (pH <7.3, bicarbonate >15.0 mmol/L) may accompany those affected by acute kidney injury or severe sepsis." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- PASQUEL 2020 frequency (abstract, verbatim): "There were 1,211 patients who had a first admission with confirmed hyperglycemic crises criteria, 465 (38%) who had isolated DKA, 421 (35%) who had isolated HHS, and 325 (27%) who had combined features of DKA-HHS." Combined DKA-HHS was defined as "a DKA ICD-9 code with bicarbonate ≤18 mEq/L plus effective osmolality ≥300 mOsm/kg". (Two academic hospitals, Atlanta.) [verified] — PASQUEL 2020.
- PASQUEL 2020 mortality: in-hospital mortality isolated DKA 3% (14/465), isolated HHS 5% (20/421), combined DKA-HHS 8% (27/325). Abstract: "subjects with combined DKA-HHS had higher in-hospital mortality compared with subjects with isolated hyperglycemic crises (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.7; 95% CI 1.4, 4.9; P = 0.0019)". Results text gives hazard ratios: "The HRs for DKA-HHS were 2.7 (95% CI 1.4, 5.3) vs. isolated HHS and 1.8 (95% CI 0.9, 3.6) vs. isolated DKA" (the latter not significant). [verified] — PASQUEL 2020.
- Consensus restatement of PASQUEL: "Patients with mixed DKA/HHS have higher hospital mortality than those with HHS (adjusted OR 2.7 [95% CI 1.5, 4.9]) or with DKA (adjusted OR 1.8 [95% CI 0.9, 3.6]), with inpatient mortality rates of 8% for mixed DKA/HHS, 5% for HHS and 3% for DKA [10]." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. (Minor discrepancy in CI and in OR vs HR labeling relative to the primary paper; see Gaps.)
- International mixed-state mortality (consensus, verbatim): "In Japan, inpatient mortality has been reported as 3.3–5.7% in DKA admissions, 13.2% in HHS and 5.3% in mixed DKA/HHS admissions ... In Jamaica, inpatient mortality has been reported as 6.7% in DKA admissions, 20.3% in HHS and 25% in mixed DKA/HHS admissions ... In Nigeria ... 2.7% in DKA, 0.9% in HHS and 3.6% in mixed DKA/HHS". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024 (secondary for the underlying studies).
- Special populations: consensus Table 4, frail/older adults: "Isolated HHS and mixed DKA/HHS occur more frequently than DKA"; COVID-19: "Higher frequency of mixed DKA/HHS especially in older adults." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Hypokalemia is most frequent in the mixed group: K+ <3.5 mEq/L in 62% of combined DKA-HHS vs 54% DKA vs 51% HHS (within 48 h). [verified] — PASQUEL 2020 Table 1.
- Consensus research gap: "no prospective studies have determined the best treatment for HHS and the combination of DKA and HHS." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.

## 3. Mortality and prognosis: HHS vs DKA, predictors

### Takeaway
Mortality figures for HHS vary 10-fold depending on case definition and setting. US administrative data for primary-diagnosis HHS in type 2 diabetes show low and falling inpatient mortality (1.44% in 2008 to 0.77% in 2018), while clinically defined academic cohorts show about 5% (8% if mixed), single-center older cohorts 16-41%, and a 2026 meta-analysis of 63,935 cases found pooled mortality 21.1% overall but 4.8% in North America. Death is driven more by age, comorbidity, infection and AKI than by the glucose or osmolality values themselves; treatment complications (severe hypoglycemia, severe hypokalemia) are independently associated with death.

## 4. VTE and arterial thrombosis in HHS: risk data and prophylaxis recommendations

### Takeaway
JBDS is the only society that gives a firm, explicit rule: "Everyone with HHS should receive prophylactic low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) for the full duration of admission unless contraindicated," with therapeutic anticoagulation only for suspected thrombosis or ACS. The US-led 2024 consensus is softer and covers both DKA and HHS: "unless thrombosis is suspected, prophylactic dose low-molecular-weight heparin should be used." The risk data are mixed: a California cohort found VTE HR 3.0 vs uncomplicated diabetes (HHS) but no excess with DKA, whereas a 4.7-million-admission Taiwanese cohort found no excess VTE with HHS (aOR 1.06). No US guideline gives a specific agent or dose; no RCT exists.

### Cited Findings
- JBDS (verbatim): "Whilst thrombotic complications such as myocardial infarction, stroke or peripheral arterial thrombosis occur more frequently in HHS, it is not known whether or not these can be prevented by prophylaxis with low dose LMWH or anti‐platelet therapy, or if a full therapeutic dose should be used ... People with HHS have an increased risk of arterial and VTE ... Other work has estimated that people with diabetes and hyperosmolality have a risk of VTE similar, or only marginally above those with acute renal failure, acute sepsis or acute connective tissue disease. The risk of venous thromboembolism is greater than in diabetic ketoacidosis ... Other factors, such as hypernatraemia and increasing vasopressin concentrations can promote thrombogenesis by producing changes in haemostatic function consistent with a hypercoagulable state. Everyone with HHS should receive prophylactic low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) for the full duration of admission unless contraindicated. There are no data to recommend that this advice be extended to therapeutic anticoagulation. Full, therapeutic anticoagulation should only be considered in those with suspected thrombosis or acute coronary syndrome." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023 (identical wording in JBDS-HHS PDF 2022). No evidence grade is attached (JBDS uses no grading system in this document).
- JBDS PDF care pathway, 24 h to day 3: "Continue LMWH until day of discharge (consider extended treatment in those deemed very high risk. This may require specialist advice)". Summary: "prophylactic LMWH is required in most people". 0-60 min: "Assess for contraindications of low molecular weight heparin (LMWH)". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022. (Note the summary's "most people" vs the body's "Everyone".)
- Consensus Table 3, Thrombosis (verbatim): Evidence: "Both DKA and HHS, but especially HHS, are thought to be prothrombotic states. There is evidence that clot microstructure may be altered in people with acidosis and dehydration, but this is reversible." Risk: "Although case series highlight the risk of venous and arterial thromboembolism in HHS, a nationwide Taiwanese study examining the risk of venous thromboembolism in people with HHS vs those hospitalised without HHS found similar rates." (No grade; consensus is ungraded.)
- California cohort: Keenan CR, Murin S, White RH. ## 5. Other HHS complications: rhabdomyolysis, AKI, hypernatremia, osmotic demyelination, seizures and focal deficits, foot protection, pancreatitis

### Takeaway
AKI is the dominant organ complication (about half of hyperglycemic crisis admissions; 56% of US HHS admissions in 2013-2022, rising) and the strongest modifiable-looking predictor of death in PASQUEL 2020 (aOR 5.56). Rhabdomyolysis occurred in about 1 in 6 diabetic emergencies in an older Taiwanese ED series and tracked with higher osmolality and higher mortality. ODS is tied to too-rapid correction in relatively hyponatremic patients; prevention is the 3.0-8.0 mOsm/kg/h and 10 mmol/L/24 h sodium limits. Seizures and focal deficits can be presenting features of HHS. JBDS uniquely mandates foot assessment on admission and daily, with heel off-loading.

### Cited Findings
**AKI**
- Consensus Table 3 (verbatim): "Using RIFLE criteria, 50% of adult patients admitted with DKA and HHS have acute kidney injury"; "Acute kidney injury is more common in older adults, those with higher osmolarity and those with higher admission glucose levels"; mitigation: "Acute kidney injury usually resolves with rehydration. Monitoring renal function daily is recommended." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Lin 2026 (Shock; full citation in Section 3): AKI in 56.1% of 80,557 US HHS hospitalizations, increasing from 45.9% (2013) to 63.6% (2022); AKI associated with mortality 4.6% vs 1.3%, dialysis 1.3% vs 0.1%. [verified] — PubMed abstract.
- PASQUEL 2020: AKI aOR for death 5.56 (95% CI 2.69-11.50). [verified]
- 2026 meta-analysis: AKI reported as a complication in 7.6% (pooled, heterogeneous definitions). [verified] — French 2026 abstract. (Conflict with the 50-56% figures reflects definition/ascertainment; see Gaps.)
- JBDS HDU trigger: "Serum creatinine >200 μmol/L and/or acute kidney injury" (200 µmol/L ≈ 2.3 mg/dL by arithmetic). JBDS: "if acute kidney injury is present, hyperkalaemia may be present owing to reduced clearance and therefore closer monitoring is required." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.

**Rhabdomyolysis**
- Wang LM, Tsai ST, Ho LT, et al. Rhabdomyolysis in diabetic emergencies. *Diabetes Res Clin Pract.* 1994;26(3):209-214. doi:10.1016/0168-8227(94)90062-0. PMID 7736901. Abstract: "about 44 cases of RM in 265 diabetic emergencies (including DKA or hyperosmolar, or both)" (about 17%), defined as CK >1000 IU/L without MI, stroke or ESRD; RM patients had higher BUN (83.3 vs 58.8 mg/dL), creatinine (4.45 vs 2.97 mg/dL) and osmolarity (386.5 vs 351.6 mOsm/kg); 1-week mortality with vs without RM: DKA 38.5% vs 9.7%, HHNK 35.5% vs 26.7%. [verified] — PubMed abstract (abstract truncated at 250 words).
- Case-level: HHS-related rhabdomyolysis with CK 37,710 U/L requiring temporary hemodialysis (Chen IW, Lin CW. *Medicine (Baltimore).* 2018;97(50):e13647. doi:10.1097/MD.0000000000013647. PMID 30558060). [verified] — PubMed abstract. PMID 15887451). [secondary]

**Hypernatremia and sodium**
- ADA 2009: "An increased or even normal serum sodium concentration in the presence of hyperglycemia indicates a rather profound degree of free water loss." [verified] — ADA 2009.
- JBDS HDU trigger "Sodium >160 mmol/L"; hypernatremia promotes thrombogenesis (see Section 4); sodium rise during glucose fall is expected (2.4 mmol/L per 5.5 mmol/L glucose fall) and "is only a concern if the osmolality is not declining concurrently." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS PDF 12-24 h: "Do not expect biochemistry to have normalised by 24 hrs (sodium and osmolality are likely to be raised)". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.

**Osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS)**
- Consensus Table 3 (verbatim): "Previously known as central pontine myelinolysis, osmotic demyelination syndrome can occur with rapid correction of hyponatraemia. The incidence is unclear." Risk: "The risk is specifically associated with rapid correction of hyponatraemia. May complicate treatment of adults with HHS where hyperosmolar patients may be relatively hyponatraemic." Mitigation: "In patients with HHS, the fall in serum osmolarity should be corrected with 0.9% saline solution. The fall in serum osmolality should be between 3.0 and 8.0 mOsm kg−1 h−1." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- JBDS: cerebral oedema and ODS "are uncommon but can be seen as a complication of the rapid changes in osmolality during treatment of HHS"; detect via "deteriorating conscious level", assessed "every 1-2 hours". [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023 / PDF 2022.

**Seizures, focal deficits, cognition**
- ADA 2009: "Mental status can vary from full alertness to profound lethargy or coma, with the latter more frequent in HHS. Focal neurologic signs (hemianopia and hemiparesis) and seizures (focal or generalized) may also be features of HHS". [verified] — ADA 2009.
- JBDS: "Acute cognitive impairment may not be necessarily present ... Alterations in cognitive status are more common when the osmolality rises >330 mOsm/kg. HHS can have marked effects on cerebral function and be associated with transient changes in cognitive performance and also with longer‐term effects." Causes listed include "cerebral oedema in severe cases ... acute changes in osmolality, dehydration, infection/sepsis, hypoglycaemia during treatment or kidney injury." Recommends "A daily assessment of cognition during admission with a comparison to the pre‐morbid state". [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- 2026 case + literature review: aphasic non-convulsive status epilepticus complicating HHS, treated with insulin and levetiracetam (Bokhari O, Alqhtani S, Alqahtani L, et al. Aphasic status epilepticus complicating hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state. *Pract Neurol.* 2026;26(5):479-483. doi:10.1136/pn-2025-004936. PMID 41344907). [verified] — PubMed abstract.
- Nonketotic hyperglycemia seizures (often focal, epilepsia partialis continua) respond mainly to correcting hyperglycemia/hyperosmolality; phenytoin is classically described as ineffective. [recalled] Hyperglycemic hemichorea/diabetic striatopathy is another HHS-associated movement disorder (2026 case report PMID 42571486, title only seen). [recalled/title only]

**Foot protection (JBDS-specific)**
- JBDS (verbatim): "People with HHS are at high risk of pressure‐related foot ulceration. An initial foot assessment should be undertaken on admission and daily during admission. Heel protectors and an appropriate mattress should be provided for those with immobility, neuropathy, peripheral vascular disease or lower limb deformity. If the individuals are too confused or drowsy to cooperate with the assessment of sensation assume they are at high risk." [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023. The US consensus does not mention foot protection. [verified absence in text read]

**Pancreatitis (new 2026 data)**
- Cho E, Lee JH, You GR, et al. Incidence and predictors of acute pancreatitis in patients with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS): a multicenter retrospective study. *Endocr Pract.* 2026;32(9):1369-1377. doi:10.1016/j.eprac.2026.04.015. PMID 42061649. PMCID PMC13317179. Korea, 406 patients: acute pancreatitis 14.8% overall, "17.5% vs 6.8%" DKA vs HHS; predictors BMI, abdominal pain, shock, GGT, hypertriglyceridemia; not associated with LOS or mortality. [verified] — PubMed abstract.
- Consensus: "Nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain are common in DKA (>50%) but are uncommon in HHS". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.

## 6. Cerebral edema: adults (brief) and children (brief, exact)

### Takeaway
In adults, clinically apparent cerebral edema is rare (consensus: thought to occur in <0.1% of DKA/HHS events) but has about 30% mortality; prevention in HHS is slow osmolality correction, and treatment is mannitol plus airway support. In children with DKA, cerebral injury occurs in roughly 0.3-1% (ADA 2009: 0.3-1.0%; PECARN FLUID: clinically apparent brain injury 0.9%), and Glaser 2001 linked it to low pCO2, high BUN and bicarbonate therapy (RR 4.2). PECARN FLUID (n = 1,389 episodes) found no effect of fluid rate or saline concentration on neurologic outcomes. ISPAD 2022 dosing (mannitol 0.5-1 g/kg; 3% saline 2.5-5 mL/kg) could not be read in the primary this session; flag for the pediatric cross-checker.

### Cited Findings
**Adults**
- Consensus Table 3 (verbatim): "Cerebral oedema is rare in adults. The underlying cause is not fully understood but may reflect osmotic changes, hypoperfusion and/or inflammatory responses. In adult patients with HHS and DKA, rapid shifts in osmolarity may also be associated with cerebral oedema thought to occur in <0.1% of events." Risk: "Cerebral oedema is a serious complication with a reported mortality of ∼30% compared with those without oedema. Cerebral oedema may be subclinical and visible only on imaging studies." Mitigation: "Recognising potential risk factors and being alerted to changes in mental status is advised, with a low threshold for brain imaging. Mannitol infusion and mechanical ventilation are suggested for treatment of cerebral oedema. In adults with HHS, a slow rate for correction of hyperosmolarity is indicated." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. (No mannitol dose given for adults.)
- Consensus HHS glucose limit is explicitly framed: decline "should not exceed 5–6.7 mmol l−1 h−1 (90–120 mg dl−1 h−1) to prevent cerebral oedema." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- JBDS: osmolality fall >8.0 mOsm/kg/h "(or > 3 mOsm/kg/hr in those at high risk of developing cerebral oedema)" should prompt reducing fluids and/or insulin. [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- ADA 2009: cerebral edema "is extremely rare in adult patients during treatment of DKA"; prevention "might include avoidance of excessive hydration and rapid reduction of plasma osmolarity, a gradual decrease in serum glucose, and maintenance of serum glucose between 250–300 mg/dl until the patient's serum osmolality is normalized and mental status is improved. Manitol infusion and mechanical ventilation are suggested". Signs: "headache, gradual deterioration in level of consciousness, seizures, sphincter incontinence, pupillary changes, papilledema, bradycardia, elevation in blood pressure, and respiratory arrest." [verified] — ADA 2009.
- Adult source behind "<0.1%" likely: Siwakoti K, Giri S, Kadaria D. Cerebral edema among adults with diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperglycemic hyperosmolar syndrome: incidence, characteristics, and outcomes. *J Diabetes.* 2017;9(2):208-209. doi:10.1111/1753-0407.12448. PMID 27423142 (letter; PubMed abstract is garbled and gives no numbers). [verified metadata only; numbers not confirmed]
- Adult case reports: 38-year-old with DKA developed pupil dilation 11 h after presentation despite slow correction and died of herniation (Namatame K, Igarashi Y, Nakae R, et al. *Acute Med Surg.* 2023;10(1):e860. doi:10.1002/ams2.860. PMID 37346084); 47-year-old with severe DKA died within 72 h despite hypertonic therapy (Rosa-Santiago SM, et al. *Cureus.* 2026;18(4):e107254. PMID 42153053). [verified] — PubMed abstracts.

**Children (brief)**
- ADA 2009: "Cerebral edema, which occurs in ∼0.3–1.0% of DKA episodes in children ... is associated with a mortality rate of 20–40% and accounts for 57–87% of all DKA deaths in children". [verified] — ADA 2009 (secondary for pediatric data).
- Glaser N, Barnett P, McCaslin I, et al. Risk factors for cerebral edema in children with diabetic ketoacidosis. *N Engl J Med.* 2001;344(4):264-269. doi:10.1056/NEJM200101253440404. [verified] — PubMed abstract.

NCT00629707. [verified] — PubMed abstract.

## 7. Treatment complications: hypoglycemia, hypokalemia, hyperchloremic acidosis, fluid overload, arrhythmia, rebound hyperglycemia, recurrence

### Takeaway
The consensus Table 3 figures are confirmed in their sources with one important error: hypoglycemia (<70 mg/dL) 16-28% (16% = PASQUEL 2020 DKA; 27.6% = UK 2014 DKA audit), severe hypoglycemia (<40 mg/dL) 2%, and hypokalemia (<3.5 mmol/L) about 55% in DKA and 51% in HHS are all supported. But the consensus statement that severe hypokalemia (<2.5 mmol/L) "occurs in 16% of people with DKA and 9% of people with HHS" appears to reproduce PASQUEL's patient COUNTS (16 and 9) as percentages; PASQUEL Table 1 gives 3% (16/465) and 2% (9/421). Both severe hypoglycemia (aOR 4.8) and severe hypokalemia (aOR 4.9) are independently associated with in-hospital death.

### Cited Findings
**Hypoglycemia**
- Consensus Table 3 (verbatim): "Hypoglycaemia is a common complication encountered in the treatment of DKA. In studies of DKA treatment, the risk of hypoglycaemia (<3.9 mmol/l [70 mg/dl]) varied between 16% and 28%, with severe hypoglycaemia (<2.2 mmol/l [40 mg/dl]) occurring in 2% of cases." Risk: "Hypoglycaemia (<2.2 mmol/l [40 mg/dl]) during treatment was associated with a 4.8-fold increase in mortality (adjusted OR 4 [95% CI 1.4, 16.8])." Mitigation: "Frequent blood glucose monitoring (every 1–2 h) is mandatory to recognise hypoglycaemia. When glucose levels are reduced to <13.9 mmol/l (250 mg/dl), it is advised to reduce the insulin infusion rate to 0.05 U kg−1 h−1 and replacement fluids should be modified to contain 5–10% dextrose to prevent hypoglycaemia." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. (Internal typo: "4.8-fold" vs "adjusted OR 4"; primary says aOR 4.8.)
- PASQUEL 2020 Table 1 (within 48 h): glucose <70 mg/dL: DKA 16% (74), HHS 12% (52), combined 15% (49); <40 mg/dL: 2% in each group (7, 9, 8). Abstract: "In all groups, hypoglycemia (<40 mg/dL) during treatment was associated with a 4.8-fold increase in mortality (aOR 4.8; 95% CI 1.4, 16.8)." [verified] — PASQUEL 2020.
- UK national DKA survey 2014: "27.6% of patients developing hypoglycaemia and 55% reported as having hypokalaemia" (283 forms, 72 hospitals). Dhatariya KK, Nunney I, Higgins K, Sampson MJ, Iceton G. National survey of the management of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) in the UK in 2014. *Diabet Med.* 2016;33(2):252-260. doi:10.1111/dme.12875. PMID 26286235. [verified] — PubMed abstract. (Likely source of the "28%" upper bound and possibly the "~55% of DKA".)
- ADA 2009: "many patients with DKA who develop hypoglycemia during treatment do not experience adrenergic manifestations of sweating, nervousness, fatigue, hunger, and tachycardia." [verified] — ADA 2009.
- JBDS: early insulin in HHS carries "higher risk of hypokalaemia and hypoglycaemia"; keep glucose 10-15 mmol/L in first 24 h; dextrose when <14 mmol/L. [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023/PDF 2022.

**Hypokalemia and arrhythmia**
- Consensus Table 3 (verbatim): "Hypokalaemia is a common complication owing to intracellular shift of potassium following insulin treatment. Hypokalaemia (<3.5 mmol/l) occurred in ∼55% of DKA and 51% of HHS patients. Severe hypokalaemia <2.5 mmol/l occurs in 16% of people with DKA and 9% of people with HHS." Risk: "Severe hypokalaemia ≤2.5 mmol/l was associated with increased inpatient mortality (adjusted OR 4.9 [95% CI 1.3, 18.8])." Mitigation: "Potassium should be carefully monitored every 4 h during treatment. Potassium replacement should be added to fluid resuscitation." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- PASQUEL 2020 Table 1: K+ <3.5 mEq/L DKA 54% (252), HHS 51% (208), combined 62% (196); K+ ≤2.5 mEq/L DKA 3% (16), HHS 2% (9), combined 3% (9). Abstract: "Hypokalemia ≤3.5 mEq/L was frequent (55%). Severe hypokalemia (≤2.5 mEq/L) was associated with increased inpatient mortality (aOR 4.9; 95% CI 1.3, 18.8; P = 0.02)." [verified] — PASQUEL 2020. => consensus "16%/9%" appears to be a counts-for-percent transcription error (inference, high confidence).
- Consensus body: "Within 48 h of admission, potassium levels typically decline by 1–2 mmol/l during treatment of DKA, HHS and mixed DKA/HHS"; "insulin therapy should be delayed until the potassium level increases to >3.5 mmol/l to avoid life-threatening arrhythmias and respiratory muscle weakness"; "we recommend measuring serum potassium 2 h after starting insulin administration and every 4 h thereafter until the resolution of DKA"; "Use of too low or too high doses of potassium compared with the recommended potassium replacement protocols in the management of DKA has been associated with longer hospital stays". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Consensus research section: "Dhatariya et al reported that despite potassium replacement following protocol in the UK, 67% of patients had a potassium level <4 mmol/l within 24 h of presentation ... Similar findings were reported in Canada and the USA, where approximately 50% of patients developed hypokalaemia (<3.5 mmol/l) despite 91% of them receiving potassium replacement." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- ADA 2009: low K on admission means "severe total-body potassium deficiency and require careful cardiac monitoring and more vigorous potassium replacement because treatment lowers potassium further and can provoke cardiac dysrhythmia"; insulin delayed until K >3.3 mEq/L (2009 threshold; now >3.5 mmol/L in 2024). [verified] — ADA 2009. (Change: 3.3 → 3.5.)
- JBDS HHS: K <3.5 or ≥6 on admission triggers HDU discussion; hypokalaemia/hyperkalaemia "are less common in HHS than DKA but monitoring and replacement are essential". [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023/PDF 2022.

**Hyperchloremic (normal anion gap) acidosis**
- Consensus Table 3 (verbatim): "Hyperchloraemic non-anion gap acidosis may be seen during the recovery phase of DKA, but the risk is unknown. It is likely to be caused by loss of keto-anions, which are metabolised to bicarbonate, and excess fluid infusion of chloride-containing fluids during treatment." Risk: "Observed during the recovery phase of DKA, it is self-limiting with few clinical consequences." Mitigation: "There is some evidence that hyperchloraemic acidosis occurs less frequently with balanced electrolyte solutions and when slower saline infusion is administered." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Consensus fluids: 0.9% saline in large volumes "may be associated with hyperchloraemic normal anion gap metabolic acidosis and prolonged length of ICU and hospital stay"; balanced crystalloids give "faster DKA resolution ... shorter hospital length of stay and less frequent development of hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis." The anion gap "should not be used as a criterion" for resolution because of this. [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. (Fluid trial details are covered in sibling `fluids_electrolytes_bicarbonate.md`.)

**Fluid overload, pulmonary edema, ARDS**
- Consensus: in older adults, heart failure or ESKD on dialysis, "the use of a standard fluid replacement protocol may be associated with treatment-related complications, including volume overload, need for mechanical ventilation and longer length of stay"; use "smaller boluses ... (e.g., 250 ml boluses)". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- JBDS: "Caution is needed, particularly in the elderly, where too rapid rehydration may precipitate heart failure but insufficient may fail to reverse acute kidney injury"; fluid overload listed among harms to prevent and assessed every 1-2 h. [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- 2026 meta-analysis: pulmonary oedema 4.8% of HHS cases; acute coronary syndrome 3.9%. [verified] — French 2026 abstract.
- Lin 2026: mechanical ventilation 4.2% (AKI) vs 2.0% (no AKI) in US HHS. [verified]
- ARDS/noncardiogenic pulmonary edema during DKA treatment is a rare, classically described complication attributed to reduced colloid osmotic pressure with crystalloid loading. [recalled; no source read this session]

**Rebound hyperglycemia and recurrence**
- Consensus: "To prevent the recurrence of hyperglycaemia or ketoacidosis during the transition period to subcutaneous insulin, it is important to allow an overlap of 1–2 h between the administration of subcutaneous insulin and the discontinuation of intravenous insulin"; co-administration of basal insulin 0.15-0.3 U/kg during the infusion "reduces time to DKA resolution, duration of insulin infusion and length of hospital stay and prevents rebound hyperglycaemia, all without increased risk of hypoglycaemia". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. JBDS sample chart: "ALWAYS continue subcutaneous intermediate* or basal insulin". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- Recurrence/readmission (consensus): "In US nationwide studies, up to 22% of people admitted with DKA had at least one readmission within 30 days or the same calendar year. Among those readmitted within 30 days, 40.8% represented recurrent DKA episodes, with approximately 50% being readmitted within 2 weeks"; "Compared with patients with a single DKA admission, those with 2–5 admissions have a threefold higher risk of death, while those with six or more admissions have a sixfold higher risk of death". [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024. HHS-specific readmission data were not found. [gap]

## 8. Monitoring schedule during treatment (what each society says)

### Takeaway
US consensus: capillary glucose every 1-2 h; electrolytes, phosphate, creatinine, BHB and venous pH every 4 h; potassium 2 h after insulin starts then every 4 h; serum osmolality every 4 h in HHS; ECG at presentation; renal function daily. JBDS HHS is more intensive: glucose, Na, K, urea and calculated osmolality hourly for the first 6 h, then 2-hourly to 12 h, then 4-hourly if improving; ketones hourly until resolution (PDF); GCS every 1-2 h; hourly pulse, BP, SpO2, urine output via catheter; continuous pulse oximetry and consider cardiac monitoring; daily cognition and foot checks.

### Cited Findings
- Consensus (verbatim): "Capillary blood glucose testing should be performed during treatment every 1–2 h using a hospital-calibrated glucose meter, and blood should be drawn every 4 h for determination of electrolytes, phosphate, creatinine, β-hydroxybutyrate and venous pH until resolution of DKA. In patients with HHS, in addition to measuring glucose, creatinine and electrolytes, serum osmolarity should be measured every 4 h." [verified] — CONSENSUS 2024.
- Consensus potassium: "measuring serum potassium 2 h after starting insulin administration and every 4 h thereafter until the resolution of DKA." [verified]
- Consensus ECG: "an electrocardiogram should be performed to assess for evidence of biochemically induced repolarisation abnormalities, such as peaked T waves from hyperkalaemia and ischaemia." [verified] (Given at initial evaluation; no repeat-ECG schedule stated.)
- Consensus AKI: "Monitoring renal function daily is recommended." Cerebral oedema: "being alerted to changes in mental status is advised, with a low threshold for brain imaging." [verified]
- JBDS PDF monitoring (verbatim): "Establish monitoring regime appropriate to the individual – generally hourly blood glucose, Na+, K+, urea and calculated osmolality [(2xNa+) + glucose + urea] for the first 6 hours, then 2 hourly osmolality if the response is satisfactory (i.e. a fall of 3.0-8.0 mOsm/kg/hr). • Chart measured or calculated osmolality / glucose / sodium • Continuous pulse oximetry • Consider continuous cardiac monitoring". Summary: "measure or calculate osmolality ... every hour for the first 6 hours, then 2 hourly for the next 6 hours". 12-24 h: "Continue charting BG hourly. Measurement of sodium and calculated osmolality can be reduced to 4 hourly if improvement maintained (if not, continue 2 hourly)". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- JBDS PDF ketones: "Check ketone concentrations hourly until HHS resolution". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- JBDS PDF sample chart: "Insert catheter and monitor fluid balance (input/output); Check pulse, BP, saturations, urine output and CBG hourly. Check GCS every 1-2hrs". "Monitor vital signs and chart National Early Warning Score (NEWS)"; "Maintain accurate fluid balance chart (minimum urine output 0.5 ml/kg/hr)". 24 h-day 3: "Daily urea and electrolytes"; "Daily foot checks". [verified] — JBDS-HHS PDF 2022.
- JBDS complication checks: "assessment of complications of treatment e.g. fluid overload, cerebral oedema or CPM / osmotic demyelination syndrome (as indicated by a deteriorating conscious level) must be undertaken frequently (every 1-2 hours)." [verified]
- JBDS cognition: "A daily assessment of cognition during admission with a comparison to the pre‐morbid state". [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- JBDS point-of-care: venous gases are adequate ("Unless it is necessary to also measure oxygen saturation, venous rather than arterial samples are sufficient"); laboratory osmolality "may run once a day", so use calculated or blood-gas osmolality for rapid repeats. [verified] — JBDS-HHS 2023.
- ADA 2009 (superseded) also required "frequent blood glucose monitoring (every 1–2 h)". [verified]
- Phosphate/magnesium: consensus includes phosphate in the 4-hourly panel; JBDS gives no schedule. [verified]

## 9. Differential diagnosis: conditions that mimic or overlap with DKA/HHS (one-table worth)

### Takeaway
Ketoacidosis without marked hyperglycemia (alcoholic, starvation, pregnancy/hyperemesis, SGLT2-inhibitor euglycemic DKA) and other high-anion-gap acidoses (lactic, salicylate, methanol, ethylene glycol, renal failure) are the main mimics. Glucose, BHB, lactate, osmolar gap, salicylate level and history separate them. Stupor without effective osmolality ≥320 should prompt a search for another cause.

### Cited Findings
| Condition | Distinguishing facts | Source/tag |
|---|---|---|
| Starvation ketosis | History of intake "<2090 kJ/day (500 kcal/day)"; glucose mildly elevated "(rarely >200 mg/dl)" to low; "serum bicarbonate concentration in starvation ketosis is usually not <18 mEq/l" | CONSENSUS 2024 [verified]; ADA 2009 [verified] |
| Alcoholic ketoacidosis | "chronic ethanol use with a recent binge culminating in vomiting and acute starvation may develop ketoacidosis with or without hyperglycaemia"; can cause "profound acidosis"; glucose rarely >200 mg/dL | CONSENSUS 2024 [verified]; ADA 2009 [verified] |
| Ketosis of pregnancy / hyperemesis | "vomiting of hyperemesis gravidarum leads to excess counterregulatory hormone concentrations, also predisposing to ketone formation"; euglycemic DKA in pregnancy | CONSENSUS 2024 [verified] |
| Euglycemic DKA (SGLT2 inhibitors) | "May present with near-normal glucose concentrations or euglycaemic DKA (glucose <11.1 mmol/l [200 mg/dl])"; give dextrose with saline from the start; stop SGLT2i on admission (covered in depth by another researcher) | CONSENSUS 2024 Table 4 [verified] |
| Lactic acidosis | "plasma lactate should be measured on admission" because lactic acidosis is more common in diabetes and in volume contraction; JBDS: lactate "can indicate lactic acidosis related to sepsis" | ADA 2009 [verified]; JBDS-HHS 2023 [verified] |
| Salicylate, methanol, ethylene glycol, paraldehyde | High-AG acidosis; "Measurement of serum salicylate and blood methanol level may be helpful. Ethylene glycol (antifreeze) is suggested by the presence of calcium oxalate and hippurate crystals in the urine ... these intoxicants ... can produce an osmolar gap in addition to the anion gap acidosis" | ADA 2009 [verified] |
| Renal failure | Listed among high-AG acidoses to distinguish ("acute chronic renal failure") | ADA 2009 [verified] |
| Other cause of coma | "The occurrence of stupor or coma in a diabetic patient in the absence of definitive elevation of effective osmolality (≥320 mOsm/kg) demands immediate consideration of other causes of mental status change." | ADA 2009 [verified] |

- Additional distinguishing points not verified this session: salicylate toxicity typically shows mixed respiratory alkalosis plus AG metabolic acidosis with tinnitus; metformin-associated lactic acidosis in renal failure; toxic alcohols give a raised osmolar gap early and AG later. [recalled]
- Consensus: "Mixed acid-base disorders are present in about one-third" (per sibling file) and the anion gap can be normal-AG during recovery (see Section 7). [secondary via sibling]

## 10. 2024-2026 literature on HHS (searched PubMed 2024-2026, n = 182 hits for "hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state"; ClinicalTrials.gov interventional search)

### Takeaway

## Gaps and conflicts

**Disagreements between JBDS and the US-led 2024 consensus (for the handout's "HHS specifics")**
1. Insulin timing: JBDS = fluids alone first; FRIII 0.05 U/kg/h only when glucose (body text) or osmolality (abstract) stops falling, unless ketones >3.0 mmol/L (2023 article) or >1.0 mmol/L with pH >7.3 (2022 PDF pathway). Consensus = FRIII 0.05 U/kg/h for HHS without significant ketonemia, with fluids-first mentioned only as "Some have recommended". The consensus text does not say "start insulin immediately"; its Fig. 4 (image) may show sequencing but could not be read.
2. Dextrose trigger: JBDS <14 mmol/L (about 250 mg/dL), 5% or 10% at 125 mL/h, continue saline. Consensus text: <13.9 mmol/L (250 mg/dL) (stated for DKA and in Table 3 for "DKA and HHS"). ADA 2009 (superseded): 300 mg/dL in HHS with insulin 0.02-0.05 U/kg/h and target 250-300 mg/dL. Whether the 2024 consensus Fig. 4 HHS arm uses 300 mg/dL (16.7 mmol/L) could NOT be confirmed (image only). Recommend the writer not state "300 mg/dL" as current US guidance without seeing Fig. 4.
3. Glucose target during first 24 h: JBDS 10-15 mmol/L (180-270 mg/dL); consensus has no HHS-specific target in text (DKA: about 200 mg/dL); ADA 2009: 250-300 mg/dL.
4. Glucose fall limit: JBDS "not more than 5 mmol/h" (also "between 4.0 and 6.0 mmol/hr" in the PDF); consensus "should not exceed 5–6.7 mmol l−1 h−1 (90–120 mg dl−1 h−1)". 6.7 mmol/L/h = 120 mg/dL/h; 5 mmol/L/h = 90 mg/dL/h.
5. Sodium correction factor: consensus 1.6 mmol/L per 5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) glucose fall; JBDS 2.4 mmol/L per 5.5 mmol/L.
6. Fluid type: consensus allows balanced crystalloid or 0.9% saline equally; JBDS HHS names 0.9% saline as the principal fluid.
7. Resolution: glucose <13.9 mmol/L (250 mg/dL) and UO >0.5 mL/kg/h and cognition "improved" (consensus) vs glucose <15 mmol/L (270 mg/dL) and UO ≥0.5 mL/kg/h and cognition "back to the pre‐morbid state" (JBDS).
8. Mixed DKA/HHS definition: consensus BHB ≥3.0 OR ketonuria ≥2+ OR pH <7.30 OR HCO3 <18; JBDS requires all of osmolality ≥320, pH <7.3, HCO3 <15, ketones >3.0.
9. Potassium: consensus 20-30 mmol/L once K <5.0 (target 4-5); JBDS HHS 40 mmol/L for K 3.5-5.5, none if >5.5, none in the first 1-h bag.
10. VTE: JBDS "Everyone ... for the full duration of admission unless contraindicated"; consensus "unless thrombosis is suspected, prophylactic dose low-molecular-weight heparin should be used" (DKA and HHS); JBDS PDF summary says "most people". Taiwan data (aOR 1.06) vs California data (HR 3.0 vs uncomplicated diabetes) conflict.
11. Foot protection and daily cognition checks: JBDS only.
12. Austrian 2026 update treats HHS like DKA (insulin bolus), diverging from both.

**Errors or inconsistencies found in sources**
- Consensus Table 3 severe hypokalemia "16% of people with DKA and 9% of people with HHS" vs PASQUEL Table 1 3% (16/465) and 2% (9/421): likely counts misreported as percentages.
- Consensus Table 3 hypoglycemia: "4.8-fold increase in mortality (adjusted OR 4 [95% CI 1.4, 16.8])": primary aOR is 4.8 (4.81).
- Consensus mixed vs HHS "adjusted OR 2.7 [95% CI 1.5, 4.9]" vs PASQUEL Results "HR 2.7 (95% CI 1.4, 5.3) vs isolated HHS" and abstract "aOR 2.7; 95% CI 1.4, 4.9" vs isolated crises overall.
- JBDS abstract (osmolality stops falling) vs body (glucose stops falling) as the insulin trigger.
- JBDS PDF "0.25 ml/kg/hr" cautious fluid rate for <50 kg or heart/renal disease (attributed to NICE) looks implausibly low (17.5 mL/h for 70 kg) and may be a typo for a different unit; not verified against NICE.

**Values not confirmed this session**
- 2024 consensus Fig. 4 (treatment pathway) content, including any HHS dextrose threshold, insulin titration steps, and bolus instructions (image only in both PMC copies).
- ISPAD 2022 primary text: cerebral injury incidence, mortality, timing, and mannitol/hypertonic saline doses and infusion times (Wiley 403; no PMC copy). Values given are [recalled]/[secondary]; pediatric cross-checker must verify.
- Siwakoti 2017 adult cerebral edema incidence numbers (letter; abstract garbled).
- Consensus ref 34 (30-day and 1-year post-crisis mortality 0.1%/2.0% and 0.9%/9.5%) primary source not retrieved.
- PASQUEL 2020 full text was read via a summarizing fetch (WebFetch), not raw text; numbers are consistent with the abstract but table values should be spot-checked if printed.
- No HHS-specific data on readmission/recurrence rates, ARDS incidence, routine CK monitoring, or arrhythmia incidence were found.
- Mortality from hypokalemia/hypoglycemia is association (aOR) data from one two-center retrospective cohort (PASQUEL 2020); causality not established.

**Recent changes / superseded items**
- ADA 2009 consensus (300 mg/dL dextrose trigger for HHS; insulin 0.02-0.05 U/kg/h after that point; same 0.1 U/kg/h starting dose as DKA; K threshold 3.3 mEq/L; effective osmolality >320) is superseded by the 2024 consensus (HHS FRIII 0.05 U/kg/h; K threshold 3.5 mmol/L; effective osmolality >300 or total >320; rate limits adopted from JBDS).
- No change to JBDS HHS guidance since the February 2022 PDF / 2023 Diabet Med article found as of 2026-11-22.
- Mortality estimates are moving: US administrative HHS mortality fell to 0.77% (2018), but AKI-coded HHS admissions are rising (63.6% in 2022), and the 2026 meta-analysis reports 21.1% pooled mortality with large regional variation; any single "HHS mortality" number in the handout should state the setting and definition.
