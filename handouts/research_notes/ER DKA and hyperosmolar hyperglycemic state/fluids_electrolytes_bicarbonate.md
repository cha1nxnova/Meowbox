## 1. Guideline recommendations on fluids (choice, first-hour volume, later rates, 0.45% saline, dextrose, two-bag)

### Takeaway
The 2024 ADA/EASD/JBDS/AACE/DTS consensus allows either 0.9% saline or balanced crystalloid ("determined by local availability, cost and resources") at 500-1000 mL/h for the first 2-4 h in adults without cardiac or renal compromise, correcting deficits over 24-48 h, adding 5-10% dextrose once glucose is <250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L) in DKA, with 250 mL boluses for older adults, heart failure and dialysis patients. In HHS it reserves 0.45% saline for osmolality not falling despite positive fluid balance and caps falls in glucose (90-120 mg/dL/h), sodium (10 mmol/L per 24 h) and osmolality (3.0-8.0 mOsm/kg/h). It gives no formal grade to these statements.

## 2. Balanced crystalloid vs 0.9% saline: trial and meta-analysis evidence (adults)

### Takeaway
Adult evidence is a handful of small or secondary-analysis trials. The biochemistry is consistent: balanced fluids give lower chloride and higher bicarbonate (about +1.5 to +2.5 mmol/L at about 12 h). Clinical outcomes are not consistent. The best US ED data (the SMART/SALT-ED DKA subgroup, n=172) found faster resolution (median 13.0 vs 16.9 h; aHR 1.68). SCOPE-DKA (ICU, n=90) found more resolution at 24 h (69% vs 36%) but not at its 48 h primary endpoint. BRISK-ED (ED pilot, n=52) and the 2025 Tunisian RCT (ICU, n=88) found no faster resolution. Meta-analyses split by method: Alghamdi 2022 (8 RCTs, GRADE moderate) found saline about 3.5 h slower to resolution and about 0.9 d longer in hospital, but the 2024-2026 meta-analyses that added the newer neutral trials found no significant difference in time to resolution, AKI or LOS. The pivotal Phase 3 BEST-DKA trial (Plasma-Lyte 148 vs saline, about 20 Australian hospitals, 547 enrolled, primary completion July 2026) had no published results I could find as of 2026-11-22. No AKI or mortality signal favors either fluid.

## 3. Potassium: thresholds, doses, maximum rates, monitoring (US consensus vs JBDS)

### Takeaway
2024 US/international consensus: start potassium once serum K is <5.0 mmol/L, target 4-5 mmol/L, give 20-30 mmol K per liter of IV fluid. If K is <3.5 mmol/L, give K at 10 mmol/h and delay insulin until K is >3.5. Check K 2 h after insulin starts, then every 4 h. JBDS (UK) uses a 5.5 mmol/L ceiling and 40 mmol/L premixed bags; for K <3.5 it calls for senior review and more concentrated K (central line in HDU for HHS), and it does not say to delay insulin. The 2009 ADA consensus (superseded) used 3.3 and 5.0-5.2 mEq/L cut-points. FDA KCl labeling caps routine rates at 10 mEq/h (200 mEq/24 h) when K >2.5, allowing up to 40 mEq/h (400 mEq/24 h) only for K <2.0 with ECG changes or paralysis, under continuous ECG monitoring. Despite protocols, hypokalemia occurs in about half of US patients.

### Cited Findings
**2024 consensus (verbatim; Diabetes Care units confirmed as mmol):**
- "Despite experiencing a total-body potassium depletion of 3–6 mmol/kg... most patients with DKA present with normal or high serum potassium levels... Within 48 h of admission, potassium levels typically decline by 1–2 mmol/l during treatment of DKA, HHS and mixed DKA/HHS. To prevent hypokalaemia, potassium replacement should be started after serum levels fall below 5.0 mmol/l to maintain a potassium level of 4–5 mmol/l. For most patients with DKA, 20–30 mmol of potassium per litre of intravenous fluid is sufficient to maintain a serum potassium concentration within the target range. Low-normal or low potassium levels (<3.5 mmol/l) are present on admission in 5–10% of patients with DKA; in such cases, potassium replacement should begin at a rate of 10 mmol/h, and insulin therapy should be delayed until the potassium level increases to >3.5 mmol/l to avoid life-threatening arrhythmias and respiratory muscle weakness. Severe hypokalaemia ≤2.5 mmol/l during treatment of DKA and HHS has been reported to be associated with a threefold increase in mortality. To avoid hypokalaemia, we recommend measuring serum potassium 2 h after starting insulin administration and every 4 h thereafter until the resolution of DKA. Use of too low or too high doses of potassium compared with the recommended potassium replacement protocols in the management of DKA has been associated with longer hospital stays." No grade attached. [verified] — [PMC11343900](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11343900/); Diabetes Care units ("mmol/L", "10 mmol/h") confirmed in [PMC11272983](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11272983/)
- Table 3 (complications): "Hypokalaemia (<3.5 mmol/l) occurred in ∼55% of DKA and 51% of HHS patients. Severe hypokalaemia <2.5 mmol/l occurs in 16% of people with DKA and 9% of people with HHS." [verified] — [PMC11343900](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11343900/)

## 4. Phosphate

### Takeaway
No guideline recommends routine phosphate. The 2024 consensus reserves it for muscle weakness, respiratory or cardiac compromise "with the phosphate <1.0 mmol/L" (about 3.1 mg/dL), giving 20-30 mmol potassium phosphate in replacement fluids, with the same approach in HHS. The 2009 ADA wording used <1.0 mg/dL (0.32 mmol/L) and 20-30 mEq/L. That unit change is a real conflict. Three small 1980s RCTs (Fisher and Kitabchi 1983, n=30; Wilson 1982, n=44; plus Keller and Berger 1980) showed no clinical benefit, and Fisher and Kitabchi showed lower ionized calcium with phosphate. Current FDA labeling caps a single dose at 45 mmol P and rates at about 6.4 mmol/h P (10 mEq/h K) peripheral and about 15 mmol/h central.

### Cited Findings
- 2024 consensus, verbatim: "In DKA, there is a shift of phosphate from intracellular to extracellular fluid, with an excess urinary phosphate loss leading to hypophosphataemia. Whole-body losses can be up to 1.0 mmol/kg; however, unless there is evidence of muscle weakness, such as respiratory or cardiac compromise with the phosphate <1.0 mmol/l, routine administration of phosphate is not indicated. Several prospective randomised studies have failed to show any beneficial effect of phosphate replacement on the clinical outcome of DKA, and excessively rapid phosphate replacement may precipitate hypocalcaemia. When necessary, 20–30 mmol of potassium phosphate can be added to replacement fluids. There is scarce data on phosphate deficiency or the effects of phosphate replacement in HHS, so we recommend a similar approach to phosphorus replacement." The Diabetes Care version also reads "<1.0 mmol/L" and "20–30 mmol" (confirmed). No grade. [verified] — [PMC11343900](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11343900/); [PMC11272983](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11272983/)
- 2024 consensus also says phosphate should be drawn every 4 h with electrolytes until resolution. [verified] — [PMC11343900](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11343900/)
- 2009 ADA (superseded): "careful phosphate replacement may sometimes be indicated in patients with cardiac dysfunction, anemia, or respiratory depression and in those with serum phosphate concentration <1.0 mg/dl. When needed, 20–30 mEq/l potassium phosphate can be added to replacement fluids." Maximum rate for severe hypophosphatemia given as 4.5 mmol/h (1.5 mL/h of K2PO4). [verified, fetch-tool extraction] — [PMC2699725](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2699725/)

## 5. Magnesium

### Takeaway
None of the 2024 consensus, the ADA Standards of Care 2026 or JBDS DKA 2023 contains any magnesium recommendation. JBDS HHS 2022 is the only guideline that addresses it: hypomagnesemia is common but probably an epiphenomenon, there are no replacement data, and IV magnesium is reserved for severe symptomatic depletion (ECG or neurologic changes). Any "replace Mg if <2 mg/dL" row on an ED chart is local practice, not guideline-based.

### Cited Findings

## 6. Sodium bicarbonate

### Takeaway
All current guidelines say no routine bicarbonate. The 2024 consensus says to "consider" it only for pH <7.0: 100 mmol 8.4% NaHCO3 in 400 mL sterile water every 2 h until pH >7.0, with no KCl in the recipe. This replaces the 2009 ADA trigger of pH <6.9 with 20 mEq KCl added and run at 200 mL/h over 2 h. JBDS gives no pH threshold and leaves bicarbonate to ICU use when pH stays low on inotropes. The evidence is thin: three small adult RCTs (none enrolled pH <6.85) and retrospective cohorts (Viallon 1999; Duhon 2013, n=86 with pH <7.0, no faster resolution of acidosis) show no clinical benefit, plus more potassium needs. A 2026 MIMIC-IV propensity analysis reports less renal and neurologic worsening with bicarbonate but no mortality difference (observational, at high risk of confounding). I found no 2024-2026 RCT of bicarbonate in DKA. Sodium bicarbonate injection is in active FDA shortage.

In addition, potential detrimental effects of bicarbonate therapy have been reported, such as an increased risk of hypokalaemia, decreased tissue oxygen uptake, cerebral oedema and development of paradoxical central nervous system acidosis. ## 7. Hyperchloremic non-anion-gap acidosis during recovery; how resolution criteria handle it

### Takeaway
Saline-driven hyperchloremic normal-anion-gap acidosis is expected in recovery. The guidelines call it self-limited with "few clinical consequences," but it keeps bicarbonate low and can falsely prolong "unresolved DKA" (and insulin infusions) if resolution is judged by bicarbonate or anion gap. The 2024 consensus fixes this by defining resolution as ketones <0.6 mmol/L plus pH >=7.3 or bicarbonate >=18 mmol/L, with the explicit instruction not to use the anion gap. JBDS uses ketones <0.6 and pH >7.3 and says not to use bicarbonate as a surrogate. Trials that used older ADA (anion-gap-based) definitions, such as SMART/SALT-ED, may overstate the clinical advantage of balanced fluids. That is an inference, not proven.

### Cited Findings
- 2024 consensus resolution criteria: "Resolution of DKA is defined as achieving plasma ketone <0.6 mmol/l and venous pH ≥7.3 or bicarbonate ≥18 mmol/l. Ideally, the blood glucose concentration should also be <11.1 mmol/l (200 mg/dl). The anion gap should not be used as a criterion, as it may be misleading because of the presence of hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis caused by large volumes of 0.9% sodium chloride solution... urinary ketone measurement should be avoided as a criterion of DKA resolution." HHS resolution: osmolality <300 mOsm/kg, hyperglycemia corrected, urine output >0.5 mL/kg/h, improved cognition, glucose <13.9 mmol/L (250 mg/dL). [verified] — [PMC11343900](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11343900/)
- 2024 consensus Table 3, normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis: "Hyperchloraemic non-anion gap acidosis may be seen during the recovery phase of DKA, but the risk is unknown. It is likely to be caused by loss of keto-anions, which are metabolised to bicarbonate, and excess fluid infusion of chloride-containing fluids during treatment." "Observed during the recovery phase of DKA, it is self-limiting with few clinical consequences." Mitigation: "There is some evidence that hyperchloraemic acidosis occurs less frequently with balanced electrolyte solutions and when slower saline infusion is administered." [verified] — [PMC11343900](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11343900/)
- 2009 ADA: "Hyperchloremic non–anion gap acidosis, which is seen during the recovery phase of DKA, is self-limited with few clinical consequences." [verified, fetch-tool extraction] — [PMC2699725](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2699725/)
- JBDS DKA 2023: resolution is "ketones less than 0.6 mmol/L and venous pH over 7.3 (do not use bicarbonate as a surrogate at this stage because the hyperchloraemic acidosis associated with large volumes of 0.9% sodium chloride will lower bicarbonate levels)." [verified] — [JBDS 02 PDF](https://abcd.care/sites/default/files/site_uploads/JBDS_Guidelines_Current/JBDS_02_DKA_Guideline_with_QR_code_March_2023.pdf)

- Inference: under 2024 ketone-based criteria, part of the "faster resolution" with balanced fluids (driven by bicarbonate or anion gap) may disappear. The Soliman 2026 sensitivity analysis restricted to ADA-composite definitions shrank the effect to MD -0.79 h (-5.31 to 3.72). [verified number; interpretation is inference]

## 8. Dextrose timing and the "two-bag" system: evidence

### Takeaway
Guidelines agree on adding dextrose (5-10%) once glucose is <250 mg/dL (13.9-14 mmol/L) in DKA while continuing insulin until ketoacidosis resolves (JBDS: D10 at 125 mL/h alongside saline). For HHS, JBDS 2022 also uses <14 mmol/L (about 250 mg/dL). The often-quoted "300 mg/dL in HHS" comes from the superseded 2009 ADA consensus. Neither the 2024 consensus nor the ADA 2026 Standards formally endorse the two-bag system. A 2026 Annals of Emergency Medicine meta-analysis of 21 mostly observational studies (9 adult, 3329 adult visits) found less hypoglycemia (RR 0.50), resolution about 1.8 h faster, and in adults about 3.7 h shorter insulin infusions and less hypokalemia (RR 0.84) with the two-bag method.

## Gaps and conflicts

**Disagreements between sources**
- **Fluid of choice:** The 2024 ADA/EASD/JBDS/AACE/DTS consensus allows either saline or balanced crystalloid by "local availability, cost and resources," noting faster resolution with balanced fluids. JBDS DKA 2023 still defaults to 0.9% NaCl with premixed KCl, a UK safety (NPSA) rationale. JBDS HHS 2022 recommends 0.9% NaCl. ADA SOC 2026 is silent. None gives a formal grade. [verified]
- **First-hour volume:** The consensus says 500-1000 mL/h for 2-4 h. JBDS says 1 L over the first hour, then 1 L per 2 h, 2 h, 4 h, 4 h, 6 h; for SBP <90, 500 mL over 10-15 min, repeatable. The 2009 ADA said 15-20 mL/kg/h (1-1.5 L first hour). [verified]
- **Potassium cut-points:** Consensus: start <5.0, give 20-30 mmol/L, hold insulin if <3.5 and give 10 mmol/h. JBDS: start <5.5, give 40 mmol/L; <3.5 → senior review and more concentrated K (central/HDU), with no instruction to hold insulin. 2009 ADA: start below 5.0-5.2, hold insulin if <3.3. [verified except 2009 flowchart doses, recalled]
- **Phosphate threshold units:** The 2024 consensus (both Diabetes Care and Diabetologia texts) says "phosphate <1.0 mmol/L" (about 3.1 mg/dL, near the normal range) and "20–30 mmol" potassium phosphate. The 2009 ADA said "<1.0 mg/dL" (0.32 mmol/L) and "20–30 mEq/L." The 2024 "mmol/L" may be a unit-conversion artifact. For a US chart, flag it and do not silently convert. A 20-30 mmol phosphate dose carries about 29-47 mEq K (1.47-1.57 mEq K per mmol P), not 20-30 mEq K. [verified texts; interpretation is inference]
- **Bicarbonate:** 2024 consensus: consider at pH <7.0; 100 mmol in 400 mL sterile water every 2 h to pH >7.0; no KCl in the recipe. 2009 ADA: pH <6.9; 100 mmol in 400 mL with 20 mEq KCl at 200 mL/h over 2 h. JBDS: no pH threshold; ICU discretion. ADA SOC 2026: "generally not recommended." Calculation check (inference): 100 mmol NaHCO3 diluted to 400-500 mL total is about 200-250 mmol/L Na, roughly 400-500 mOsm/L, so it is hypertonic, not "isotonic" as the consensus states. An isotonic preparation is about 150 mEq/L (for example, 150 mEq in 1 L sterile water or D5W). Verify with a pharmacist reference. [verified quotes; arithmetic inference]
- **Dextrose threshold in HHS:** "300 mg/dL" appears only in the superseded 2009 ADA text. JBDS HHS 2022 uses <14 mmol/L (about 250 mg/dL), and the 2024 consensus gives no HHS-specific dextrose threshold, only DKA <250 mg/dL. Recommend that the chart cite 250 mg/dL for DKA and note the HHS variance. [verified]
- **0.45% saline:** The 2009 ADA switched to 0.45% NaCl at 250-500 mL/h when corrected Na was normal or high. The 2024 consensus and JBDS HHS restrict 0.45% to osmolality that is not falling despite adequate positive balance (JBDS: no fluid weaker than 0.45%). This is a substantive change: the routine corrected-sodium switch is superseded. [verified]
- **Balanced-fluid efficacy:** Alghamdi 2022 and Szabó 2024 (positive) vs Liu 2024, Gupta 2025 and Soliman 2026 (neutral on resolution, consistent on chloride and bicarbonate). Differences come from inclusion of observational studies, pediatric trials, resolution definitions, and the newer neutral RCTs (BRISK-ED, Trifi). [verified]

**Recent changes and superseded items**
- 2024 consensus supersedes the 2009 ADA consensus (Kitabchi 2009). Changes: balanced crystalloids explicitly allowed; 0.45% saline no longer routine; insulin 0.05 U/kg/h at glucose <250 mg/dL; potassium hold threshold 3.5 (was 3.3); bicarbonate threshold pH <7.0 (was <6.9) without the KCl add-in; resolution now ketone-based, with the anion gap excluded. [verified]
- JBDS DKA was revised March 2023. Its "awaited" systematic review of fluid choice has since been overtaken by several 2024-2026 meta-analyses (Section 2). [verified]
- ADA Standards of Care 2026 adds no new fluid or electrolyte specifics; it relies on the 2024 consensus. [verified]
- New since 2024: Trifi RCT (2025/2026), Soliman and Hamed 2026 reviews, Ann Emerg Med 2026 two-bag meta-analysis, BEST-DKA completion (results pending), DRINK trial recruiting. [verified]
- FDA declared the 0.9% NaCl shortage resolved on 2025-08-08. Dextrose and sodium bicarbonate injections remain in shortage in November 2026. [verified]
- Pediatrics: see the companion pediatric notes (PECARN FLUID; pediatric balanced-fluid RCTs 2025-2026). Not covered here.
