### Takeaway (Section 1)
Direct evidence that a pharmacist changes DKA outcomes is thin: small single-center retrospective or pre-post studies, almost all ICU-based, with no RCT and no US ED-specific study of "ED pharmacist vs no ED pharmacist" for DKA found. The best signals: a 24-h ICU pharmacist co-management model cut protocol hypoglycemia from 23.7% to 5.4% (Noll 2019, n=59 vs 74), a tele-ICU pharmacist was associated with 7.3-h faster DKA resolution (Olson 2024), and pharmacist-monitored computerized dosing reduced hypoglycemia events numerically (Defayette 2020). Pharmacists are co-authors and implementers of most ED order-set and SQ-insulin (SQuID) programs, and a 2026 AJHP paper credits pharmacist-led safety modifications for sustaining SQuID. Time to insulin as a pharmacist-attributable outcome has not been published for adults (one pediatric QI conference report; see Section 2).

## 2. DKA order sets, nurse-driven and electronic protocols: effects on errors, hypoglycemia, hypokalemia, time to closure

### Takeaway
Standardized order sets consistently improve guideline adherence and usually shorten time to anion gap or ketone closure by about 2-8 h; effects on hypoglycemia are mixed (some show fewer events, one ICU order set showed more), and hypokalemia improves mainly with two-bag systems. Computerized/nurse-titrated insulin calculators reliably cut hypoglycemia (by 70% in one EHR calculator study; 0 vs 10 patients with GlucoStabilizer) without speeding resolution. Evidence is retrospective pre-post or cohort only.

## 3. Insulin infusion safety: ISMP high-alert status and guidance, error types, TJC/ISMP harm reports

### Takeaway
All insulin (SC and IV) is an ISMP high-alert class, U-500 carries "special emphasis," and in an ISMP 2014 survey IV insulin ranked first among high-alert drugs of concern. ISMP's 2026-2027 Targeted Medication Safety Best Practices (TMSBP) call for all infusions on smart pumps with dose error-reduction software (DERS) at >95% compliance, separate programmable bolus limits if bolusing from a continuous infusion, independent double checks limited to the highest-risk drugs (IV insulin is named), and a hypoglycemia protocol embedded in every insulin order set. Common DKA insulin errors are wrong concentration or 10-fold errors, bolus from a vial with a syringe, missing dextrose when glucose falls, home SC insulin restarted on top, and withheld basal doses.

### Takeaway (insulin error checklist, synthesized from the sources above; inference where marked)
Pharmacist catch list for the ED insulin drip (each tied to a source above): (1) standard concentration only (1 unit/mL; no custom/"wildcard" concentrations; smart pump DERS with hard limits); (2) actual measured metric weight for units/kg dosing (TMSBP BP3); (3) no IV insulin drawn from a vial with a non-insulin or 1-mL tuberculin syringe (10-fold errors cluster in the ED, Ro & Jones 2026); bolus, if used, programmed from the pump with separate bolus limits (TMSBP BP8d); (4) potassium known and >3.5 mmol/L before starting insulin (2024 consensus threshold, see sibling insulin_strategies.md); (5) dextrose added when glucose falls below 250 mg/dL and the bag actually hung is the one ordered (Noll 2019 error); (6) hypoglycemia protocol embedded in the order set (TMSBP FAQ); (7) basal insulin given before the drip is stopped and home SC insulin not stacked on top during transition (Noll 2019; ISMP 2017 "withholding of a BASAL dose"); (8) U-500 never IV; home U-500 doses documented in units (PA PSA 2025; U-500 vials discontinued in US). ## 4. Potassium safety: concentrated KCl, premixed bags, maximum rates, cardiac monitoring

### Takeaway
Concentrated KCl for injection and potassium phosphates injection are ISMP high-alert drugs. The Joint Commission's 2002 National Patient Safety Goal drove removal of concentrated KCl vials from patient care units. US labels set the usual maximum at 10 mEq/h (and 200 mEq/24 h) when K >2.5 mEq/L, allowing up to 40 mEq/h only for K <2.0 mEq/L with ECG changes or paralysis, under continuous cardiac monitoring. Premixed highly concentrated bags carry "use central route whenever possible," and 300-400 mEq/L concentrations are central-only. In DKA, potassium is replaced (usually 20-40 mEq per liter of fluid) to keep K 4-5 mmol/L, and insulin is held if K <3.5 mmol/L (see sibling fluids_electrolytes_bicarbonate.md for guideline doses).

## 5. Discharge and prevention: ADA Standards of Care 2026, 2024 consensus, sick-day rules, ketone meters, readmission, follow-up

### Takeaway
ADA 2026 grades the discharge pieces: Rec 16.17 (B) requires DKA/HHS recognition-prevention-management education in every discharge plan; Rec 16.18 (B) requires an individualized structured discharge plan; Recs 6.21 (C) and 6.22 (B) require crisis-history review at every encounter and structured education for all people with T1D and anyone at risk. Narrative text (not separately graded) says people on intensive insulin "should not stop or hold their basal insulin even if not eating," should have ketone tools and check ketones when ill or glucose >200 mg/dL, and should hold SGLT2 inhibitors and metformin when oral intake fails. Follow-up within 1 month is advised, and within 1-2 weeks if medicines changed; the 2024 consensus cites follow-up within 2-4 weeks and notes readmissions cluster within 2 weeks. Blood ketone meters halved hospital/ED visits vs urine strips in one pediatric/young-adult RCT. ED discharge for mild DKA is supported by protocols and observational data, but no US guideline gives formal ED discharge criteria.
