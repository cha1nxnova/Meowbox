# Blunt and penetrating trauma guidelines for the ER pharmacist, and trauma infection prophylaxis (current to November 2026)

**Scope.** US adult ER, stabilization and the first 24 h. Hemorrhage/TXA/reversal, TBI, SCI, analgesia and VTE belong to other researchers. They appear here only where a blunt or penetrating guideline makes a specific drug recommendation.

**Tag legend.** Every bullet carries one of these:
- [verified] = read this session in the primary document, its PubMed abstract, or PMC full text.
- [secondary] = from a search-engine digest, an institutional protocol, or a review. The primary page was not opened, or it was blocked: east.org, guidelinecentral.com and mayoclinic.org all returned HTTP 403.
- [recalled] = from memory, not confirmed this session. Do not print without checking.

**Research date.** 2026-11-20. A PubMed sweep for EAST guidelines published 2022-2026 found 41 hits; 40 titles were reviewed.

## Q1. EAST practice management guidelines relevant to the ER phase (with GRADE wording)

### Takeaway

Several key EAST guidelines are 10 to 15 years old but are still the newest available. They use the older, pre-GRADE "Level 1/2/3" wording:
- penetrating abdominal antibiotics (2012)
- open fractures (2011)
- blunt splenic and hepatic nonoperative management (2012)
- penetrating neck injury (2008)
- pelvic fracture hemorrhage (2011)
- blunt cardiac injury (2012)
- geriatric trauma (2012)

The GRADE-era EAST guidelines with ER drug content are fewer:
- **Tube thoracostomy prophylaxis (2022):** "we conditionally recommend."
- **No routine vaccines after splenic angioembolization (2022):** "we conditionally recommend against."
- **BCVI antithrombotic therapy (2020):** "We recommend the use of ATT."
- **Rhabdomyolysis (2022):** conditionally against bicarbonate and mannitol.
- **Rib fractures in older adults (2023):** no recommendation for or against ketamine or epidural.
- **Trauma in pregnancy (2025):** Kleihauer-Betke testing to dose Rh(D) immune globulin.

### Inferences

- No newer EAST guideline turned up in the 2022-2026 sweep for open fractures, penetrating abdominal antibiotics, blunt spleen or liver nonoperative management, penetrating neck, blunt cardiac injury or pelvic fracture. The handout should label these "old (2008-2012) but still the newest EAST guidance."
- EAST's 2022 change on tube thoracostomy prophylaxis is a common ER pharmacist intervention point. It moved from no recommendation (2012) to a conditional recommendation. It supports a dose at insertion, not continuing until the tube comes out.

### Gaps

- The full text of Kim 2020 was not read and is not in PMC. Its body may state aspirin vs heparin and timing; the abstract does not.
- The Hoff 2011 full text was not read. east.org returned 403 and PubMed has no abstract, so its wording here is secondary.
- Any antibiotic or tetanus content in Tisherman 2008 (penetrating neck) is unknown.
- One of the 41 EAST 2022-2026 hits was not displayed.

## Q2. WTA algorithms and ACS TQIP Best Practices with drug content

### Takeaway

The Western Trauma Association (WTA) has new or updated algorithms for:
- BCVI (Magee 2026)
- penetrating thoracic injury (Lorenzo 2026)
- the mangled extremity (Kerwin 2026)
- blunt splenic injury from ED to discharge (Shatz 2023)

None has a PubMed abstract, so their drug content was not read.

The ACS TQIP Orthopaedic Best Practices Guideline (released November 2015) was read in full for open fractures:
- IV antibiotics within 1 h of presentation.
- Cefazolin for type 1/2.
- Add a gram-negative agent such as gentamicin for type 3.
- Add penicillin for severe contamination or impaired vascularity.
- Stop 24 h after the procedure, or continue up to 72 h for severe contamination.

TQIP's tetanus wording conflicts with ACIP/CDC.

### Cited Findings

**ACS TQIP Best Practices in the Management of Orthopaedic Trauma ("Released November 2015") [verified; PDF text extracted].** [ACS TQIP Ortho BPG PDF](https://www.facs.org/media/mkbnhqtw/ortho_guidelines.pdf)

Key points, verbatim:
- "Patients with open fractures should receive intravenous antimicrobials within one hour of presentation to reduce the risk of infection."
- "Patients with Gustilo open type 1 or 2 fractures should receive a first-generation cephalosporin (for example, cefazolin)."
- "Gram negative coverage should be considered in patients with Gustilo type 3 fractures (for example, gentamicin)."
- "The addition of a penicillin for anaerobic coverage should be considered in the presence of severe contamination or impaired vascularity."
- "In the presence of documented [beta]-lactam allergies, consider regimens with a similar spectrum of coverage."
- "Antibiotics should be administered for no longer than 24 hours after a surgical procedure. In cases of severe contamination, antibiotics may be continued for as long as 72 hours after a surgical procedure."

Body text, verbatim:
- Type III wording is firmer than in the key points: "a first generation cephalosporin and another antimicrobial with gram-negative coverage (for example, gentamicin) is recommended."
- "Scheduled dosing of antibiotics should be continued until surgical management is performed."
- "A minimum of 24 hours of antibiotics should be administered from the start of the surgical procedure."
- "Antibiotics should not be administered beyond 72 hours unless a second operative intervention occurs within that time period."

Other points:
- Irrigation and debridement within 24 h; the panel "does not endorse the 'six hour rule.'"
- The scope is open fractures from blunt force.
- The document gives no doses.

TQIP tetanus text, verbatim [verified]:
- "tetanus toxoid should be administered if the last booster dose was given more than 10 years prior to evaluation or if the vaccination history is unknown or unclear. Tetanus immunoglobulin should also be given when it has been longer than 10 years since the last booster dose or when the patient had an incomplete primary immunization."
- This conflicts with CDC/ACIP (see Q6). An open fracture counts as a dirty/major wound, so toxoid is due at 5 or more years. A fully primed patient does not get TIG just because more than 10 years have passed.

TQIP performance indicator, Appendix A [verified]:
- "Patients with open fractures receive intravenous antibiotics within 60 minutes of presentation. ***Optimal antibiotic regimen may vary based on extent of tissue damage and contamination."

TQIP quality metric [secondary; search summary]:
- An ACS case study reports the MTQIP benchmark of 90 minutes.
- The TQIP open-fracture cohort was expanded in Fall 2024 to humerus, radius, ulna, femur, tibia and fibula.
- [ACS case study](https://www.facs.org/quality-programs/qi-resources/case-studies/improving-antibiotic-time-for-open-fracture/)

## Q3. Open fracture antibiotic prophylaxis (and the full infection-prophylaxis chart)

### Takeaway

All guidance agrees on these points:
- Start cefazolin as early as possible, within 60 minutes of arrival (TQIP; AAOS "Strong" for cefazolin or clindamycin).
- For Gustilo type III, add gram-negative coverage. TQIP and EAST name gentamicin; EAST endorses once-daily aminoglycoside dosing.
- For fecal, farm or soil contamination, or impaired vascularity, add penicillin.
- Avoid fluoroquinolones (EAST Level 2).
- Stop 24 h after wound closure or the procedure. For type III or severe contamination, continue up to 72 h after injury, but no more than 24 h after soft-tissue coverage.

Newer data (2022-2026) question both routine gram-negative add-on and longer courses. No US society has changed its recommendation.

## Q4. Penetrating abdominal trauma prophylaxis (choices and doses)

### Takeaway

Give one preoperative dose of broad-spectrum aerobic plus anaerobic coverage. Continue for no more than 24 h, and only when a hollow viscus is injured (EAST 2012 "Level I"). Do not extend it for damage control laparotomy. Agent and dose details come from the 2000 EAST original and ASHP 2013 conventions, which were recalled, not re-read.

### Cited Findings

- **EAST 2012 (Goldberg) [verified].** Level I: 24 h maximum with hollow viscus injury. No data support more than 24 h in damage control. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/TA.0b013e3182701902)
- **EAST 2000 (Luchette).** *J Trauma.* 2000;48(3):508-18. PMID 10744294 [citation verified]. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005373-200003000-00024) Content [recalled]:
  - Level I: a single preoperative dose of broad-spectrum aerobic and anaerobic coverage.
  - Level I: no further doses without hollow viscus injury; up to 24 h with one.
  - Level III: in hemorrhagic shock, increase the dose 2-3x and repeat after every 10 units transfused.
  - Aminoglycosides may be suboptimal because of altered pharmacokinetics in trauma.
- **SIS 2017 (Mazuski) [citation and abstract verified].** Mazuski JE, Tessier JM, May AK, Sawyer RG, et al. The Surgical Infection Society revised guidelines on the management of intra-abdominal infection. *Surg Infect (Larchmt).* 2017;18(1):1-76. doi:10.1089/sur.2016.261. PMID 28085573. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1089/sur.2016.261)
  - Bowel injury repaired within 12 h: treat 24 h or less [recalled].
- **Agent choices, per ASHP 2013 colorectal prophylaxis conventions [recalled; Bratzler 2013 citation verified]:** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2146/ajhp120568)
  - cefoxitin 2 g IV
  - cefotetan 2 g IV
  - ertapenem 1 g IV
  - cefazolin 2 g plus metronidazole 500 mg IV
  - ceftriaxone 2 g plus metronidazole 500 mg IV
  - severe beta-lactam allergy: clindamycin 900 mg or metronidazole 500 mg, plus gentamicin 5 mg/kg, a fluoroquinolone, or aztreonam

### Inferences

- The ER pharmacist's job here:
  - Give the first dose right away when laparotomy is likely.
  - Redose for long cases or massive transfusion.
  - Push to stop at 24 h, and do not continue it because of drains or an open abdomen (EAST 2012).
- Cefoxitin's short redosing interval (every 2 h) and US cefotetan supply problems can steer the choice toward cefazolin plus metronidazole, or ertapenem [recalled practice point].

### Gaps

- The Goldberg 2012 full text was not read. The specific agents, doses and shock-dosing wording in the 2012 update are unverified.
- No EAST, WTA or SIS update on penetrating abdominal prophylaxis was found for 2013-2026.

## Q5. Tube thoracostomy presumptive antibiotics (EAST 2022)

### Takeaway

EAST 2022 (Freeman, GRADE): "We conditionally recommend that antibiotic prophylaxis be given at the time of insertion to reduce empyema in adult patients who require TT for traumatic hemothorax or pneumothorax."
- No drug or duration is specified.
- The benefit is concentrated in penetrating trauma (empyema OR 0.25).
- There is no effect on pneumonia or mortality.
- Cefazolin was the most studied agent.

## Q6. Tetanus prophylaxis in wounds (ACIP/CDC)

### Takeaway

CDC wound-management guidance (updated and reviewed September 8, 2026):

| Vaccination history | Clean, minor wound | Dirty or major wound |
|---|---|---|
| Unknown, unvaccinated, or incomplete primary series | Vaccinate | Vaccinate, plus TIG 250 IU IM |
| Complete primary series | Vaccinate if last dose was 10 or more years ago | Vaccinate if last dose was 5 or more years ago; TIG only for HIV or severe immunodeficiency |

- Since the October 2019 ACIP update, either Td or Tdap may be used.
- No substantive change in 2024-2026 was found.

### Cited Findings

**CDC "Clinical Guidance for Wound Management to Prevent Tetanus" (published May 15, 2024; updated and reviewed September 8, 2026) [verified via WebFetch summary].** [CDC page](https://www.cdc.gov/tetanus/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)
- Unknown history, unvaccinated, or incomplete primary series: vaccinate for all wounds.
- Complete primary series, clean and minor wound: vaccinate if the last dose was 10 or more years ago.
- Complete primary series, dirty or major wound: vaccinate if the last dose was 5 or more years ago.
- Last dose less than 5 years ago with a complete series: no vaccine needed for any wound.
- Examples of dirty or major wounds: "penetrating or puncture wounds; wounds containing dirt, soil, feces, or saliva (such as animal or human bites); and wounds with devitalized tissue (burns, compound fractures, crush injuries, frostbite...)."
- TIG for clean, minor wounds: "never indicated."
- TIG for dirty or major wounds: unknown history, never vaccinated, incomplete primary series, HIV, or severe immunodeficiency.
- TIG dose: 250 IU IM. CDC does not stockpile TIG.
- "Topical or systemic antibiotics are not recommended to prevent tetanus."

**ACIP 2019 update [verified].** Havers FP, Moro PL, Hunter P, Hariri S, Bernstein H. Use of tetanus toxoid, reduced diphtheria toxoid, and acellular pertussis vaccines: updated recommendations of the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, United States, 2019. *MMWR Morb Mortal Wkly Rep.* 2020;69(3):77-83. doi:10.15585/mmwr.mm6903a5. PMID 31971933; PMC7367039. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.15585/mmwr.mm6903a5)
- Change: "allow use of either Td or Tdap where previously only Td was recommended."
- This includes "tetanus prophylaxis when indicated for wound management in persons who had previously received Tdap."

**ACIP 2018 compilation [verified abstract].** Liang JL, Tiwari T, Moro P, Messonnier NE, et al. Prevention of pertussis, tetanus, and diphtheria with vaccines in the United States: recommendations of the ACIP. *MMWR Recomm Rep.* 2018;67(2):1-44. doi:10.15585/mmwr.rr6702a1. PMID 29702631; PMC5919600. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.15585/mmwr.rr6702a1)
- A compilation with no new recommendations.
- Its wound table uses "unknown or <3 doses" vs ">=3 doses" [recalled].
- Tdap is preferred for wound management in adults who have never had Tdap [recalled].
- Pregnancy: Tdap [recalled].

**Conflict to flag [verified both].** TQIP Ortho 2015 says to give toxoid if more than 10 years since the last booster, and TIG if more than 10 years since the last booster. That contradicts ACIP/CDC for open fractures, which count as dirty wounds. [TQIP PDF](https://www.facs.org/media/mkbnhqtw/ortho_guidelines.pdf)

### Inferences

- An open fracture, gunshot wound, stab wound or crush injury is a dirty or major wound. The usual ER pharmacist trigger for toxoid is therefore 5 years, not 10.
- Use Tdap when the patient has no documented prior Tdap. Either Td or Tdap is fine if Tdap was given before.
- TIG goes in a separate site from the toxoid [recalled practice point].

### Gaps

- The exact current MMWR table wording (">=3 doses") was not re-read.
- No 2024-2026 ACIP vote changing tetanus wound management was found. ACIP's membership changed in 2025 [recalled], so check for any late-2026 action.

## Q7. Post-splenectomy vaccines (CDC/ACIP as of 2026) and trauma-specific timing

### Takeaway

After a trauma splenectomy, give:
- **Pneumococcal:** one dose of PCV20 or PCV21. Or PCV15 followed by PPSV23, at least 8 weeks later for asplenia.
- **MenACWY:** 2 doses at least 8 weeks apart, then a booster every 5 years.
- **MenB:** Bexsero or Trumenba series; booster at 1 year, then every 2-3 years.
- **Hib:** 1 dose if not previously vaccinated.

On timing, a 1998 trauma RCT found better functional antibody response at day 14 than at day 1 or day 7. Many centers still vaccinate at discharge when follow-up is uncertain.

EAST 2022 conditionally recommends against routine vaccination after splenic angioembolization alone.

### Cited Findings

**CDC Adult Immunization Schedule Notes, ages 19 and older, United States, 2025 [verified via WebFetch summary].** The page is dated July 2, 2025, and was the version served on 2026-11-20. No 2026 adult schedule was found at that URL. [CDC adult notes](https://www.cdc.gov/vaccines/hcp/imz-schedules/adult-notes.html)
- **Pneumococcal:**
  - Not previously vaccinated, or unknown: one dose of PCV15, PCV20 or PCV21. If PCV15 is used, give PPSV23 at least 1 year later; for asplenia, "the minimum interval may be shortened to 8 weeks."
  - Already received PCV20 or PCV21: no further dose.
  - Previously received PCV13 plus one PPSV23: give PCV20 or PCV21 at least 5 years after the last pneumococcal dose.
- **MenACWY (Menveo or MenQuadfi):** 2 doses at least 8 weeks apart; booster every 5 years while risk remains.
- **MenB (Bexsero or Trumenba; same brand for all doses):**
  - Primary series at 0, 1-2 and 6 months, per the fetch summary.
  - Booster 1 year after the series, then every 2-3 years.
  - Bexsero was historically a 2-dose series for high-risk persons [recalled]. Recheck the page before printing.
- **Penbraya (MenABCWY):** can replace separate MenACWY and MenB doses when both are due the same day.
  - Penmenvy (GSK's MenABCWY, FDA-approved in 2025) was not mentioned on the page [verified absence; approval recalled].
- **Hib:** one dose for asplenia if no prior Hib. "Elective splenectomy: 1 dose preferably at least 14 days before splenectomy."

**Shatz 1998 RCT [verified abstract].** Shatz DV, Schinsky MF, Pais LB, Romero-Steiner S, Kirton OC, Carlone GM. Immune responses of splenectomized trauma patients to the 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine at 1 versus 7 versus 14 days after splenectomy. *J Trauma.* 1998;44(5):760-5; discussion 765-6. doi:10.1097/00005373-199805000-00004. PMID 9603075. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005373-199805000-00004)
- 59 patients.
- IgG antibody levels were similar whether vaccinated at day 1, 7 or 14.
- Functional (opsonophagocytic) activity was lower with early vaccination.
- "Better functional antibody responses ... seemed to occur with delayed (14-day) vaccination."

**ACIP general best practice [recalled].** If vaccines were not given before splenectomy, give them at least 14 days after; if the patient may not return, give them before discharge.

**EAST 2022 (Freeman, Injury) [verified].** "we conditionally recommend against routine post-splenectomy vaccinations" after angioembolization. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.injury.2022.08.006)

**WTA 2023 splenic algorithm [gap].** Its vaccine recommendations were not read. Its first author, Shatz DV, also led the 1998 RCT. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/TA.0000000000003829)

### Inferences

- A pharmacist-ready order set:
  - PCV20 or PCV21, one dose.
  - MenACWY dose 1; dose 2 at least 8 weeks later.
  - MenB dose 1; complete the series per brand.
  - Hib, one dose.
  - Give at day 14, or at discharge if that comes first and the patient may not return.
- The 1998 data are for PPSV23, a polysaccharide vaccine. Applying day-14 timing to conjugate vaccines (PCV20/21) is an extrapolation.

### Gaps

- No 2026 CDC adult schedule was found. Whether ACIP, reconstituted in 2025, changed asplenia recommendations in 2026 is unverified. The 2025 notes are the latest confirmed.
- MenB brand-specific dosing for high-risk adults needs direct confirmation.
- The WTA 2023 splenic vaccine recommendations were not obtained.

## Q8. BCVI antithrombotics and other blunt-injury drug items

### Takeaway

EAST 2020 strongly recommends ("We recommend") antithrombotic therapy for BCVI. It lowered stroke (OR 0.20) and mortality (OR 0.17). The abstract does not name an agent.
- Older EAST wording (2010) treats heparin and antiplatelet therapy as equivalent and says to start heparin without a bolus.
- Aspirin is usually favored because it carries less bleeding risk (secondary meta-analysis summary).
- Timing: "as early as safely possible" (institutional protocol).

Other drug items:
- Rhabdomyolysis: aggressive IV fluids; no bicarbonate or mannitol.
- Pregnancy: Kleihauer-Betke test, then Rh(D) immune globulin.

The blunt cardiac injury, pelvic fracture and penetrating neck guidelines contain no drug recommendations, as far as was read.

### Cited Findings

- **Kim 2020 EAST BCVI [verified].** Recommendations as quoted in Q1. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/TA.0000000000002668)
- **EAST 2010 BCVI page [secondary].** [EAST BCVI page](https://www.east.org/mobile/practice-management-guideline/trauma/87)
  - "either heparin or antiplatelet therapy ... seemingly equivalent results."
  - Heparin "without a bolus."
  - Grade I-II injuries: "antithrombotic agents such as aspirin or heparin."
- **Aspirin vs heparin meta-analysis [secondary; primary study not identified].** As summarized in search results: no difference in stroke; less bleeding with aspirin.
- **Vanderbilt 2026 institutional BCVI guideline [secondary].** Antithrombotic therapy "will be initiated as early as safely possible after injury identification." [VUMC BCVI PMG 2026](https://www.vumc.org/trauma-and-scc/sites/default/files/public_files/Protocols/BCVI-PMG_2026.pdf)
- **Typical aspirin dose [recalled].** 81-325 mg daily.
- **WTA 2026 BCVI algorithm (Magee) [verified citation].** Exists; content not read. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/TA.0000000000005197)
- **Rhabdomyolysis (EAST 2022) and Rh(D) immune globulin in pregnancy (EAST 2025) [verified].** See Q1.
- **Blunt cardiac injury (EAST 2012) [verified].** ECG plus troponin I; no drug recommendations.
- **Pelvic fracture (EAST 2011) [verified].** No drug content in the abstract.
- **Geriatric trauma (EAST 2012) [verified].** Anticoagulated older adults need coagulation assessment and a head CT as soon as possible.

### Inferences

- The ER pharmacist's BCVI question is whether to start aspirin now or wait, which depends on any concurrent TBI or solid-organ injury. Cross-reference the TBI and VTE researchers' notes.

### Gaps

- The agent and timing wording in the EAST 2020 and WTA 2026 BCVI full texts was not obtained.

## Q9. Bite wounds and facial lacerations; ATLS 11th edition

### Takeaway

None of the trauma-society guidelines reviewed (EAST, WTA, TQIP) addresses antibiotics for bite wounds or facial lacerations. CDC counts bites as dirty wounds for tetanus purposes.

ATLS 11th edition is in use; a search snippet said Mayo had implemented it by January 2026. Its antibiotic and tetanus content could not be read.

### Cited Findings

- **CDC tetanus page [verified].** Animal and human bites are "dirty or major" wounds because of saliva. [CDC page](https://www.cdc.gov/tetanus/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)
- **Bite antibiotics [recalled].** Covered by the IDSA 2014 skin and soft-tissue infection guideline (Stevens DL et al., *Clin Infect Dis* 2014;59:e10-52): amoxicillin-clavulanate as preemptive therapy for higher-risk bites. This is not a trauma-society source.
- **ATLS 11th edition [secondary].** A Mayo Clinic news page ("ATLS update: what trauma professionals need to know") says ATLS 11 was fully implemented at the Mayo Clinic Trauma Center as of January 2026. The page itself returned 403. [Mayo page](https://www.mayoclinic.org/es/medical-professionals/trauma/news/atls-update-what-trauma-professionals-need-to-know/mac-20602992)
- **ATLS 10th edition (2018) [recalled; not read].** Its musculoskeletal chapter calls for early IV antibiotics in open fractures (a first-generation cephalosporin, adding gram-negative coverage for severe or type III fractures) and a review of tetanus status.

### Inferences

- For the handout, cite ACIP/CDC for tetanus and EAST/TQIP/AAOS for open-fracture antibiotics. Mark ATLS 11 as "not reviewed."

### Gaps

- The ATLS 11th edition text (2025) was not accessible, so any changes to its antibiotic or tetanus statements are unknown.
