## 1. AANS/CNS 2013 guidelines: exact recommendations and levels (Walters 2013 series; Ryken cardiopulmonary; Hurlbert pharmacologic; Dhall VTE)

### Takeaway
The 2013 AANS/CNS chapters give Level III recommendations to manage acute cervical SCI in an ICU, correct SBP <90 mm Hg, and keep MAP 85 to 90 mm Hg for the first 7 days. Methylprednisolone and GM-1 ganglioside are each "not recommended" (Level I). For VTE, LMWH or rotating beds and prophylaxis for patients with severe motor deficits are Level I; starting within 72 h and continuing 3 months are Level II. The MAP target has since been revised by AO Spine/Praxis 2024 (section 3).

Marked bradycardia (<45 bpm) occurred in 71% of severe cervical, 12% of mild cervical, and 4% of thoracolumbar injuries. Sinus slowing required "bolus injections of atropine or placement of a temporary pacemaker in 29% of the severe cervical SCI patients." Vale et al.: 9/10 complete cervical SCI needed pressors after volume to keep MAP 85, versus 52% of incomplete cervical and 9/29 thoracic. Neumann et al.: GCS <9, vasopressor need, and mechanical ventilation predicted mortality.

- Prophylaxis, **Level II**: "Low dose heparin therapy alone is not recommended as a prophylactic treatment strategy." / "Oral anticoagulation alone is not recommended as a prophylactic treatment strategy." / "Early administration of VTE prophylaxis (within 72 hours) is recommended." / "A 3-month duration of prophylactic treatment for deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE) is recommended."

### Inferences
- The 72-hour timing and the 3-month duration are **Level II**, not Level I or III. The Level I LMWH recommendation in 2013 lists "low molecular weight heparins, rotating beds, or a combination of modalities." Adjusted-dose heparin is discussed in the abstract but does not appear in the 2013 Level I box text.
- The 2013 Level I "not recommended" for methylprednisolone remains the only US neurosurgical-society statement found against steroids. AO Spine 2017 (co-sponsored with AANS/CNS) later softened this to a weak "suggest" within 8 h (section 2).

### Gaps
- No formal AANS/CNS retirement or replacement of the 2013 cardiopulmonary chapter was found. The AO Spine/Praxis 2024 guideline explicitly "update[s]" it. Whether AANS/CNS formally endorsed the 2024 MAP update was not established.

## 2. AO Spine 2017 clinical practice guideline (Global Spine J 2017;7(3 Suppl))

### Takeaway
AO Spine 2017 issued only weak ("we suggest") recommendations. A 24-h high-dose MPSS infusion may be offered within 8 h as a treatment option. MPSS should not be offered after 8 h or for 48 h. Anticoagulant thromboprophylaxis (LMWH or fixed low-dose UFH) should be started within 72 h. The 2017 timing-of-surgery recommendation was later upgraded in 2024 (section 3).

### Cited Findings
- **Citation correction [verified: PubMed]:** The steroid guideline is Fehlings MG, Wilson JR, Tetreault LA, et al. A Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Patients With Acute Spinal Cord Injury: Recommendations on the Use of Methylprednisolone Sodium Succinate. Global Spine J. 2017;7(3 Suppl):**203S-211S**. doi:10.1177/2192568217703085. PMID 29164025; PMC5686915. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5686915/). Pages 84S-94S are a different paper: Fehlings MG, Tetreault LA, Wilson JR, et al. "...Introduction, Rationale, and Scope." doi:10.1177/2192568217703387. PMID 29164036.
- GRADE wording [verified]: "a strong recommendation is worded as 'we recommend,' whereas a weaker recommendation is indicated by 'we suggest.'"
- MPSS recommendations, exact, with quality and strength from the full-text table [verified: PMC full text]:
  1. "We suggest not offering a 24-hour infusion of high-dose MPSS to adult patients who present after 8 hours with acute spinal cord injury." Quality: Moderate. Strength: Weak.
  2. "We suggest a 24-hour infusion of high-dose MPSS be offered to adult patients within 8 hours of acute spinal cord injury as a treatment option." Quality: Moderate. Strength: Weak.
  3. "We suggest not offering a 48-hour infusion of high-dose MPSS to adult patients with acute spinal cord injury." Quality: "No included studies." Strength: Weak.
- The guideline was developed "under the auspices of AOSpine North America, AOSpine International, and the AANS/CNS" [verified].
- Evidence used [verified]: across all timings there was no motor benefit at 6 wk (MD 1.23; 95% CI -1.08 to 3.54), 6 mo (1.19; -2.34 to 4.72), or 12 mo (-1.17; -4.80 to 2.47). Within 8 h, the pooled RCTs showed +3.88 motor points (95% CI 0.50-7.27; P=.02), and +3.21 (0.10-6.33; P=.04) when a cohort study was added. The 8-h cut point "was not selected at baseline, but chosen after data collection based on the median time from injury to treatment." Pooled harms were not significant: death RD -1.51; GI hemorrhage RD 4.51 (95% CI -1.92 to 10.94); pneumonia RD 4.69 (-3.19 to 12.57); sepsis RD 0.74. There was a "weak trend for increased risk of pulmonary embolism." NASCIS III 48 h vs 24 h: severe pneumonia higher (P=.02), severe sepsis P=.07.
- Context [verified]: a 2006 NASS survey found 86% would give MPSS, with "medicolegal reprisal" cited as a major motivator.
- Thromboprophylaxis guideline: Fehlings MG, Tetreault LA, Aarabi B, et al. ...Recommendations on the Type and Timing of Anticoagulant Thromboprophylaxis. Global Spine J. 2017;7(3 Suppl):212S-220S. doi:10.1177/2192568217702107. PMID 29164026; PMC5684841 [verified: abstract]:
  - "We suggest that anticoagulant thromboprophylaxis be offered routinely to reduce the risk of thromboembolic events in the acute period after SCI"
  - "We suggest that anticoagulant thromboprophylaxis, consisting of either subcutaneous low-molecular-weight heparin or fixed, low-dose unfractionated heparin (UFH) be offered ... Given the potential for increased bleeding events with the use of adjusted-dose UFH, we suggest against this option"
  - "We suggest commencing anticoagulant thromboprophylaxis within the first 72 hours after injury, if possible, in order to minimize the risk of venous thromboembolic complications during the period of acute hospitalization."
- Timing of decompression, 2017: [recalled] a weak recommendation to consider early surgery (within 24 h) as an option for adults regardless of level (Fehlings MG et al. Global Spine J 2017;7(3 Suppl):195S-202S; possibly PMID 29164024; NOT verified this session). **SUPERSEDED by the 2024 "recommend" (section 3).**

### Inferences
- The request's phrase "suggested as an option" paraphrases recommendation 2. The exact text is "...be offered to adult patients within 8 hours of acute spinal cord injury as a treatment option" (weak, moderate-quality evidence).
- The 2024 AO Spine/Praxis update covered only surgical timing, hemodynamics, and intraoperative SCI. The 2017 MPSS and anticoagulation recommendations therefore remain the latest AO Spine statements on those topics [inference from the verified 2024 scope statement].

## 3. AO Spine/Praxis 2024 updates and 2025 to 2026 consensus (MAP targets, duration, pressor choice, SCPP, timing)

### Takeaway
2024 GRADE guideline: "We suggest" MAP at least 75-80 but not higher than 90-95 mm Hg, for 3-7 days. Both recommendations are weak, based on very-low-quality evidence. The guideline makes no recommendation on vasopressor choice or SCPP targets. Surgery within 24 h is now "recommended" (strong, moderate evidence). The 2026 AO Spine Knowledge Forum appraisal (Neal) restates the MAP range as a conditional recommendation and makes no recommendation for SCPP-guided care or riluzole.

### Cited Findings
- Kwon BK, Tetreault LA, Martin AR, et al. A Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Patients With Acute Spinal Cord Injury: Recommendations on Hemodynamic Management. Global Spine J. 2024;14(3 Suppl):187S-211S. doi:10.1177/21925682231202348. PMID 38526923; PMC10964888. [verified: full text; pages confirmed by citation in Neal 2026] — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10964888/)
  - Recommendation (MAP target), exact: "We suggest the augmentation of mean arterial blood pressure to at least 75-80 mmHg but not higher than 90-95 mmHg in order to optimize spinal cord perfusion in acute traumatic SCI." Quality of Evidence: Very Low. Strength of Recommendation: Weak. Population: "Adult patients with acute SCI."
  - Recommendation (duration), exact: "We suggest the augmentation of mean arterial pressure for a duration of 3-7 days in order to optimize spinal cord perfusion in acute traumatic spinal cord injury." Quality of Evidence: Very Low. Strength of Recommendation: Weak.
  - Pressor choice: no recommendation. "89% of the GDG agreed that this decision should be made by the treating physician," because the choice depends on "the presence of a central line, concurrent cardiopulmonary dysfunction and co-morbidities."
  - Upper limit clarification: it applies only to active augmentation. "the GDG is not suggesting ... treating MAP above 90-95 with anti-hypertensives, but rather avoiding medical augmentation of MAP above 90-95." Example: a spontaneous BP of 125/80 (MAP 95) needs no treatment.

### Inferences
- For a US handout, the most current society position on MAP is the AO Spine/Praxis 2024 range: 75-80 lower, 90-95 upper, for 3-7 days, weak/very low. It sits uneasily with the 2025 RCT, which found no neurologic benefit and more harm from >85-90 versus >65-70 (section 5). Expect guideline revision and institutional protocol drift toward the lower end of the range.

### Gaps
- No 2025-2026 GRADE guideline update was found that incorporates the Sajdeya 2025 RCT.
- No AANS/CNS or EAST statement on MAP targets dated 2024-2026 was found this session (EAST was not searched).
- CASPER (CSF drainage) results were not found; Neal 2026 says the trial "has been completed."

## 4. Neurogenic shock: definition, incidence, pressor choice, fluids, bradycardia drugs and doses

### Takeaway
Neurogenic shock is hypotension with relative bradycardia from loss of sympathetic tone. It is mainly seen in cervical and high-thoracic injuries and occurs in roughly 1 in 5 acute SCI. No guideline mandates a specific pressor. The AO Spine 2024 review notes that dopamine causes more cardiac complications than phenylephrine (especially in older patients) and raises intrathecal pressure (lowering SCPP). It also notes that no human SCI data exist for norepinephrine. For SCI bradycardia, the ACC/AHA/HRS 2018 guideline gives atropine and methylxanthines (aminophylline/theophylline) Class IIa, with SCI-specific doses, and temporary pacing IIa for refractory cases.

### Cited Findings
- Definition and incidence [verified, via Ryken 2013]: Guly et al. defined neurogenic shock as SBP <100 mm Hg and HR <80 bpm, with an incidence of 19.3% (95% CI 14.8-23.7) in 490 acute SCI. Bradycardia was universal in severe cervical SCI (Lehmann data, section 1). Arrhythmias and hypotension are concentrated in the first 14 days.
- Mechanism and level [recalled]: loss of sympathetic outflow (T1-L2), with cardiac accelerator fibers at T1-T4. Classic teaching is that hemodynamically significant neurogenic shock usually occurs with injuries at or above about T6. Hemorrhagic shock must be excluded first in trauma.
- Pressor evidence summarized in Kwon 2024 [verified: full text]:
  - Inoue et al.: cardiovascular complications "more common with dopamine compared to phenylephrine," "particularly remarkable in older patients." Readdy et al. found similar results in central cord syndrome and penetrating SCI.
  - "while many clinicians use norepinephrine to support MAP, there were no studies within our systematic review that evaluated the safety or effectiveness of this vasopressor in patients with SCI."
  - In a crossover within a CSF-pressure trial, dopamine produced "a MAP-independent increase in intrathecal pressure, thus reducing SCPP," suggesting "an advantage from an SCPP standpoint to use norepinephrine over dopamine."
  - In a pig model (Streijger), norepinephrine was better than phenylephrine at restoring cord perfusion. Williams found dobutamine better than norepinephrine at restoring cardiac output and cord oxygenation ("cardio-centric approach").
  - UCSF series: adverse events in 74.0%-94.0% of patients when pressors or inotropes were used for MAP >=85, mostly cardiogenic (bradycardia, AF/flutter, VT, troponin rise, ST changes). Weinberg found complications were tied to vasopressor use rather than to the MAP level itself.
- Phenylephrine reflex bradycardia [recalled, pharmacology]: phenylephrine is a pure alpha-1 agonist without beta-1 chronotropy. It can worsen bradycardia (baroreflex) in cervical/high-thoracic SCI. Norepinephrine (alpha-1 plus beta-1) or dopamine are often preferred when bradycardia coexists. Phenylephrine is reasonable for lower thoracic injuries with isolated vasodilation. This is common teaching; no guideline text verified this session states it.
- Fluids [verified/recalled mix]: Kwon 2024 describes MAP augmentation "through volume resuscitation and the use of vasopressors and inotropes," and Squair used "fluid volume enhancement followed by the use of vasopressors" [verified]. Specific limits (for example, avoiding large crystalloid volumes once euvolemic because of pulmonary edema risk in denervated vasculature and reduced cardiac contractility) [recalled; no numeric limit found in any verified source].
- Bradycardia guideline: Kusumoto FM, Schoenfeld MH, Barrett C, et al. 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Guideline on ... Bradycardia and Cardiac Conduction Delay: Executive Summary. Circulation. 2019;140(8):e333-e381. doi:10.1161/CIR.0000000000000627 (also J Am Coll Cardiol 2019;74(7):932-987, PMID 30412710; full guideline J Am Coll Cardiol 2019;74(7):e51-e156, PMID 30412709). [verified: PDF text](https://ern-ithaca.eu/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Kusumoto_MD_heartritm2_Circul2019.pdf)
  - **COR IIa, LOE C-LD:** "In patients with SND associated with symptoms or hemodynamic compromise in the setting of acute spinal cord injury, aminophylline or theophylline is reasonable to increase heart rate and improve symptoms." This is Class IIa, not IIb.
  - **COR IIa, C-LD:** "In patients with SND associated with symptoms or hemodynamic compromise, atropine is reasonable to increase sinus rate."
  - **COR IIa, C-LD:** "In patients with persistent hemodynamically unstable SND refractory to medical therapy, temporary transvenous pacing is reasonable ... until a permanent pacemaker is placed or the bradycardia resolves."

  - Perrine JA, et al. J Pharm Pract. 2023;36(3):579-583. doi:10.1177/08971900211064701. PMID 35050815: n=26; 65.4% responders (pressor discontinued or fewer episodes).

- Isoproterenol is listed in the ACC/AHA table [verified]. No SCI-specific isoproterenol study was retrieved.

### Inferences
- Practical hierarchy consistent with the verified sources: treat hypotension (after excluding hemorrhage) with volume, then a vasopressor. Choose an agent with beta-1 activity (norepinephrine; dopamine is a second choice given its complication and SCPP signals) when bradycardia coexists. Use atropine for acute symptomatic bradycardia, and consider aminophylline or theophylline (Class IIa) for recurrent or refractory SCI bradycardia, then pacing.
- Theophylline levels: the guideline notes efficacy often occurs below 10-20 mcg/mL. Pharmacists should monitor for interactions (CYP1A2) and seizures rather than chase traditional ranges [inference; interaction point recalled].

### Gaps
- No RCT comparing norepinephrine, phenylephrine, or dopamine in human SCI exists per Kwon 2024.
- The Inoue (dopamine vs phenylephrine) primary paper was not read directly; its effect sizes were not extracted.
- No verified numeric fluid limit.

## 5. MAP augmentation trials 2020-2026 and ongoing trials (ClinicalTrials.gov)

### Takeaway
The only completed MAP-target RCT with published results is a US multicenter trial (JAMA Netw Open 2025). It found no neurologic benefit of an augmented MAP (>85-90) versus conventional (>65-70) for 7 days, and found more organ dysfunction, longer ventilation, and twice the respiratory complications. The trial was underpowered (n=92, 38 completers). The Canadian MAPS trial was terminated at n=17. A 3-arm US RCT (MAP >=65 vs 85-90 vs SCPP >=65) is recruiting.

### Cited Findings
- Sajdeya R, Yanez ND, Kampp M, et al. Early Blood Pressure Targets in Acute Spinal Cord Injury: A Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA Netw Open. 2025 Sep 2;8(9):e2525364 (article number inferred from the DOI). doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.25364. PMID 40965887; PMC12447234. NCT02878850. [verified: abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40965887/)
  - Design: 13 large US trauma centers, Oct 3 2017 to Jul 26 2023, adults >=18. Randomized 1:1 to augmented MAP (>85-90 mm Hg) or conventional (>65-70 mm Hg) "for 7 days or until intensive care unit discharge." Primary end point: change in ASIA motor and sensory scores to 6 mo.
  - n=92 (mean age 53.78; 83% male). At 6 mo, 38 completed follow-up and 15 had died.

- The 2025 RCT is the first randomized signal, though underpowered with heavy attrition. It points toward harm (respiratory/organ dysfunction) without benefit at MAP >85-90. This supports the AO Spine 2024 move to a lower floor (75-80) and a firm upper cap (90-95). Pharmacists should anticipate more vasopressor-related complications at higher targets.

## 6. Steroids: NASCIS II and III, Fehlings 2017 meta-analysis, complications, current US position

### Takeaway
NASCIS II's benefit was confined to a post hoc subgroup treated within 8 h (6-month motor change 16.0 vs 11.2). NASCIS III added a 48-h arm with more severe sepsis and pneumonia. AANS/CNS 2013 says methylprednisolone is "not recommended" (Level I), citing harms and lack of FDA approval. AO Spine 2017 weakly "suggests" a 24-h infusion within 8 h as an option. In US practice, high-dose MPSS is not standard of care; where used, it requires shared decision-making.

### Cited Findings
- **NASCIS II:** Bracken MB, Shepard MJ, Collins WF, et al. N Engl J Med. 1990;322(20):1405-1411. doi:10.1056/NEJM199005173222001. PMID 2278545 [verified: abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2278545/).
  - Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; 95% treated within 14 h.
  - MP (n=162): "bolus of 30 mg per kilogram of body weight, followed by infusion at 5.4 mg per kilogram per hour for 23 hours." Naloxone (n=154): 5.4 mg/kg then 4.0 mg/kg/h x 23 h. Placebo n=171.
  - At 6 mo, MP within 8 h vs placebo: motor 16.0 vs 11.2 (P=0.03); pinprick 11.4 vs 6.6 (P=0.02); touch 8.9 vs 4.3 (P=0.03). MP after 8 h and naloxone showed no difference. "Mortality and major morbidity were similar in all three groups."
  - Bolus over 15 min, 45-min pause, then 23-h infusion [recalled: standard NASCIS II protocol description; NOT in the abstract or the 2017 guideline text read this session].
- **NASCIS III:** Bracken MB, Shepard MJ, Holford TR, et al. JAMA. 1997;277(20):1597-1604. PMID 9168289 [verified: abstract]. DOI [recalled]: 10.1001/jama.1997.03540440031029.
  - n=499 within 8 h; all received a 30 mg/kg bolus, then MP 5.4 mg/kg/h x 24 h (n=166), MP x 48 h (n=167), or tirilazad 2.5 mg/kg q6h x 48 h (n=166).
  - 48 h vs 24 h overall: P=.09 (6 wk), P=.07 (6 mo). In the 3-8 h subgroup: P=.04 and P=.01, more likely to improve one full grade (P=.03), and "more severe sepsis and severe pneumonia." Mortality P=.97.
  - Authors' conclusion: start within 3 h, continue 24 h; start at 3-8 h, continue 48 h. **SUPERSEDED.**
- AANS/CNS 2013 critique of NASCIS III [verified: Hurlbert chapter]: 2x severe pneumonia, 4x severe sepsis, and "6 times more deaths observed in the 48-hour group due to pneumonia, respiratory distress syndrome, and respiratory failure (P = .056)." The NASCIS III benefit was "discovered only in a post-hoc analysis of a partial dataset" and was "downgraded to Class III."
- Harms text [verified: Hurlbert chapter]: "Harmful side effects ... significant in 3 Class I studies, including wound infection, hyperglycemia requiring insulin administration, and GI hemorrhage. Although not statistically significant, similar trends were observed in ... NASCIS II and III, including GI hemorrhage, sepsis, pneumonia, and death due to respiratory failure." Class II evidence showed a higher risk of "infection (respiratory, urinary, wound) and steroid-induced myopathy."
- CRASH (TBI) data cited in the chapter [verified]: 2-week mortality with MP 21.1% vs 17.9% (RR 1.18, 95% CI 1.09-1.27); "for every 29 patients treated with MP, 1 died." This matters for SCI with concomitant TBI.
- **Fehlings 2017 systematic review/meta-analysis:** Fehlings MG, Wilson JR, Harrop JS, et al. Global Spine J. 2017;7(3 Suppl):116S-137S. doi:10.1177/2192568217706366. PMID 29164020; PMC5684849 [verified: abstract]. Found "moderate evidence that the 24-hour NASCIS II ... regimen has no impact on long-term neurological recovery when all postinjury time points are considered." Within 8 h, there was "an additional 3.2 points (95% CI 0.10 to 6.33; P=.04) of motor recovery." The regimen "should be considered a treatment option."
- AO Spine 2017 recommendations: exact text in section 2 [verified].
- Practice data: 53.6% of global AO Spine respondents use steroids, more in LMICs [verified, PMID 36036628]. In the ASAP polytrauma survey, 60.2% do not use them [verified, PMID 35468806].

### Inferences
- US position (as of Nov 2026): the AANS/CNS Level I "not recommended" (2013) has not been replaced by any US neurosurgical statement found. The AO Spine 2017 weak option within 8 h exists but rests on a post hoc 8-h cut point (chosen after data collection, per the 2017 guideline text). Most US trauma centers do not give high-dose MPSS routinely [inference; no US-only practice survey was found this session].
- If MPSS is used (24-h regimen, within 8 h only), pharmacy issues include hyperglycemia/insulin, GI bleed prophylaxis, infection surveillance, and avoiding use in concomitant moderate-severe TBI (CRASH) [inference from verified harms data].

### Gaps
- No 2024-2026 update of the steroid recommendation was found from AO Spine or AANS/CNS.
- No US-specific steroid-utilization data dated 2020-2026 was found.

## 7. Neuroprotective drug trials (riluzole, minocycline, VX-210/Cethrin, hypothermia, GM-1)

### Takeaway
No neuroprotective drug has guideline endorsement. RISCIS (riluzole 100 mg BID x 24 h, then 50 mg BID x 13 days) missed its primary end point after early termination at 55% enrollment, though post hoc and global analyses favor riluzole. AO Spine 2026 makes no recommendation. Minocycline, hypothermia, and GM-1 lack demonstrated efficacy; GM-1 is "not recommended" (Level I, AANS/CNS 2013).

### Cited Findings
- **RISCIS:** Fehlings MG, Moghaddamjou A, Harrop JS, et al. J Neurotrauma. 2023;40(17-18):1878-1888. doi:10.1089/neu.2023.0163. PMID 37279301; PMC10460693; NCT01597518 [verified: abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37279301/).
  - Population: AIS A-C, C4-C8, <12 h from injury (ages 18-75 per Neal 2026).
  - Dose: "100 mg twice per day (BID) for the first 24 h followed by 50 mg BID for the following 13 days."
  - Enrollment: 193 of 351 planned; halted for COVID-19.
  - Primary (completed cases): UEMS +1.76 (95% CI -2.54 to 6.06); total motor +2.86 (-6.79 to 12.52). "The primary analysis did not achieve the predetermined end-point." "No drug-related serious adverse events."
  - AEs [verified, Neal 2026]: 1722 AEs with riluzole (9 deaths, 110 SAEs) vs 1786 with placebo (10 deaths, 132 SAEs).
- RISCIS global statistical test: Fehlings MG, Pedro KM, et al. EBioMedicine. 2025;118:105863. doi:10.1016/j.ebiom.2025.105863. PMID 40712181 [verified: abstract]. n=131; GST rank sum 207 vs 185 (p=0.04); 58% probability of better overall outcome with riluzole.
- RISCIS PK sub-study: Chow DS, et al. J Neurotrauma. 2023;40(17-18):1889-1906. doi:10.1089/neu.2022.0499. PMID 37130044 [verified: abstract]. Time-varying clearance and volume of distribution after SCI; "No appreciable hepatic toxicity."
- AO Spine 2026 (Neal) [verified]: "we are not able to make a recommendation on the use of riluzole in patients with acute cervical tSCI"; the RISCIS evidence was rated GRADE "moderate."
- Minocycline:
  - Fahim F, et al. Arch Acad Emerg Med. 2026;14(1):e46. doi:10.22037/aaem.v14i1.2983. PMID 42836077 [verified: abstract]: pooled OR 1.70 (95% CI 0.95-3.06) for neurologic improvement (2 clinical studies), not significant.
  - MASC Phase III, NCT01828203: status UNKNOWN; planned n=248 [verified: registry]. No primary publication was found.
- Hypothermia: Vedantam A, Levi AD. Neurosurg Clin N Am. 2021;32(3):377-387. doi:10.1016/j.nec.2021.03.009. PMID 34053725 [verified: abstract]. Modest systemic hypothermia was "safe and feasible," and a multicenter RCT is under way. 80% of ASAP respondents do not use it [verified].
- VX-210 (Cethrin, Rho inhibitor applied to dura at surgery): Phase 2b/3 SPRING trial stopped for futility [recalled: NCT02669849; Fehlings MG et al. J Neurotrauma 2021; NOT verified].
- GM-1: Level I "not recommended" (AANS/CNS 2013) [verified].
- Reviews: Sherrod BA, et al. Clin Spine Surg. 2024;37(9):433-439. doi:10.1097/BSD.0000000000001695. PMID 39264675. An KD, et al. Korean J Neurotrauma. 2025;21(4):237-254. PMID 41220890: "preventing any drug from achieving guideline-level endorsement" [verified: abstracts].

### Inferences
- Riluzole is FDA-approved only for ALS [verified via Pedro 2025 abstract, PMID 41117139]. Use in SCI is off-label and should be confined to protocols or trials pending a confirmatory trial. If used, the first 24-h loading dose is 100 mg BID via enteral route [verified dose].

### Gaps
- The VX-210 result and the RECOVERY hypothermia trial status were not verified.
- No confirmatory riluzole RCT registration was found (not searched specifically).

## 8. VTE prophylaxis in acute SCI (CSCM 2016; AO Spine 2017; AANS/CNS 2013; CHEST)

### Takeaway
LMWH is the preferred agent. Start within 72 h once bleeding risk allows (AANS/CNS Level II; AO Spine 2017 "suggest"). Combine with mechanical compression early. Avoid adjusted-dose UFH, warfarin, and IVC filters as primary prophylaxis. CSCM 2016 says continue for at least 8 weeks in patients with limited mobility; AANS/CNS 2013 says 3 months (Level II). Enoxaparin 30 mg q12h and 40 mg daily are trial regimens, not formally graded doses.

### Cited Findings
- Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine. Prevention of Venous Thromboembolism in Individuals with Spinal Cord Injury: Clinical Practice Guidelines for Health Care Providers, 3rd ed. Top Spinal Cord Inj Rehabil. 2016;22(3):209-240. doi:10.1310/sci2203-209. PMID 29339863; PMC4981016. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4981016/). [secondary: PMC full text read via fetch-tool summary of about the first 100k of 205k characters; the wording below is a close paraphrase, not verbatim]
  - 1.0 (1C): intermittent pneumatic compression with or without graduated stockings as early as feasible, unless lower-extremity injury precludes it.
  - 2.0: LMWH in the acute care phase once there is no active bleeding (grade not captured). 2.1 (1C): if LMWH is delayed for bleeding, reassess bleeding risk daily.
  - 3.0 (1B): recommend against low-dose or adjusted-dose UFH unless LMWH is unavailable or contraindicated.
  - 4.0 (1C): no warfarin or VKA in the early acute phase. 5.0 (2C): DOACs may be considered in the rehabilitation phase, not early.
  - 6.0 (2C): combine mechanical and anticoagulant prophylaxis in acute care.
  - 7.0 (1C): anticoagulant prophylaxis for **at least 8 weeks** with limited mobility (at least until discharge from inpatient rehabilitation). Longer for motor complete injury, LE fracture, older age, prior VTE, cancer, or obesity.
  - 8.0 (1C): no IVC filter as primary prophylaxis. 9.0/9.1 (2B): no routine duplex screening.
  - 10.0 (2C): mechanical prophylaxis for children. 10.1 (1C): anticoagulant prophylaxis for adolescents.
  - Dosing: no formal dose recommendation. The text cites enoxaparin 40 mg daily as similar to 30 mg BID in a retrospective cohort; UFH 5,000 units q8h appears only as a trial comparator. DVT can appear as early as 72 h, and over 80% of DVTs occur within 2 weeks without prophylaxis.
- AO Spine 2017: suggest LMWH or fixed low-dose UFH, start within 72 h, and suggest against adjusted-dose UFH (exact text in section 2) [verified]. It conflicts with CSCM 3.0, which recommends against low-dose UFH unless LMWH is unavailable.
- AANS/CNS 2013: Level I LMWH or rotating beds or a combination; Level II within 72 h; Level II 3 months (exact text in section 1) [verified].
- CHEST: [recalled] the CHEST 2012 (AT9) nonorthopedic surgery guideline suggests pharmacologic prophylaxis after acute SCI once hemostasis is established. CHEST 2016/2021 VTE treatment updates do not address SCI prophylaxis specifically. NOT verified this session.
- Enoxaparin regimens [recalled]: 30 mg SC q12h (the classic trauma/SCI regimen) or 40 mg SC daily. Many trauma centers adjust by weight or anti-Xa level. Hold around surgery: the Dhall abstract says hold "1 day prior to and 1 day following surgical intervention" [secondary].

### Inferences
- The "8 to 12 weeks" phrase in the request matches older practice summaries. In the verified sources, CSCM 2016 says "at least 8 weeks" and AANS/CNS 2013 says "3-month duration." A handout could say "at least 8 weeks (CSCM 2016) to 3 months (AANS/CNS 2013 Level II)."

### Gaps
- The exact CSCM 2016 recommendation 2.0 wording and grade, and any formal timing recommendation in the unread half of the document, were not verified.
- No CSCM 4th edition (2017-2026) was found; existence was not checked.
- The CHEST SCI text was not verified.

## 9. Pharmacy pearls (succinylcholine, stress ulcer prophylaxis, bowel, autonomic dysreflexia, peripheral vasopressors, glucose)

### Takeaway
Succinylcholine is generally considered safe only in the first day or two after SCI and should be avoided from about day 3 onward for months because of acetylcholine-receptor upregulation. Vasopressor choice and route are left to the clinician; MAP targets need arterial-line monitoring and often central access. Steroid use adds GI bleed, hyperglycemia, and infection risk. Autonomic dysreflexia is a later-phase emergency in injuries at or above T6.

### Cited Findings
- **Succinylcholine** [secondary]: OpenAnesthesia states the hyperkalemia risk after burns, SCI, and stroke peaks at 7-10 days, with "minimal risk within the first two days," and that AChR upregulation lasts up to 2 years. — [OpenAnesthesia](https://www.openanesthesia.org/keywords/succinylcholine/); [emDocs ToxCard](https://www.emdocs.net/toxcard-dangers-of-succinylcholine/). A conflicting source notes potassium rises detectable within hours of denervation. In rat T6 transection, potassium after succinylcholine was elevated at days 10 and 30 [secondary: search summary]. A specific 72-h human threshold was not found. [recalled] The classic review is Martyn JA, Richtsfeld M. Anesthesiology 2006;104:158-169 (safe window often quoted as <24-72 h); rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg is the usual alternative, with sugammadex available for reversal.
- **Monitoring:** AANS/CNS Level III recommends cardiac, hemodynamic, and respiratory monitoring and an ICU setting [verified]. MAP-target titration in practice requires an arterial line [recalled].
- **Central vs peripheral pressors:**
  - Kwon 2024 says pressor choice depends on "the presence of a central line." Central line placement and vasopressor risks (arrhythmia, ischemia) are cited as harms of augmentation [verified].
  - NCT06451133 tracks CLABSI and CVC pneumothorax as outcomes [verified].
  - [recalled] General ICU evidence supports short-term peripheral norepinephrine via a well-functioning proximal IV, 20 G or larger, with low extravasation rates (about 2% in Cardenas-Garcia J Hosp Med 2015;10:581-585). Phenylephrine is often favored peripherally but risks reflex bradycardia in cervical SCI. Extravasation treatment: phentolamine 5-10 mg in 10 mL NS SC. SCI-specific peripheral pressor data were not found.
- **Steroid-related:** hyperglycemia requiring insulin, GI hemorrhage, wound infection, and pneumonia [verified: Hurlbert 2013].
- **Stress ulcer prophylaxis** [recalled]: SCI (especially cervical, mechanically ventilated) has long been listed as a stress-ulcer risk factor (ASHP 1999 therapeutic guideline). Mechanical ventilation and coagulopathy are the dominant risk factors in ICU SUP guidance. Consider SUP while ventilated, in shock, or on high-dose steroids, and stop when risk resolves. No SCI-specific SUP recommendation was verified this session.
- **Bowel** [recalled]: neurogenic bowel and paralytic ileus are common in the first days. Start a scheduled bowel program early: stool softener or osmotic agent plus a stimulant suppository (bisacodyl) daily, timed for reflex evacuation in upper motor neuron injuries. Opioids worsen ileus. Reference to verify: Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine neurogenic bowel guideline (Johns J et al. Top Spinal Cord Inj Rehabil 2021;27(2):75-151; not verified).
- **Autonomic dysreflexia** [recalled]: occurs with lesions at or above T6, usually after spinal shock resolves (weeks), occasionally earlier. Triggers are bladder distension and bowel impaction. Management: sit upright, loosen clothing, check the catheter and bowel. If SBP stays at or above about 150 mm Hg, give a rapid-acting short-duration antihypertensive (for example, nitroglycerin paste, avoided if a PDE5 inhibitor was used in the prior 24-48 h, or immediate-release nifedipine). Reference: CSCM Autonomic Dysreflexia guideline (2nd ed 2001; updated edition about 2020; not verified).
- **Theophylline pearls:** SCI efficacy often occurs at levels below 10-20 mcg/mL [verified: ACC/AHA Table 8]. Watch CYP1A2 interactions such as ciprofloxacin [recalled].
- **Riluzole (if on a protocol):** 100 mg BID x 24 h, then 50 mg BID x 13 days, enteral [verified]. PK changes over time after SCI; no appreciable hepatotoxicity in the PK sub-study [verified].
- **Bradycardia triggers** [recalled]: tracheal suctioning and hypoxia provoke vagal bradycardia and asystole in cervical SCI. Pre-oxygenate, and consider pre-treating with atropine or glycopyrrolate.

### Inferences
- A pressor plan for ER pharmacists: confirm the MAP goal (for example, 75-80 floor, do not augment above 90-95). If bradycardic (cervical or high thoracic), favor norepinephrine over phenylephrine. If no central line, run peripheral norepinephrine short-term per institutional policy while access is obtained. Have atropine ready for suctioning or intubation.

### Gaps
- Most pearls in this section are [recalled] and need verification against primary sources: the Martyn 2006 review, the CSCM bowel and AD guidelines, SCCM SUP 2024, and peripheral vasopressor cohorts.

## 10. Pediatric SCI and SCIWORA (brief)

### Takeaway
This handout is adult-focused. For children, CSCM 2016 suggests mechanical VTE prophylaxis for all ages and recommends anticoagulant prophylaxis for adolescents. SCIWORA (SCI without radiographic abnormality) occurs mainly in young children and is an MRI-era diagnosis.

### Cited Findings
- CSCM 2016: 10.0 (2C) mechanical prophylaxis for children of all ages; 10.1 (1C) anticoagulant prophylaxis for adolescents, especially with LE or pelvic fractures [secondary: fetch summary of PMC4981016].
- Theophylline was used for cervical SCI bradycardia in a 7-year-old (case report). Karim F, et al. Cureus. 2020;12(10):e10941. doi:10.7759/cureus.10941. PMID 33200055 [verified: abstract].
- SCIWORA [recalled]: term from Pang and Wilberger (J Neurosurg 1982). It occurs predominantly in children under about 8 years because of ligamentous laxity. AANS/CNS 2013 has dedicated chapters on pediatric cervical injury and SCIWORA (Neurosurgery 2013;72 Suppl 2; Rozzelle et al.), recommending MRI of the region of suspected injury. Not verified this session.

### Inferences
- None beyond the above.
