- Patanwala AE, Hays DP. Pharmacist's activities on a trauma response team in the emergency department. Am J Health Syst Pharm. 2010;67(18):1536-8. doi:10.2146/ajhp090311. PMID 20811031. Level I academic center, ED pharmacist 40 h/wk; 2-month period; 304 interventions; dosage recommendations 60%, drug information 27%; top classes analgesics, sedatives, antimicrobials, vaccines, fluids; pharmacist facilitated drug administration in 83% of interventions. [verified: abstract]
- Montgomery K, Hall AB, Keriazes G. Pharmacist's impact on acute pain management during trauma resuscitation. J Trauma Nurs. 2015;22(2):87-90. doi:10.1097/JTN.0000000000000112. PMID 25768964. Level II center, 2009-2013, 340 patients receiving IV opioid in trauma bay; mean time to first analgesic 17 vs 21 min with vs without pharmacist (P=.03); pain-score reduction similar (2.4 vs 2.7 points). [verified: abstract]
- Porter AL, Zaeem M, Hewes A, Hale S, Jones C, Gestring ML (first six authors listed). Pharmacist involvement in trauma resuscitation across the United States: a 10-year follow-up survey. Am J Health Syst Pharm. 2019;76(16):1226-1230. doi:10.1093/ajhp/zxz124. PMID 31369114. Survey March 2017, response 33% (110/335 ACS trauma centers); pharmacist involvement rose from 23% (2007) to 70% (2017) (p<0.001); 71% of these provided by ED-based pharmacists; bedside roles: calculating doses 96%, preparing medications 89%, medication information 79%; administrative roles 45% (education, med safety, QI, ACS accreditation prep); main reason for no pharmacist: unfamiliarity with role/benefits. [verified: abstract]
- Masic D, Hidalgo DC, Kuhrau S, Chaney W, Rech MA. Pharmacist presence decreases time to prothrombin complex concentrate in emergency department patients with life-threatening bleeding and urgent procedures. J Emerg Med. 2019;57(5):620-628. doi:10.1016/j.jemermed.2019.06.027. PMID 31447188. Retrospective 2014-2018; 116 included (PharmD n=50, control n=66); 68.1% warfarin; ICH 67.2%; median time to 4F-PCC 66.5 vs 206.5 min (p<0.001); pharmacist at bedside only independent predictor (beta -163.5 min, 95% CI -249.4 to -77.7); no difference in hemostasis or mortality; ICU LOS 2 vs 5 d (p<0.01); hospital LOS 5.5 vs 8 d (p=0.02). [verified: abstract]
- Korsch, Knapp. Improving massive transfusion protocol response in the rural Level III trauma center setting. J Trauma Nurs. 2026;33(1):60-66. doi:10.1097/JTN.0000000000000897. PMID 41505189. QI at 2 rural Level III centers Aug 2023-Feb 2024; 35 respondents across 8 MTP activations incl 5 pharmacists (14%); appropriate calcium administration 87.5% of cases; barriers: inaccurate paging 50%, communication 62.5%, rapid infuser issues 50%. [verified: abstract]

- Mercer K, Ofoegbuna I, Razzack H, Feinstein J, Gonzalez M, et al. Presence of emergency medicine pharmacists is associated with shorter time to administration of prothrombin complex concentrate administration regardless of dosing strategy. Am J Emerg Med. 2025;95:147-152. doi:10.1016/j.ajem.2025.05.032. PMID 40450951. Single center 2016-2022; 147 pts (94 weight-based, 47 fixed-dose); fixed-dose without pharmacist 58.5 vs weight-based 71 min; fixed-dose WITH pharmacist 21 vs 58.5 min without (p<0.0001), i.e., 37.5 min faster; pharmacist presence not associated with less protocol-discordant use (p=0.0783). [verified: abstract]
- Corio JL, Sin JH, Hayes BD, Goldstein JN, Fuh L. Impact of a pharmacist-driven prothrombin complex concentrate protocol on time to administration in patients with warfarin-associated intracranial hemorrhage. Pharmacist approves/doses/prepares/delivers 4F-PCC (previously blood bank). n=48 (24 vs 24); median time to 4F-PCC 35 min (IQR 25-62) vs 70 min (IQR 34-89) pre-protocol (p=0.034); no difference in dosing appropriateness. [verified: abstract]

- Santiago R, Slocum G, Riggi G. Resuscitating massive transfusion knowledge: an update for the pharmacist. Am J Health Syst Pharm. 2025;82(24):1314-1327. doi:10.1093/ajhp/zxaf164. PMID 40580496. [verified: full text searched via pdftotext]
  - The word "pharmacist/pharmacy" does NOT appear anywhere in the 2022 Standards (revised July 2025): grep count 0. There is NO ACS requirement that a pharmacist respond to trauma activations at Level I or II. [verified]
  - Standard 5.8 Massive Transfusion Protocol, TYPE I, all levels (LI, LII, LIII, PTCI, PTCII): "All trauma centers must have a massive transfusion protocol (MTP) that is developed collaboratively between the trauma service and the blood bank." Additional info: includes a trigger for activation, a process for cessation, and strategies for preservation of unused blood; appropriate clotting studies immediately available. [verified]
  - Standard 5.9 Anticoagulation Reversal Protocol, TYPE II, all levels: "All trauma centers must have a rapid reversal protocol in place for patients on anticoagulants." Additional info: "The protocol should include therapeutic options and indications for the use of each reversal agent." Measure of compliance: the rapid reversal protocol. [verified]
  - Standard 5.6 Care Protocols for the Injured Older Adult, TYPE II, LI and LII: protocols must include identification of vulnerable geriatric patients, geriatric-expertise input, delirium/dementia/depression, goals of care, "Medication reconciliation and avoidance of inappropriate medications", mobility, safe transitions. Geriatric provider liaison required at LI/LII. [verified]
  - Standard 5.10 Pediatric Readiness, TYPE II, all levels: each ED must perform a pediatric readiness assessment during the verification cycle (pedsready.org) and have a plan to address gaps. [verified]
  - Verification consequence: noncompliance with any Type I standard = not verified; up to 3 Type II deficiencies = verified with 1-year certificate (per standards text). [verified]
