## Q1. Analgesia: rib fractures, multimodal opioid-sparing care, older adults, TBI

### Takeaway
No US trauma society gives a strong, high-certainty analgesia recommendation. EAST 2016 "conditionally recommend[s]" epidural and multimodal analgesia for blunt thoracic trauma. EAST/CWIS 2023 (older adults) "offer[s] no recommendation for or against ketamine, epidural, or other locoregional analgesia". The ACS TQIP Acute Pain Best Practices (Nov 2020; ungraded consensus) is the most practical US source. It says scheduled acetaminophen plus an NSAID unless contraindicated, analgesic-dose ketamine and/or fentanyl first-line in unstable ED patients, short early NSAID courses do not raise nonunion risk, NSAIDs are generally avoided in TBI/ICH, and gabapentinoids have limited broad benefit. Among RCTs, ketamine infusion missed its primary pain endpoint in rib fractures (Carver 2019). Serratus anterior plane block improved early pain and roughly halved 24-h opioid use in an ED pragmatic RCT (SABRE 2024), without a change in pneumonia or mortality.

- **ACS TQIP Best Practices Guidelines for Acute Pain Management in Trauma Patients.** American College of Surgeons Trauma Quality Programs. "Released November 2020". "Supported by the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Administrative Council". It is a consensus document; no GRADE strength labels were seen in the sections read. Exact quotes [verified: full PDF downloaded from facs.org and read for the sections below] — [ACS PDF](https://www.facs.org/media/exob3dwk/acute_pain_guidelines.pdf):
  - Pharmacologic key points: "Include acetaminophen (APAP) and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) in pain management unless contraindicated." "Overreliance on opioids, specifically as monotherapy for analgesia, may contribute to opioid dependence." Acetaminophen: "Scheduled dosing is ideal". IV vs oral analgesic effects are similar; IV can cause hypotension; avoid in acute liver failure; reduce doses in older adults and chronic liver disease.
  - Ketamine: "Subdissociative- and subanesthetic-dose ketamine, also called analgesic-dose ketamine, is generally accepted as any dose under 0.3–0.5 mg/kg." It "offers analgesia without compromising respiratory status or inducing hypotension." Use "with caution in patients with a significant cardiovascular history". On TBI: "Historically, a concern existed for increased intracranial pressure (ICP) following the use of ketamine in patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI); however, recent reviews demonstrate safety when using ketamine for this population."
  - NSAIDs, bleeding: "all NSAIDs have antiplatelet activity contributing to an increased risk of bleeding at any site"; COX-2 selective agents have "decreased risk of inhibiting platelet aggregation". "Prophylactic use of preoperative ketorolac is contraindicated due to bleeding risk. Weigh the risk and impact of bleeding against the benefit of analgesia on a case-by-case basis." NSAIDs "require caution when used in coagulopathic patients or those otherwise at risk for stress-related mucosal bleeding."
  - NSAIDs, TBI: "While no high-quality evidence exists regarding the use of NSAIDs in the presence of TBI or intracranial hemorrhage, most practitioners avoid the use of both COX-1 and COX-2 inhibitors in this setting."
  - NSAIDs, fracture and spine fusion: "Recent literature demonstrates that short-term, early NSAID use does not increase risk of fracture nonunion." In spinal fusion, "early short (less than 48 hours) administration" has no significant deleterious effect. "Avoid longer term (2 weeks or longer) use of NSAIDs in the setting of fracture."
  - NSAIDs, kidney: scheduled NSAIDs "may contribute to or complicate the differential diagnosis of acute kidney injury, which develops in at least 30% of patients in ICU settings."
  - Opioids: "Generally, administer opioids orally unless the specific clinical circumstance warrants parenteral administration." Extended-release and transdermal products "are not appropriate for treatment of acute pain and should not be used. Avoid specific products such as meperidine and codeine." A bowel regimen "containing a stimulant laxative is recommended for all patients receiving opioids unless contraindicated." Taper "with a desired goal of no opioid therapy upon hospital discharge in patients who were opioid-naïve". Query the PDMP before prescribing. Avoid combining opioids with other CNS depressants outside monitored settings.
  - Gabapentinoids: opioid-sparing in select groups (SCI, burn), but "limited evidence exists to support a broad analgesic effect". They "produce additive CNS depression and increase risk of death when used in combination with opioids". Both are renally eliminated, so use lower doses or avoid in renal disease and in elderly patients.
  - Lidocaine: in a high-quality THA RCT, "no difference in pain scores or opioid requirements" with perioperative lidocaine infusion; data in trauma "are lacking". "Do not administer a continuous infusion of lidocaine to patients with a regional block when also infusing an amide anesthetic (e.g., ropivacaine or bupivacaine) due to the risk of local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)."
  - Muscle relaxants: "Methocarbamol use in particular is associated with a decreased hospital length of stay in trauma patients with closed rib fractures".
  - Regional key points: "Use continuous regional analgesia instead of a single injection technique when pain is expected to last beyond 12 hours." "For thoracic and abdominal pain, epidural and paravertebral blocks are comparably effective and provide superior analgesia compared to systemic opioids." Extremity blocks are "not contraindicated, but [may] potentially mask compartment syndrome."
  - Regional table: serratus anterior (T2-9; chest wall trauma) "Case reports suggest may facilitate extubation". Erector spinae (T2-L3) "Improved pain control and incentive spirometry, no effect on opioid consumption". Quadratus lumborum is "considered a deep plexus block"; follow neuraxial bleeding and VTE-prophylaxis rules.
  - ED key points: "Medication effects may be magnified in hemodynamically unstable patients. Consider analgesic-dose ketamine and/or fentanyl as first-line options for unstable patients with severe pain." Ultrasound-guided nerve blocks are effective. Patients with chronic opioid use or SUD have greater needs, and "providers should not withhold opioids solely due to a history of SUD." Fentanyl has "minimal effects on hemodynamic status", but its short half-life needs frequent reassessment.
  - ICU key points: "Use opioids as first-line agents for patients with severe, acute nociceptive pain in the ICU setting as part of an MMA pain management plan." Sedation is a "secondary target, typically with benzodiazepine-sparing regimens. Light-to-moderate levels of sedation, along with daily sedation holidays when appropriate".
  - Older adults: "Because older adults are more sensitive to sedating, gastric irritation, and anticholinergic effects of medications, starting doses need to be empirically reduced." Hepatic clearance may fall "by up to 40%". Serum creatinine may underestimate GFR decline. Opioid and benzodiazepine respiratory depression is exaggerated. NSAIDs (Table 13): "Avoid chronic use unless alternatives are not available. When used, combine with gastroprotective agent (e.g., PPI or misoprostol)". Acetaminophen maximum is 2,000-3,000 mg/day in older adults.

### Inferences
- For a pharmacist handout, the defensible US "standard" is a scheduled multimodal backbone: acetaminophen plus an NSAID unless contraindicated (bleeding, TBI/ICH, AKI, coagulopathy), with opioids for breakthrough. Add analgesic-dose ketamine or a regional block for rib fractures or opioid-tolerant patients. All the society statements are conditional or consensus. The only strong-sounding US statements (TQIP) are ungraded.
- Ketamine infusion has weak efficacy evidence in rib fractures (Carver: negative primary; EAST/CWIS 2023: trend toward less opioid only). Its main argument is opioid-sparing in severely injured or opioid-tolerant patients, not pain scores.
- Fascial plane blocks (SAPB, ESPB) have growing ED RCT support for short-term pain and opioid reduction. No trial has shown effects on pneumonia or mortality, which matches EAST 2023's "no recommendation". Anticoagulation timing matters mainly for neuraxial and deep-plexus blocks (epidural, paravertebral, QL), not superficial plane blocks [recalled: ASRA treats superficial plane blocks as lower risk].
- In TBI, the main analgesia concerns are oversedation (it masks the neuro exam) and NSAID antiplatelet effects. Fentanyl and analgesic-dose ketamine are the hemodynamically friendly ED options. TQIP endorses ketamine safety in TBI.

### Gaps
- No EAST or AAST guideline titled "Pain management in acute trauma" (2022-2026) was found on PubMed. A "multimodal analgesia trauma guideline" search returned only TQIP-adjacent and OTA/ACP documents. If the handout author has one in mind, it was not located.
- The full-text EAST 2016 PICO-by-PICO wording was not read (paywalled). Only the abstract's combined statement is verified.
- NSAID bleeding outcome data specific to trauma were not retrieved beyond TQIP statements and Drahos (AKI only). Fracture-healing RCT data (for example, ibuprofen vs placebo in fracture healing) were not retrieved.
- Intranasal fentanyl trials in adult trauma were not retrieved; dose is [recalled].
- Lidocaine infusion RCTs in rib fractures were not retrieved; dosing is [recalled] except the TQIP bolus.

## Q2. RSI in trauma: induction agent, shock dosing, neuromuscular blocker, post-intubation analgosedation

### Takeaway
The only trauma-society airway guideline (EAST 2012) is old. It says "There is no pharmacologic induction agent of choice" and recommends succinylcholine for RSI. The 2025/2026 NEJM RSI trial (ketamine vs etomidate, n=2,365) **excluded patients with a primary diagnosis of trauma**, so there is no trauma subgroup. Overall it found no mortality difference (28.1% vs 29.1%) and **more cardiovascular collapse with ketamine** (22.1% vs 17.0%). Three 2026 meta-analyses agree. For hemorrhagic shock, reduced-dose induction (ketamine ~0.5-1 mg/kg or etomidate ~0.15-0.2 mg/kg), resuscitate-before-intubate, rocuronium when succinylcholine is contraindicated, and analgesia-first post-intubation sedation are expert practice, not graded trauma guideline statements.

### Cited Findings
- **EAST 2012 PMG: Emergency tracheal intubation immediately following traumatic injury.** Mayglothling J, Duane TM, Gibbs M, McCunn M, Legome E, Eastman AL, Whelan J, Shah KH. J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2012;73(5 Suppl 4):S333-S340. DOI 10.1097/TA.0b013e31827018a5. PMID 23114490. It updates the 2002 EAST guideline.
  - Methods: 69 articles (2000-2012); 6 Level 1, 4 Level 2 and 2 Level 3 recommendations.
  - Abstract wording: "Rapid sequence intubation with direct laryngoscopy continues to be the recommended method for ETI, although the use of airway adjuncts such as blind insertion supraglottic devices and video laryngoscopy may be useful in facilitating successful ETI and may be preferred in certain patient populations. There is no pharmacologic induction agent of choice for ETI; however, succinylcholine is the neuromuscular blockade agent recommended for rapid sequence intubation." [verified: abstract]
  - [recalled] Level 1 indications include airway obstruction, hypoventilation, severe hypoxemia, GCS ≤8, cardiac arrest and severe hemorrhagic shock. Etomidate was noted as acceptable and hemodynamically stable. Check full text before quoting.
  - Flag: predates the 2023 video-laryngoscopy RCT (DEVICE) and the 2025/2026 RSI trial; likely outdated on device and paralytic preference. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23114490/) / [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/TA.0b013e31827018a5)
- **RSI trial.** Casey JD et al., Pragmatic Critical Care Research Group. "Ketamine or Etomidate for Tracheal Intubation of Critically Ill Adults." N Engl J Med 2026;394(16):1608-1620 (online Dec 2025). DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa2511420. PMID 41369227. PMC12711137. NCT05277896. [verified: PMC full text]
  - Design: pragmatic, unblinded, multicenter (14 sites; 6 EDs, 8 ICUs; 6 US centers), with exception from informed consent.
  - **Exclusion: patients "presenting with a primary diagnosis of trauma"**. Authors' limitation: "Our trial excluded patients presenting with a primary diagnosis of trauma, and the results may not apply to these patients." **No trauma subgroup exists.**
  - Dosing nomograms: ketamine full 2.0, intermediate 1.5, reduced 1.0 mg/kg; etomidate 0.3 / 0.25 / 0.2 mg/kg. Clinician-selected doses: median ketamine 140 mg (1.6 mg/kg, IQR 1.4-2.0); etomidate 20 mg (0.28 mg/kg, IQR 0.24-0.31). Neuromuscular blocker choice was at clinician discretion; 99.7% received one.
  - Population: n=2,365 (ketamine 1,176; etomidate 1,189); median age 60; 46.7% sepsis; 22.0% on vasopressors; 55.7% intubated in the ED.
  - Primary (in-hospital death by day 28): 330/1,173 (28.1%) vs 345/1,186 (29.1%); site-adjusted RD -0.8 points (95% CI -4.5 to 2.9); P=0.65. Sepsis subgroup (n=1,101): 38.8% vs 38.2%. Risk-modeling and effect-modeling analyses found no heterogeneity of effect.

- **2026 meta-analyses incorporating RSI** [verified: abstracts]:
  - Zampieri 2026, Crit Care 30(1); DOI 10.1186/s13054-026-06067-w; PMID 42121165. 9 RCTs, n=4,672. Ketamine vs etomidate mortality OR 0.96 (0.80-1.16; moderate certainty). Ketamine "probably increases cardiovascular collapse (OR 1.44, 1.20-1.71; moderate certainty)". It may increase post-induction hypotension (OR 1.34) and vasopressor use (OR 1.45). No difference in first-pass success or arrest.
  - McDougall 2026, Chest; DOI 10.1016/j.chest.2026.07.5239; PMID 42612901. 21 RCTs, n=6,031. Hemodynamic instability RR 1.31 (1.15-1.48) ketamine vs etomidate. Ketamine-propofol vs etomidate RR 0.44 (0.27-0.72; low certainty). Etomidate increased adrenal suppression.
  - Loughnan 2026, Br J Anaesth; DOI 10.1016/j.bja.2026.02.023; PMID 41951491. 10 RCTs, n=4,673. Instability RR 1.28 (1.13-1.45). Mortality, ventilator-free days and ICU-free days showed no differences.
- **Conflicting observational data.** Maia 2025, JAMA Netw Open 8(12):e2548060; DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.48060; PMID 41396604. Brazilian ED registry target-trial emulation, n=1,810. Weighted 28-day mortality was higher with etomidate (60.5% vs 54.4%; RR 1.14, 1.03-1.27). Hemodynamic instability was higher with ketamine (24.2% vs 18.9%). [verified: abstract]
- **Shock dosing** [recalled]: in hemorrhagic shock, reduce induction doses (ketamine 0.5-1 mg/kg; etomidate 0.1-0.2 mg/kg). Give a full or increased paralytic dose (rocuronium 1.2-1.6 mg/kg; succinylcholine 1.5-2 mg/kg), because slowed circulation delays onset. Resuscitate first ("resuscitate before you intubate") with blood products and push-dose or infusion vasopressors where appropriate (ATLS 11th ed and emergency airway texts). Avoid or minimize propofol and midazolam. Not verified this session; no graded trauma-society statement was located.
- **Succinylcholine contraindications relevant to trauma** [secondary: sibling SCI notes]:
  - OpenAnesthesia: hyperkalemia risk after burns, SCI and stroke peaks at 7-10 days, with "minimal risk within the first two days". Acetylcholine receptor upregulation lasts up to 2 years.
  - [recalled] Martyn JA, Richtsfeld M. Anesthesiology 2006;104(1):158-169. Commonly cited safe window is <24-72 h after burn, crush or denervation injury. Additional contraindications: known hyperkalemia, prolonged immobilization, rhabdomyolysis or crush injury, personal or family history of malignant hyperthermia, and neuromuscular disease.
  - Rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg is the alternative; sugammadex 16 mg/kg is available for immediate reversal. [recalled] — see spinal_cord_injury.md section 9
- **Post-intubation analgosedation** [recalled]: give analgesia first. Typical options are fentanyl 25-100 mcg/h (about 0.5-2 mcg/kg/h) and/or a ketamine infusion; add propofol (5-50 mcg/kg/min; hypotension) or dexmedetomidine for light sedation per PADIS 2018. Start promptly after rocuronium to avoid awareness under paralysis.
  - [verified: TQIP 2020 ICU section] Opioids are first-line for severe acute pain in the ICU; sedation is a "secondary target"; light-to-moderate sedation.
- **Etomidate dose in RSI trial** for chart: 0.2-0.3 mg/kg [verified]; ketamine 1-2 mg/kg [verified].

- For the handout: "Neither ketamine nor etomidate is clearly superior for mortality. Etomidate causes less peri-intubation hemodynamic collapse in critically ill (non-trauma) adults." Trauma patients were excluded from RSI, so applying it to hemorrhagic shock is extrapolation. The hemodynamic signal argues against assuming ketamine is the "safe" shock agent; dose reduction and pre-intubation resuscitation matter more than agent choice.
- EAST 2012's succinylcholine preference should be presented as historical. Current ED practice treats rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg as equivalent for first-pass success [recalled: Cochrane Tran 2015 found succinylcholine superior to rocuronium overall, but no statistical difference at rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg]. ## Q3. VTE prophylaxis in trauma: agents, dosing, anti-Xa, timing by injury type, IVC filters, PREVENT CLOT

### Takeaway
Current US practice rests on two consensus documents, neither GRADE-graded: the WTA 2020 algorithm (full text verified) and the AAST/ACS-COT 2022 inpatient protocol (Yorkgitis; abstract only verified). Both favor enoxaparin over UFH, start within 24 h for most patients and within 72 h for nearly all, and include TBI patients once CT is stable and solid-organ nonoperative patients. WTA calls 40 mg BID the "standard for most trauma patients". It keeps 30 mg BID for age >65, weight <50 kg, CrCl 30-60, and TBI or spine injury, with anti-Xa titration (peak 0.2-0.4 IU/mL). Continue doses through most surgeries; do not hold reflexively. PREVENT CLOT (NEJM 2023, n=12,211 fracture patients) showed aspirin 81 mg BID noninferior to enoxaparin 30 mg BID for 90-day death (0.78% vs 0.73%), with more DVT (2.51% vs 1.71%) and equal PE (1.49%). Prophylactic IVC filters are not recommended (Ho 2019 RCT: no benefit).

This is a strong pharmacist talking point: guideline-directed dosing was higher when a pharmacist was on the team (88.2% vs 69.0%).
- PREVENT CLOT supports aspirin 81 mg BID as a reasonable option for isolated operative extremity or pelvic fracture patients without other high-risk features. The higher DVT rate (mostly distal [recalled]) and the trial's young population (mean age 44.6) limit generalization to TBI, SCI, polytrauma or older patients. WTA 2020's "only if the patient declines injection" stance on aspirin predates the trial and is outdated for that subgroup.
- Prophylactic IVC filters: Ho 2019 and WTA 2020 align against them. EAST 2002's Level III support is superseded.
- For the 72-h window: nearly every source supports starting pharmacologic prophylaxis within 24-72 h in TBI (stable CT), SCI and solid organ NOM. Mechanical prophylaxis should start on admission.

## Q4. Stress ulcer prophylaxis in trauma (brief)

### Takeaway
The EAST 2008 SUP practice management guideline (Guillamondegui et al.) is the trauma-specific source. It could not be retrieved this session (the PubMed citation lookup mismatched, and it was likely published only on east.org). Its content below is recalled. The most relevant recent trial is REVISE (2024; ventilated ICU patients), also recalled.
